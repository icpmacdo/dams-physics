Dams in Section

A dam is a pile of soil or a block of concrete holding back water. Whether it stands or fails comes down to one idea you already know from the beach: sand is strong when its grains press together, and water pressure pushes them apart. This course builds that idea step by step, then uses it to cut real dams open. Every drawing is live, so press play, drag the sliders and try to break things. Seven short narrated films tell the same story. Each sits in one of the chapters it retells, and several range over other chapters as well; the captions say which.

Chapter 1 · Part I · Ground

The beach

The same sand, and only the water has changed. Why does it behave three ways?

This chapter adds a heap of sand at its angle of repose

1 figure · about 5 min to read

If you have played on a beach, you have felt sand behave in three different ways.

Dry sand pours. It runs through your fingers, and the steepest heap you can make is a low cone.

Damp sand stands. Pack it into a bucket, turn it out, and you get a castle with walls that go straight up.

Loose wet sand turns to soup. At the water's edge, where every gap between the grains is full of water, the sand feels firm while you stand still. Wiggle your feet and it goes soft, and you sink to the ankles.

It is the same sand every time. Only the water between the grains has changed. Now picture an earth dam: a heap of soil the size of a hill, with a lake pressing on one side and water soaking into it. Whether it behaves like the damp castle or like the soup at the waterline depends on what that water is doing.

Pick a state, then press Jiggle to shake the ground.

The slope a dry heap settles at (engineers call it the angle of repose) is 30–35° for most sand, steeper for angular grains than for rounded ones; the sand drawn here settles at 34°. Damp sand ignores that limit. Wet sand is subtler: under still water a heap stands about as steep as a dry one, where water drains out through its face it slumps to about 19°, and loose wet sand that gets shaken loses nearly all its strength. For a dam, that last one is the dangerous one.

To explain all of this, you need two facts about water first. Its pressure grows with depth (Chapter 2). And it moves through the ground, slowly or very slowly, depending on the ground (Chapter 3). Chapter 4 puts them together.

Chapter 2 · Part I · Ground

Depth, not size

Two dams hold water 50 m deep, one a long lake and one a narrow slot. Which has to be stronger?

This chapter adds water against the left face, with the pressure triangle

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 2.1, 3 min 11 s

Swim to the bottom of a deep pool and your ears start to hurt. The water above you has weight, and the deeper you go, the more of it there is pressing down. Water passes that press on in every direction, sideways too. So the water behind a dam pushes on the dam the same way it pushes on your eardrums.

Two dams each hold back water 50 m deep. One holds a lake 10 km long. The other holds a slot of water only 2 m wide, with solid rock behind it. Which dam has to be stronger?

Water pressure depends only on how far below the surface you are. Fifty metres down it is the same in a slot as in an ocean, so the two dams feel exactly the same push. The big lake is far heavier, but its weight rests on the lake bed. The size of a lake decides how much water escapes if the dam breaks. The depth decides the load on the dam.

Each metre of depth adds the weight of one more metre of water standing on every square metre. That is a tonne of water, which presses down with about 9.81 kilonewtons. A kilonewton (kN) is roughly the weight of 100 kg, and a pressure of one kN on every square metre is a kilopascal (kPa). So every metre of depth adds 9.81 kPa. Engineers write it like this:

p = ρ · g · h   ≈  9.81 kPa × depth in metresp pressure (kPa) · ρ density of water, 1,000 kg/m³ · g gravity, 9.81 m/s² · h depth below the water surface (m)

Ten metres of water adds about 98 kPa, roughly the pressure of the whole atmosphere above us. Divers count their depth in those 10 m steps.

Drag the sensor up and down. Then change the depth of the water and watch the total push on the dam.

The area of the triangle is ½ × (pressure at the bottom) × (depth). The depth appears twice, once in the pressure and once in the height of the wet face, so the push grows with the depth squared:

F = ½ · ρ · g · H²F push of the water on each metre length of dam (kN per m) · H depth of water at the dam (m)

A dam holding 100 m of water takes four times the push of one holding 50 m. That is why tall dams are so much thicker at the bottom than at the top. The concrete dam in Fig. 6.1 runs this same sum: its readouts show the pressure at the dam's upstream foot and the push per metre.

2.1 Water in the ground has pressure too

Dig a hole in the sand near the sea and it fills with water from below. The water in the hole settles at a level, and below that level every gap between the grains is full of water. That level is the water table.

The water in those gaps is connected from gap to gap, like the water in a soaked sponge, so while it is still it obeys the same rule as the water in a lake. A metre below the water table it is at about 10 kPa. Ten metres down it is at about 98 kPa, pushing outward on every grain around it. The pressure of the water in the gaps between grains is called pore pressure, and Chapter 4 turns on it.

Chapter 3 · Part I · Ground

Which way the water goes

The sea is lower than the beach. What pushes the water out of the sand, and why does it take hours?

This chapter adds the phreatic line through the heap and one standpipe (the straw)

2 figures · about 5 min to read

Water in the ground is rarely still. It creeps through the gaps between the grains, from where its head is high to where it is low. Head is the measure below, and it is not the same as pressure: still water at the bottom of a lake is at high pressure and doesn't move. You can watch it happen as the tide goes out: for hours afterwards, little streams trickle out of the sand at the bottom of the beach.

To tell which way the water will go, engineers use a simple measure. Imagine pushing a clear straw down into wet ground and watching how high the water climbs inside it. The level the water reaches, measured from some fixed height such as sea level, is the total head at the bottom of the straw. Water flows from high head to low head.

The level in the straw adds two things: how high the bottom of the straw is, and how far the water pressure there can push water up the straw. So groundwater can flow uphill, as long as it is heading toward lower head. That is how a spring bubbles up out of a hillside.

The straws are real. Engineers sink thin pipes into dams and the ground beneath them and measure the water level inside. They are called piezometers, and reading them, along with measuring the water that seeps out, is one of the main ways engineers know what the water inside a dam is doing.

3.1 Some ground is a sieve, some is a wall

How easily water passes depends on the size of the gaps. Gravel has gaps you can see. Clay is made of flat flakes hundreds to thousands of times smaller than a grain of sand, and the gaps between them are tiny and twisting. Engineers measure how easily water gets through as the permeability, written k and given in metres per second.

3.2 Darcy's law

In 1855 and 1856 Henry Darcy, the engineer who had built the water supply of Dijon in France some fifteen years before, ran water down through sand packed in a single upright column 2.5 m tall, and found a simple rule. Double the difference in head between the two ends and the flow doubles. Double the length of the sand and the flow halves. What matters is how steeply the head drops along the way: the head lost for every metre the water travels. That slope is called the hydraulic gradient, written i.

q = k · i · A    with  i = Δh / Lq flow (m³/s) · k permeability (m/s) · i gradient · Δh head difference (m) · L length of the path (m) · A area the water flows through (m²)

Below, his test is laid on its side: a pipe full of sand joins two tanks. Drag the water levels in the tanks, or use the sliders. The thin tubes along the pipe are piezometers. Watch how the water in them lines up in a straight slope from one tank to the other.

Now add the clay plug. The pipe is the same length and the tanks are at the same heights, but nearly all the head is lost across the short piece of clay. The tubes in the sand on either side read almost the same as the tank next to them. The same flow has to pass through every part of the pipe, and the tight clay needs a much steeper gradient to push it through, so the clay takes almost the whole drop.

This is how a dam with a clay core works. A core of clay, much narrower than the dam around it, takes almost the whole drop in head, from the reservoir level down to the downstream side. The sand and rock on either side carry almost none of it, so the water pressure in the downstream half of the dam stays low. Chapter 9 shows the zones that make this work.

A one-metre block of compacted clay has water pressing on one side, with the water level 1 m higher than on the other side. How long does a drop of water take to get through the metre?

About eleven years. Through gravel, the same push gets a drop across in about 30 seconds. The race below runs four materials side by side. Its clock has to speed up enormously before the clay moves at all.

That enormous range is what makes it possible to build a dam out of soil at all. A dam needs a part that water can barely get through, and parts that let water out quickly, side by side. Clay does the first job. Sand and gravel do the second.

Chapter 4 · Part I · Ground

Grip

If water doesn't grease the grains, what does it do to them?

This chapter adds small paired arrows under the slope: grains pressing (σ′)

2 figures · about 5 min to read

This chapter explains the three kinds of sand on the beach, and with them almost every way a dam can fail. The idea comes from Karl Terzaghi, the engineer who founded soil mechanics in the 1920s, and it starts with something simpler than soil.

4.1 Friction comes from pressing

Put a book on a plank and slowly tilt the plank. The book stays put until the slope gets steep, then it slides. Friction holds it, and friction comes from the book pressing on the plank. Press twice as hard and it takes twice the force to slide it. The friction on offer is always a fixed fraction of the press.

That fraction sets a steepest angle for each pair of surfaces. Tilt beyond it and the block slides, whatever it weighs. Engineers call it the friction angle, written φ. For sand as a whole it is about 34°, the same angle as the dry heap in Chapter 1, because the surface of a heap is a ramp of sand resting on sand. A single grain sliding on another grips far less. Most of sand's 34° comes from the grains interlocking: to move, they have to roll up and over one another.

A block rests on a ramp tilted to 25°, holding comfortably. A thin water cushion sits under its base. You pump up the water pressure in the cushion. The block's weight, shape and surface stay exactly the same. What happens?

The water carries part of the block's weight, so less weight is left pressing the block onto the ramp. Friction comes from that press, so it shrinks too, while gravity pulls the block down the slope as hard as ever. Try it below.

Engineers compare the two forces with one number, the factor of safety: friction available divided by friction needed. Above 1 the block holds. Below 1 it slides. Dams are designed to keep it well above 1, often about 1.5, to leave room for everything nobody can measure exactly.

Each kilopascal of pressure in the cushion cancels a kilopascal of the block's press on the ramp. At 25° the block slides once the water carries 31% of that press, just under a third. The block itself never changed.

4.2 Soil is thousands of little blocks

Now shrink the block down to a grain of sand. A handful of soil is a crowd of grains, each resting on its neighbours at a few points of contact. Every contact is a tiny block on a tiny ramp, held by friction, and that friction comes from how hard the grains press on each other. The strength of soil is the friction of all those contacts added up.

When the gaps are full of water, the water pushes on every grain from all sides, like the cushion under the block. It carries part of the load, and the grains press on each other less. Terzaghi saw that the strength of soil follows only the press left over between the grains. He called it the effective stress, and it is the press of everything above (the total stress) minus the pore pressure:

σ′ = σ − u    and    strength = σ′ · tan φσ total stress: the weight of everything above, per square metre (kPa) · u pore pressure (kPa) · σ′ effective stress: how hard the grains press on each other · tan φ the friction fraction from the ramp, about 0.67 for sand (φ = 34°)

The total stress at some depth is the weight of everything above that point: soil, water, and anything built on top. Damp sand weighs about 18 kN per cubic metre. Soaked, it weighs about 20, because water has filled the gaps. Raise the water table in the column below and watch the three lines.

Two things to notice. First, flooding the column from damp to soaked cuts the effective stress at 10 m from 180 to about 102 kPa. That is more than 40% of the soil's strength gone, though no grain has to move. Second, once the water rises above the ground, every extra metre adds the same 9.81 kPa to the total stress and to the pore pressure, so the effective stress stops changing. A lake sitting on top of soil doesn't press its grains together any harder.

That accounts for soaked sand. It also explains a surprise in Fig. 1.1: a heap under still water stands about as steep as a dry one. The water carries part of each grain's weight, so the grains press on each other less, but the same buoyancy lightens their pull down the slope by the same share, and the angle depends only on the ratio of the two. The heap slumps to about 19° only where water drains out through its face, because the flowing water drags the grains downhill as well (Chapter 3 measures that drag).

Chapter 5 · Part I · Ground

When the water can't get out

Why does firm wet sand go pale under one foot, while the sand at the water's edge swallows the other?

This chapter adds the soaked zone below the phreatic line shaded (water carrying load)

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 5.1, 9 min 5 s

5.1 Damp sand: water that pulls

The damp sandcastle needs one more piece. In damp sand there is only a little water, and it gathers in tiny rings where the grains touch (look back at the close-up in Fig. 1.1). The surface of each ring is curved and stretched, like the skin of a soap bubble, and it pulls the two grains toward each other. The water in those rings is at less than the pressure of the air around it, a negative pore pressure that engineers call suction.

Put a negative u into σ′ = σ − u and the effective stress goes up. The grains press together harder than their own weight could make them, so friction can hold them even on a vertical face. Suction in damp beach sand is only a few kPa. But near the top of a sandcastle the grains carry almost no weight, so those few kPa are most of what holds them in place.

Dry the sand and the rings evaporate, the suction goes, and the castle crumbles. Flood it and the rings merge into one body of water at ordinary positive pressure, the suction goes again, and the wall slumps.

5.2 Loose, dense, and the liquid beach

One beach state is left: the sand at the waterline that goes soft when you wiggle your feet. To understand it, you need to watch the grains themselves move.

When grains are pushed so they slide past one another, how tightly they are packed decides what happens to the water in the gaps. Densely packed grains have to ride up over each other to move, so the heap swells and draws water in. Loosely packed grains drop into the gaps below them, so the heap shrinks and squeezes its water.

Whether that matters depends on time. Push slowly and the water has time to flow in or out; engineers call that drained. Push quickly, or in a soil like silt that water crosses slowly, and the water is trapped. That is undrained, and then the pore pressure changes instead.

In the sandbox below, every grain is simulated. Pack the grains loose or dense, add water, then shake them or shear them (push the top sideways so the grains slide past each other), and watch the water pressure and the force between grains.

A house stands on loose sand that is full of water. An earthquake shakes the ground for a few seconds, too fast for the water to drain away. What happens to the house?

It sinks and tilts. Shaking makes loose grains try to settle into the gaps between them. The water in those gaps can’t escape in time, so its pressure rises until it carries the weight the grains were carrying. With nothing pressing the grains together, friction between them vanishes and the sand flows like a heavy liquid. That is liquefaction. Try it in the sandbox below with the third experiment, then try the same shaking on dense sand.

In loose, saturated sand, a shake makes the grains try to settle into the gaps below them. The water can't get out of the way in time, so for a moment it carries the weight instead of the grains. The grains still touch, but they hardly press on each other. The effective stress falls toward zero, and the friction goes with it. The sand flows like a heavy liquid. That is liquefaction, and it is what your wiggling feet do to the sand at the waterline.

Dense sand does the opposite. Forcing its grains to slide makes it swell, and the water can't flow in fast enough to fill the growing gaps, so the pore pressure drops and the sand gets stronger the harder you push.

This is why mine waste worries engineers so much. The ground-up rock a mine pumps out as slurry, called tailings, often settles loose and stays saturated for decades. Chapter 8 shows what happens to a dam built on top of it.

5.3 Where this comes back

Soil is strong because its grains press on each other, and water pressure in the gaps pushes them apart. You will meet that pair again in every chapter that follows.

Chapter 6 · Part II · Two kinds of dam

Two dams, two jobs

What changes when the thing behind the dam can lose its own strength?

This chapter adds freeboard mark and a spillway notch at the crest

1 figure · about 5 min to read

A hydroelectric dam holds back water, and the water is the product. Its height above the turbines is stored energy, released on demand. A tailings dam holds back mine waste, called tailings: rock ground finer than flour, pumped out of the mill as a slurry with the water used to process it. Nobody wants it back. The job is to keep it in place, forever.

Both dams face the physics of Chapters 2 and 3. Water pressure grows by about 9.8 kPa for every metre of depth (p = ρgh), which comes to nearly 1,000 kPa, or 1 MPa, at the foot of a 100 m reservoir. And water seeps through anything that isn't perfectly tight.

6.1 What differs is almost everything else

6.2 Why tailings dams fail differently

Water has no strength to lose. A deposit of loose, saturated tailings has some, and can lose nearly all of it at once: it is the liquid beach from Chapter 5 at the scale of a mine. Sheared, the loose grains try to pack tighter, the trapped water takes over their load, and the deposit flows like a heavy fluid. That is liquefaction. An earthquake can set it off. So can something much quieter, with no shaking at all (static liquefaction): a rising water table, slow creep, or a small movement of the slope.

  • 1985Stava, Italy. Two upstream-raised fluorite tailings dams collapsed one into the other; 268 people killed.
  • 1994Merriespruit, South Africa. An upstream-raised gold tailings dam overtopped after heavy rain and breached; the tailings flowed into a town, a flow one later study attributes to static liquefaction. 17 killed.
  • 2014Mount Polley, British Columbia, Canada. A tailings dam raised by the modified centreline method breached in the night, releasing about 25 million m³ of tailings and water. It was not a liquefaction of the tailings. Its cause is the subject of Chapter 13. No deaths.
  • 2015Fundão, Mariana, Brazil. An upstream-raised dam liquefied; 19 killed. Tailings travelled 663 km down the Gualaxo do Norte, Carmo and Doce rivers to the Atlantic.
  • 2019Brumadinho, Brazil. Dam I, upstream-raised, failed by static liquefaction in seconds; 270 killed. The disaster led to the GISTM and to Brazil banning upstream dams.

A block on a cushion of water

A concrete dam weighs more than twice the push of the water on it. Why isn't that the end of the story?

This chapter adds a small concrete block at the left with uplift arrows under it

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 7.1, 5 min 13 s

7.1 Seepage under a concrete dam: uplift

A concrete dam is the block on the water cushion from Fig. 4.1. Water seeping under its base pushes up on it, and that uplift cancels part of the dam's weight. The weight, through friction on the rock, is what stops the dam sliding. The usual remedy is a row of drain holes bored into the rock from a tunnel inside the dam (the gallery), just downstream of the grout curtain, to let the water out. A common design assumption, used by the US Bureau of Reclamation among others, is that working drains cut the pressure at the drain line to the tailwater (the water level downstream) plus one-third of the difference between reservoir and tailwater.

Chapter 8 · Part II · Two kinds of dam

Never finished

Why would a country write the word “upstream” into its laws?

This chapter adds a raise stepped onto the crest

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 8.1, 9 min 6 s

A tailings dam is never finished while the mine runs. The mill makes tailings every day, so the dam has to grow with them. It begins as a starter dam of soil dug from pits nearby (engineers call this borrow), and the tailings are piped out over its crest. When the deposit nears the top, the dam is raised. Each raise can step toward the pond, away from it, or straight up, and that direction names the method:

  • Upstream: the crest steps toward the pond. Each raise is a small dyke built on the tailings beach.
  • Downstream: the crest steps away from the pond. Each raise is built over the previous downstream slope, on compacted fill and natural ground.
  • Centreline: the crest rises vertically. Each raise straddles the beach and the downstream slope.

Upstream

For

  • Least fill by far: each raise is a small dyke. Cheapest and fastest.
  • Can be built from the tailings themselves.

Against

  • Each raise rests on loose, saturated tailings that can liquefy, statically or in an earthquake.
  • Needs a long dry beach and a pond kept far from the crest to hold the phreatic surface down; raising must be slow.
  • Drains can't be extended upward; can't store much water.
  • New upstream dams have been barred in Chile since 1970, at first only where people live within reach downstream and under its 2007 rules everywhere, and banned outright in Brazil (since 2019).

Downstream

For

  • Every raise is founded on compacted fill or natural ground.
  • A core and drains can be extended with each raise, like a water dam.
  • Best earthquake performance; can store water.

Against

  • Most fill: both faces move out with every raise, so the section grows fastest (9,792 m³ per metre here, against 6,144 for centreline).
  • The toe marches outward, so land downstream has to be reserved from the start.

Centreline

For

  • A middle path on fill volume and footprint.
  • The crest stays put, so tailings pipelines and access roads don't move.
  • A vertical chimney drain can be raised with every lift.

Against

  • The upstream edge of each raise sits on the beach, so the beach next to the crest must be sandy, drained and compacted.

Coquitlam Dam · 1913, backed up by a new dam in 2007

A water dam built like a tailings dam

Coquitlam Dam was built by hydraulic fill: soil washed into place with water, which leaves it loose and saturated, much like an upstream tailings raise. Modern investigations found a loose zone that could liquefy in a large earthquake. Rather than repair the old fill, BC Hydro built a new compacted earth-core rockfill dam against its downstream toe, founded mostly on dense glacial soil. The new dam was finished in 2007, and BC Hydro declared the whole seismic upgrade complete in 2008. A dam built to hold water can carry the same risk as a tailings dam: what decides it is whether the fill is loose and wet or dense and drained.

Sources: BC Hydro and ASCE (2008) on the Coquitlam Dam seismic upgrade.

Every zone has one job

Why build a dam out of six different soils?

This chapter adds a core and a filter inside the heap

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 9.1, 6 min 51 s

A large embankment dam is rarely made of one material. It is zoned: built from several soils and rocks, each placed where it does one job. A zone holds water back, or lets water out without letting soil out, or adds weight. The clay core is the clay plug from Chapter 3, taking almost the whole drop in head. Filters and drains let the water that gets through escape, so the pressure downstream of the core stays low. That leaves the downstream shell fairly dry, its grains pressing hard on each other, which is where its grip comes from (Chapter 4). The upstream shell sits under the reservoir and stays soaked, but it is free-draining, so its water pressure is only what the reservoir puts there. Read any section from the middle outward and you'll find the same order: tight in the centre, coarser and more free-draining toward the faces.

Chapter 10 · Part III · Inside the embankment

Where the water goes

If every dam leaks, what makes a leak safe?

This chapter adds two flow lines and three equipotentials

2 figures · about 5 min to read

Every dam leaks. The design questions are where the water goes, how much pressure it carries, and whether it takes soil with it. You met the rule in Chapter 3: water flows from high head to low, as fast as the soil lets it. That's Darcy's law:

q = k · i · A    with  i = Δh / Lq flow rate · k permeability (hydraulic conductivity, m/s) · i hydraulic gradient: head lost per metre travelled · A area

From gravel to compacted clay, k falls by a factor of ten million: gravel around 10⁻² m/s, clean sand 10⁻⁴, silt 10⁻⁷, compacted clay 10⁻⁹. Across all natural soils the range is wider still, more than ten powers of ten. Zoning depends on that spread. A clay core much narrower than the dam can carry almost the entire head, while the free-draining shells beside it carry almost none.

Water has five main ways past a dam, numbered in the drawing below.

10.1 Seepage lab

Everything in the next drawing is calculated. The page divides a 24 m embankment and the ground beneath it into 1 m cells, applies Darcy's law between every pair of neighbours, and solves for the head in all of them at once (the steady seepage equation, ∇·(k ∇h) = 0). Along the way it finds the phreatic surface, the water table inside the dam, using the same saturated–unsaturated approach as commercial seepage software. Lines of equal head are drawn every 10% of the reservoir head. Flow lines are drawn in two sets: four start on the dam's upstream face and five on the reservoir floor. Within each set, each neighbouring pair carries the same flow, as in a hand-drawn flow net. Between the sets it differs: on the starting settings a gap between two foundation lines carries about eight times the flow of a gap between two dam lines, so compare line spacing only within a set.

10.2 Five ways to manage seepage, and one to watch it

Cut it offCore, cutoff trench, slurry wall, grout curtain, face slab, geomembrane.
Lengthen the pathAn upstream impervious blanket makes the water travel further, lowering the gradient.
Drain itChimney, blanket and toe drains, relief wells, gallery drains: control where the phreatic surface sits and bleed off pressure.

When water carries soil

Bennett and Teton both leaked through their cores. Why does one of them still stand?

This chapter adds a filter and drain at the toe

2 figures · about 5 min to read · Film 11.1, 9 min

11.1 When seepage carries soil: piping

At the toe, the foot of the downstream slope, water seeping under a dam comes up out of the ground and pushes on the sand grains it passes. When the upward gradient reaches the critical gradient, the water carries the full weight of the soil, the effective stress drops to zero, and the sand boils. It's the block from Fig. 4.1 with so much water pressure under it that it floats:

ic = (Gs − 1) / (1 + e) ≈ (2.65 − 1) / (1 + 0.65) ≈ 1.0Gs specific gravity of the grains · e void ratio · safety factor against heave = i_c / i_exit; designers want at least 3 at an unfiltered exit of an existing dam, and 4 for a new dam or a repair, or about 2 to 2.5 where the soil and the water pressures are well measured (US Bureau of Reclamation). It flags sand boils, not every kind of internal erosion

A boil at the toe is where backward erosion piping begins. Grains wash out, a small channel forms, and the channel eats its way upstream under the dam. Erosion inside a dam, of this kind and others (engineers group them as internal erosion), is one of the two big killers of embankment dams. Foster, Fell and Spannagle's 2000 study of 124 embankment-dam failures up to 1986 counted every kind as "piping": through the embankment, through the foundation, or from one into the other. It caused about as many failures as overtopping: 47% against 48% once spillway and gate failures are counted with overtopping. That is more than overtopping alone (34%). Some failures had more than one cause, so the shares add up to a little over 100%. Teton Dam in Idaho failed by a different kind of internal erosion on its first filling in 1976: water carried soil from its core into open joints in the rock, deep in a trench cut for the core.

11.2 Why a filter works

In most embankments the most important zone is a few metres of carefully graded sand. Water leaving the core pushes on every grain it passes. If the next material has big gaps, as rockfill does, core grains are carried into them, and that is the start of internal erosion. A filter's gaps are smaller than the larger core grains, so those grains lodge at the boundary and form a stable layer that holds back the finer grains behind them. The water still gets through.

Retention:    D15(filter) ≤ 4–5 × D85(soil)
Permeability: D15(filter) ≥ 4–5 × D15(soil)Terzaghi's criteria. Dx = the grain size that x% of the material (by weight) is finer than. A filter's pore throats are roughly a fifth of its D15. Sherard & Dunnigan (1989) refined the rules for clay cores.

W.A.C. Bennett Dam, Peace River · 1996

A sinkhole on the crest

In June 1996, crews checking a small pothole on the crest of BC Hydro's 183 m earthfill dam found a much larger hole beneath it. A second sinkhole turned up during the drilling that followed. Both were in the core, each around a survey casing installed during construction. BC Hydro's 2012 expert panel found that loose core soil around the first casing had settled when it got wet, and that seepage had also washed some of the core's fine grains away (internal erosion); the second may have been the settling alone. Engineers lowered the reservoir and filled the voids by compaction grouting, carefully, so the injected grout wouldn't crack the core. R.A. Stewart's 1998 account of the crisis ranks it as the gravest dam safety concern in BC Hydro's history.

Sources: R.A. Stewart (1998) and BC Hydro's expert engineering panel (2012) on the Bennett Dam sinkholes.

Will the slope stand?

This dam is standing, with water breaking out of its face. How close is it to sliding?

This chapter adds a slip circle through the downstream slope

about 5 min to read · Film 12.1, 6 min 4 s

A dam made all of compacted fill has a factor of safety of 1.12, with water breaking out 8 m up its downstream slope. Which change helps it most?

The drain does the most: the factor of safety goes from 1.12 to about 1.6. The flatter slope reaches only about 1.27, and the cutoff about 1.14. The fill is strong enough when it is dry. Its trouble is the water in it: on the critical slip surface, water pressure carries about a third of the soil's weight, so the grains press together with only two-thirds of it. A drain lets that water out before it reaches the downstream slope. You'll paint that drain yourself in Chapter 13.

12.1 What the check does, and what it leaves out

For circular slip surfaces the page uses Bishop's simplified method, the standard hand-and-computer method since 1955. It cuts the sliding mass into 40 vertical slices, works out how hard each slice presses on its base after taking away the water pressure there, and balances the turning effect of the whole mass about the circle's centre. On the weak-layer foundation it also tries sliding blocks with a flat base along the layer, using Spencer's method, which balances forces and turning effects together.

Water pressure on each slice comes straight from the seepage solution. Suction above the water level is ignored, as is usual practice, because it can vanish in a wet spell. For the upstream check the reservoir is emptied quickly: the free-draining materials (anything that passes water at 10⁻⁵ m/s or faster: rockfill, filter sand, tailings sand and the sand & gravel foundation) drain with it, while clay and fill keep their water pressure for a while (the classic rapid drawdown case). Designers accept a lower factor of safety for that short-lived condition, about 1.2. The workbench in Chapter 13 fixes the upstream slope at 2:1, which is steep for an earth dam; real ones are often flatter for exactly this reason.

Chapter 13 · Part IV · Will it stand?

Build your own dam

Can you design a dam that passes every check, and what can the checks not see?

This chapter adds the 1 m grid of the workbench over the section

1 figure · about 5 min to read · Film 13.1, 8 min 46 s

You now know enough to design an embankment yourself. Paint the inside of a 24 m dam with five materials, choose the ground it sits on, and fill the reservoir. Then the page tests your design with two calculations.

The first is the seepage solver from the seepage lab in Fig. 10.2. It finds where the water inside your dam settles, how much leaks through, and how hard it pushes on the soil where it comes out.

The second asks whether the downstream slope would slide. It tries more than two thousand curved surfaces through the slope (1,960 on a grid, then a few hundred more homing in on the weakest), each a possible slip surface. Along each one it weighs the grip the soil can offer (friction, plus a little stickiness called cohesion) against the pull of the soil's weight trying to slide it downhill. Friction depends on how hard the grains press together, so wherever the seepage solution puts water pressure on a surface, the grip there drops.

Grip divided by pull is the factor of safety. Below 1 the slope slides. Dam engineers usually want 1.5 or more with the reservoir full, to cover what they can't know about the soil.

Leaks

Seepage in litres per day for each metre of dam. Some is normal. A lot means lost water, or polluted water for a tailings dam.

Pipes

Where water leaves unprotected soil, it pushes on the grains. Rising out of level ground it lifts them; out of a slope it drags them along the face, and the same number is a guide to how hard. Safety against piping is the push that would float sand away divided by the push it gets. For water rising out of level ground the US Bureau of Reclamation wants at least 3 for an existing dam and 4 for a new one (about 2 to 2.5 where the soil is well measured); the missions here ask for 3 at every exit. The number warns of sand boils; it can't see every kind of internal erosion.

Slides

The lowest factor of safety of any slip surface at least 2 m deep. Shallower slips are surface damage that maintenance fixes.

Material strengths and permeabilities are typical values, not design values. The sandbox does not check earthquakes, the liquefaction of loose saturated sand (Chapter 5), or whether each zone is a proper filter for the one beside it. In a real dam, clay painted straight against rockfill would wash into it, which is why real cores have filters.

Mount Polley tailings dam · 2014

The layer nobody found

About 8–10 m below the original ground surface was a layer of clay, laid down in a glacial lake, that the site investigations had missed. The investigation panel found that the dam's weight went on slowly enough for the water in that clay to seep away, for the most part, as it was squeezed (drained loading). But the dam had pressed the clay harder than anything in its past, and clay in that state tries to pack tighter when it is sheared. Once it began to slide, its water couldn't get out of the way. The water pressure rose, and the clay's grip fell well below the slow, drained strength the design relied on (undrained failure). A steep rockfill slope on top, 1.3 m across for every 1 m up (1.3H:1V), supplied the push. The dam slid on the clay and released about 25 million m³ of tailings and water. The panel put it in an image of its own: building on the clay unknowingly, without recognizing that it could fail undrained, loaded the gun, and building a slope that steep on top of it pulled the trigger.

Sources: Mount Polley Independent Expert Engineering Investigation and Review Panel (2015).
