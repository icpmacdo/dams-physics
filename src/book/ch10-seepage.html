So far. built our dam, a 24 m embankment with slopes of 2:1 (two across for every one up, as in ), and in its zoned form a clay core between free-draining shells. Every zone went where it did on a guess about where the water would go. This chapter follows the water itself: where it gets in, which way it goes, and where it comes out.

After this chapter you can

  • trace the five ways water gets past a dam, and read a flow net to work out how much flows and how steeply the head falls where it leaves;
  • estimate a dam's leak by hand with Darcy's law, and check the estimate against the seepage lab's solution;
  • explain why a drain makes a dam leak more and yet makes it safer, and choose a defence from a picture of the seepage.

Walk along the downstream toe of an earth dam and sooner or later you come to a small concrete box set into the ground. Across one end stands a steel plate with a V cut into its top edge, and through the V slides a thin, clear sheet of water. It drops a few centimetres into a channel and runs off toward the river. On a regular round someone comes by, reads how deep the water stands behind the plate, and writes it down. That depth says how many litres a second are coming out.

The water in the box is the dam's leak, its seepage. It went into the ground on the floor of the reservoir, or soaked into the dam's upstream face, and crept through to this side. Nobody is alarmed by it. The US Army Corps of Engineers' manual on seepage states it as a fact before it says anything else: "All earth and rock-fill dams are subject to seepage through the embankment, foundation, and abutments.", para. 1-5, and again in para. 8-1; para. 13-3 describes the V-notch weirs. Every dam leaks. The useful question is what makes a leak safe, and here is a first test of your feel for leaks:

Our dam stands on 15 m of sand and gravel, with nothing under it to stop water passing beneath, and it is built of one kind of earth fill. Build it again with a narrow core of clay, a hundred times tighter than that fill, between shells of free-draining sand and gravel. The ground underneath stays as it was. What happens to the leak?

It rises, from 2,366 to 5,434 litres a day under each metre of dam, more than twice as much. The water was never going through the dam; almost all of it goes under. The free-draining upstream shell lets it reach the gravel close to the core, so it only has to get under the core's 21.4 m base instead of the whole 102 m. works it through.

To decide, follow the water: where it enters, which way it goes and how far, and where it comes out. That takes a map of the ways past a dam, a way of drawing the water's paths, and in the end a solver.

Five ways past a dam

You met the rule in : water flows from high head to low, as fast as the soil lets it, and Darcy's law, , says how fast. The race in showed how wide the range of permeability is: gravel, at 10⁻² m/s, passes water ten million times as easily as compacted clay, at 10⁻⁹ m/s. Zoning depends on that spread. A clay core much narrower than the dam can take almost the whole drop in head while the free-draining shells beside it take almost none.

Paths 2, 3 and 5 run through ground that was there before the dam. Its designers chose neither its permeability nor its layering; they could only find them out, and the water uses whatever they missed.

Drawing the water: the flow net

To say how much water takes path 2, and how hard it pushes where it comes out, you need the head everywhere in the ground. found it for a pipe, where the water has one way to go and the head falls along a straight line. Under a dam the water spreads out and bends. The rule in every small piece of ground is still the same, though: in steady flow nothing piles up, so each piece passes on exactly the water it receives, and it passes it toward lower head by Darcy's law.In symbols the two facts are one equation, ∇·(k∇h) = 0. Read it from the inside out: ∇h is the slope of the head, k∇h the flow it drives by Darcy's law, and ∇· (the divergence) asks how much more flows out of a tiny box than flows in. Zero means nothing piles up and nothing runs dry. In uniform soil k comes outside and it becomes Laplace's equation, the same one that governs the steady flow of heat through a metal plate. Those two facts fix the head everywhere once you know it along the edges: the reservoir floor, the ground beyond the toe, the underside of the structure.

For most of the twentieth century engineers drew the answer by hand, as a flow net: two families of lines. Flow lines are the paths the water follows. Equipotentials join points of equal total head: push one of the straws of into the ground anywhere along one, and the water in it rises to the same level. In soil that passes water equally well in every direction, the water crosses every equipotential at right angles, because straight across them is the way the head falls fastest.Philipp Forchheimer, an Austrian engineer, recognized that steady groundwater flow obeys Laplace's equation and drew some of the first flow nets (). Arthur Casagrande, at Harvard, made the net a working tool for dam designers in a paper of 1937, with rules for beginners that still hold: start with four or five flow channels, and adjust the whole net before any of its details ().

What makes a net useful is a choice of spacing. Space the flow lines so that each neighbouring pair carries the same share of the flow; the strip between them is a flow channel. Space the equipotentials so that the head falls by the same amount, Δh, between each pair; that is a head drop. Now look at one cell of the net, b wide across the flow and l long along it. By Darcy's law the flow through it, per metre of dam, is k × (Δh/l) × b. Draw the net so that every cell is a square, b = l, and the flow through each cell is k·Δh, whatever its size. With Nd drops sharing the total head H, Δh = H/Nd, and Nf channels each carrying k·Δh give the whole flow:

q = k · H · Nf / Nd

q flow per metre length of dam (m³/s per m) · k permeability (m/s) · H head lost between where the water enters and where it leaves (m) · Nf number of flow channels · Nd number of head drops

The ratio Nf/Nd depends only on the shape of the ground and the structure. The rules for sketching a net are short: lines cross at right angles, every cell looks square, an edge that passes no water (a concrete base, a rock surface) is itself a flow line, and ground under still water is an equipotential. Getting every cell square at once takes practice and an eraser. Here is a finished net under a small concrete weir (a low dam across a river, not the measuring weir of the toe), an illustrative example sunk into a bed of sand:

Notice that the squares are big where the water has room, out under the reservoir and deep under the weir, and small where it is squeezed, around the weir's buried corners. A small square means the same drop in head over a short distance: a steep gradient, and fast water. Crowding in a flow net is a picture of the gradient. Notice too where the smallest square is: at the exit, where the water turns upward beside the weir and leaves the sand. That is where the water pushes hardest on the grains it is leaving.

Reading the weir's net

Given
The net of (illustrative): Nf = 4 channels, Nd = 12 drops, head H = 10 m, sand with k = 10⁻⁴ m/s. The smallest square, at the exit, is 1.4 m deep along the weir's downstream face.
Find
The flow under each metre of weir, and the gradient where the water leaves.
The idea
Every square passes k·Δh, so the flow is a count (). In the last square the water loses one drop of head over the square's length.
  1. Δh = 10 ÷ 12 = 0.833 mthe head lost in each drop
  2. Each channel: 10⁻⁴ × 0.833 = 8.3 × 10⁻⁵ m³/sa square passes permeability times its drop in head
  3. q = 4 × 8.33 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.3 × 10⁻⁴ m³/sfour channels, each carrying the same
  4. In a day: 0.000333 × 86,400 = 28.8 m³86,400 seconds in a day
  5. i at the exit = 0.833 ÷ 1.4 = 0.595the last drop, spent over the depth of the last square

About 29 m³ of water a day passes under each metre of the weir, and it leaves the sand beside the weir at a gradient of 0.60.

Check. Make the sand ten times as permeable. The squares are the same squares, because nothing in the drawing knows about k, so the flow rises tenfold, to 3.3 × 10⁻³ m³/s, and the exit gradient stays 0.60. A gradient is a drop in head over a distance, and both come from the drawing alone.

What it means. The exit gradient of 0.60 is below the 1 at which upward-flowing water floats sand (), but not by much, and it sits exactly where the net is most crowded. The weir's weak spot is a square metre or two of sand beside its downstream face.

The Check holds a lesson for every seepage calculation. The permeability of a natural sand is rarely known to better than a factor of a few, so the flow from a net is only as good as that guess; the pattern of heads, and with it every gradient and every water pressure, comes from the geometry, which the engineer can draw. That is why engineers sketch nets carefully where they want pressures and roughly where they want quantities."For calculation of seepage quantity only a crude flow net is required. Accurate flow nets are required to determine pressure distribution": , para. 4-6. Real ground adds a complication the drawing leaves out: soil laid down in layers passes water more easily along its layers than across them, a property called anisotropy.Casagrande warned that where layering is visible the permeability along the layers "may easily be ten times greater" than across them, and much more in distinctly layered soils; even a rolled earth dam, compacted layer by layer, keeps some of it (, pp. 151–152). Engineers draw the net on a section stretched by the square root of the ratio, then squash it back (, para. 4-7). A net drawn for uniform soil is the start of the analysis, not the end.

Check yourself The water upstream of the weir rises from 10 m to 15 m above the sand. Which lines of the net move?

None of them. The net is fixed by the shape of the weir and the sand. Every drop in head, the flow and the exit gradient grow by half again, in proportion to H.

Seepage lab

A net through a whole earth dam is harder to sketch than one under a weir, because the top of the wet part of the dam is unknown until the net is finished. So the page computes it instead. Everything in the next drawing is calculated. The page divides our 24 m embankment and the 15 m of ground beneath it into 1 m cells, applies Darcy's law between every pair of neighbours, and solves for the head in all of them at once (the steady seepage equation, ∇·(k ∇h) = 0). Along the way it finds the phreatic surface, the water table inside the dam, using the same saturated–unsaturated approach as commercial seepage software. Lines of equal head are drawn every 10% of the reservoir head. Flow lines are drawn in two sets: four start on the dam's upstream face and five on the reservoir floor. Within each set, each neighbouring pair carries the same flow, as in a hand-drawn flow net. Between the sets it differs: on the starting settings a gap between two foundation lines carries about 8 times the flow of a gap between two dam lines, so compare line spacing only within a set. The lab's lines are therefore no square net, and can't count them: read the heads from its equipotentials and the flow from its readout.

Notice that the equipotentials under the dam stand almost upright and nearly evenly spaced, like the head along the pipe of . The gravel passes water 100 times as easily as the fill, so to the water the dam is nearly a lid and the gravel a pipe 15 m deep and as long as the dam's base. Notice too how little goes in through the dam's own face: 221 of the 2,366 litres a day. That picture can be checked by hand.

Our dam's leak, by hand

Given
The lab on its starting settings: reservoir 20.4 m deep, tailwater at the ground; sand and gravel 15 m deep with k = 10⁻⁵ m/s; the dam's base, toe to toe, 102 m.
Find
The leak under each metre of dam, in litres a day, and how it compares with the lab's.
The idea
Treat the gravel as the pipe of : the whole head lost along the base, through an area 15 m deep for each metre of dam ().
  1. i = 20.4 ÷ 102 = 0.200head lost per metre of path
  2. q = 0.00001 × 0.200 × 15 = 0.00003 m³/sDarcy's law through 15 m² for each metre of dam
  3. 0.00003 × 86,400 × 1,000 = 2,592 L/dayseconds in a day, litres in a cubic metre
  4. 2,592 ÷ 2,366 = 1.096the hand number against the lab's
  5. A kilometre of dam: 2,366 × 1,000 ÷ 86,400 = 27.4 L/sa thousand metres of dam, litres a day turned into litres a second

About 2,600 litres a day under each metre of dam by hand, 10% more than the lab's 2,366. A kilometre of this dam would leak about 27 litres a second.

Check. Should the hand number be high or low? Darcy's pipe sends the water straight along 102 m. The real water has to dive into the gravel at the upstream toe and climb out again beyond the downstream one, and the lab's flow lines bow downward to do it. A longer path passes less, so the straight pipe should be a little high, and it is. The pipe also ignores the water that goes through the fill, but that is small: 99% of the lab's leak crosses the dam's centre line under the dam.

What it means. Ten per cent is better than the permeability deserves. A natural gravel's k is rarely known within a factor of a few, and the Bureau of Reclamation, which teaches this very calculation, says its result should be read only as the order of magnitude of the seepage (, sec. 6.9(b)).

What the lab knows that intuition doesn't

A hand estimate is only as good as the picture of the flow behind it, and the lab earns its place where that picture is wrong. The first case is the question this chapter opened with. Give our dam a clay core a hundred times tighter than its fill, 10⁻⁹ m/s, between shells of sand and gravel, 10⁻⁴ m/s, ten times more permeable than the gravel beneath them:

The core that leaks more

Given
Both dams on the sand and gravel, no drain, no cutoff, reservoir 20.4 m. The dam of one fill leaks 2,366 L/day per metre; the cored dam, 5,434. The core is 21.4 m wide at its base.
Find
Why the cored dam leaks more, and what a full cutoff under its core does.
The idea
Almost all the water goes under both dams. What changes is how much of the base the water must get under, which the head the solver finds at the top of the gravel shows.
  1. 5,434 ÷ 2,366 = 2.30the core more than doubles the leak
  2. In through the upstream shell: 5,343; through the reservoir floor: 91 L/daythe shell is the easy way in, so the water reaches the gravel beside the core
  3. Head at the top of the gravel, 12 m upstream of the centre line: 19.1 m (94%); with no core, 12.6 m (62%)the shell spends almost none of the head, so the water arrives at the core with nearly all of it
  4. Length to get under: 102 ÷ 21.4 = 4.77the whole base under a dam of fill, which is a lid to the gravel; only the core's base under the cored dam
  5. Full cutoff under the core: 5,434 ÷ 2.177 = 2,496a wall from the core's base to the rock: the leak falls to 2.2 L/day

The core more than doubles the leak, because the shells deliver the water to the gravel right beside it and the water only has to pass under the core's 21.4 m base. Taken down to rock with a cutoff, the same core cuts the leak about 2,500 times.

Check. If the length under the dam were the whole story, a length 4.8 times shorter would pass 4.8 times the water; the lab gives 2.3. The rest of the trip does not shrink with the core: the water still has to climb out of the gravel and cross some 40 m of it to the toe, and 6.0 m of head is still left at the top of the gravel a metre downstream of the core. So the factor should fall short of the ratio of lengths, and it does.

What it means. A barrier works only if the water can't go round it. A core that stops on top of a pervious foundation is a wall with a gap under it, and its shells hurry the water to the gap. The same core joined to rock by a cutoff makes the dam almost dry.

Check yourself A full cutoff cuts the cored dam's leak about 2,500 times. Would it do as much for the dam of one fill?

No: 2,366 falls only to 166, about 14 times less. With the gravel cut off, the water goes through the fill instead, and the fill passes water a hundred times more easily than the core.

The second surprise runs the other way. A drain inside a dam is a strip of clean sand and gravel, graded so that the soil around it cannot follow the water in ( explains how), and open to the air at its outlet, so the water in it is at zero pressure. Every bit of water in the dam or the ground that can reach it heads for it. The Corps of Engineers' manual notes the consequence for a horizontal drain under the downstream half of a dam: it lowers the pressure under the dam and increases the quantity of seepage., para. 9-3 and its Figure 9-1. The lab shows by how much:

A drain that leaks more and is safer

Given
The dam of one fill on the sand and gravel, reservoir 20.4 m: 2,366 L/day per metre and a safety against piping of 1.27. The chimney drain stands 4 m downstream of the centre line, and its blanket runs from there along the base to the toe. The toe drain fills the dam's toe.
Find
The leak and the safety against piping with each drain, and why the chimney raises the leak.
The idea
A drain holds everything it touches at zero pressure. It ends the water's trip early, and it gives the water a way out that holds the soil back.
  1. Chimney and blanket: 4,052 ÷ 2,366 = 1.7171% more water
  2. Path under the dam: 55 m instead of 102; 102 ÷ 55 = 1.85the gravel's water now leaves through the blanket, which starts 4 m past the centre line, 55 m from the upstream toe
  3. Steepest exit not covered by the drain: i = 0.0029; safety 343the water leaves through the drain, not through bare ground or the face
  4. Toe drain: 2,647 L/day, 12% more; safety 12.8a shorter cut at the end of the trip

The chimney drain makes our dam leak 71% more and raises its safety against piping from 1.27 to over 300. The toe drain costs 12% more water and raises the safety to 12.8.

Check. Darcy's law predicts a path 1.85 times shorter should pass 1.85 times the water; the lab gives 1.71, a little less, because the water still has to dive into the gravel and rise into the blanket. In the gravel immediately under the blanket's start the solver finds a head of 0.54 m: the drain has taken almost all of the 20.4 m by the time the water reaches it.

What it means. The extra water costs nothing but water. In return the drain takes the water pressure out of the downstream half of the dam, so the grains there press on one another with nearly their full weight and grip accordingly, which is what meant by drains putting strength back. And it gives the water an exit that holds the soil back.

Check yourself Where would you put a piezometer to show that the chimney drain works, and what should it read?

In the fill a few metres downstream of the chimney, near the base. With the drain working, the water in it should stand barely above its tip, because the fill there is drained to about zero pressure; a reading well above that means the drain is clogged or cut off.

A dam on clay

Now take the gravel away. Switch the foundation to Clay, 5 × 10⁻⁸ m/s, half as permeable as the fill. The leak collapses to 44.4 L/day per metre, and something else appears. On gravel the phreatic surface reached the downstream slope only 2 m up; on clay it meets the slope 8 m up, and 65% of the water leaves through the slope's face. With no easy way underneath, the water comes through the dam, and that needs a different hand estimate: one for water moving through the fill with a free surface on top.

The estimate engineers use is due to the French engineer Jules Dupuit, in 1863.. The entry correction: , p. 150. Pretend the water moves level. Cut the dam into upright slices. In a slice where the saturated fill is h deep, the water flows through h square metres for each metre of dam, and the gradient is the slope of the water table there, Δh over Δx. By Darcy's law q = k × (Δh/Δx) × h, and the same q passes every slice, so where the wet part is thin the water table must be steep. Rearranged, q · Δx = k · h · Δh. Add up the slices from the downstream end to the reservoir. On the left the Δx add up to the length L. On the right the products h · Δh, as h rises from nothing to H, add up to the area of a triangle with height H and base H, ½H², the same sum that gave the push of water in :

q = k · H² / (2L)

q flow through the dam per metre length (m³/s per m) · k permeability of the fill (m/s) · H depth of the reservoir (m) · L length the water travels, from where it enters to the downstream toe (m)

Where does the water enter? The upstream wedge of the dam stands in the reservoir and its water is at nearly the reservoir's head, so measuring from the upstream toe would make the trip too long. Arthur Casagrande showed in 1937 that a simple rule does well: start the trip upstream of where the water surface meets the face, by 0.3 times the horizontal run of the wetted face.

Five ways to manage seepage, and one to watch it

The chapter's question now has an answer. How much a dam leaks matters to whoever pays for the water, and to the neighbours of a tailings pond; on its own it seldom threatens the dam. A leak is safe when it leaves where the designer meant it to, through a drain and not out of a slope; when it leaves gently, at a gradient well below the one that floats grains, through a filter that holds them back; and when it leaves the downstream part of the dam at low water pressure, so that the grains there press on one another with their full weight. Engineers have six kinds of defence for it, drawn here at one scale on our dam:

Notice that only the first panel stops the water. The other five accept that it will arrive and manage the arrival. Lengthening the path is the oldest of them. Before flow nets were in everyday use, weirs on sandy riverbeds were sized by a rule of thumb: make the water's path along the underside of the structure long enough for the soil.The rule of thumb was W. G. Bligh's "line of creep": the head a structure could hold grew with the length of its contact with the soil. E. W. Lane, at the Bureau of Reclamation, proposed counting the level parts of that path at a third of the steep ones. A Bureau study of 1934, which checked the rules against pressures measured under real dams, recommended his weighting over Bligh's (), and Lane published it in 1935 (). The lab's half-depth cutoff is a lesson in that rule's limits: it adds 15 m to a 102 m path, and the leak barely moves.

Watching is the defence that says whether the others work. Piezometers are the straws of made permanent: a tube, or a pressure sensor, sealed at a point inside the dam or its foundation, reporting the head there. Read against the reservoir level for years, their trend shows whether the drains are clear and whether the pressure in the downstream slope is where the design put it. The leak itself is measured at weirs like the one this chapter began with.A 90° V-notch converts depth into flow. The Bureau of Reclamation's form of Cone's formula, Q = 2.49 h2.48 with Q in cubic feet a second and h in feet, becomes Q = 1.342 h2.48 in cubic metres a second and metres (, eq. 7-6). Because the flow grows faster than the depth's square, a weir reads small flows finely. Engineers judge the leak against the reservoir. A leak that rises and falls with the reservoir is the ground obeying Darcy's law. A leak that grows while the reservoir does not, or water that comes out cloudy, is a warning: turbidity means soil is moving, and even clear seepage is sampled, because it can carry fine soil the eye can't see., paras. 13-3 and 13-4.

The Prologue's dam had such a system: a pond below it caught what came through, and the first sign of trouble that night was the water in that pond rising. So it is fair to ask whether the leak brought it down, and the review panel asked. Piping and cracking, water opening a path through a dam and carrying soil out along it, is one of the commonest ways earth dams fail, and the panel found details that worried it. But apart from one brief spike in the flow, in April 2013, the records showed no abnormal seepage, and the panel found no evidence that piping or cracking caused the breach. Mount Polley's seepage was not what failed it., section 4.3.3, pp. 11–12; the pond that night, p. 10. What did fail it is the business of the last chapter.

What to remember

  • Every dam leaks. A leak is safe when the water leaves where the designer chose, gently and through a filter, and leaves the downstream slope at low water pressure, so its grains keep pressing on one another.
  • A flow net of squares turns Darcy's law into counting, q = k·H·Nf/Nd. Crowded squares mean a steep gradient; the smallest sit at exits and corners, where the water pushes hardest on the grains it leaves.
  • Head over path, times k, times depth, estimates the leak under our dam to within 10%, and Dupuit's slices estimate the leak through it. A hand estimate is an order-of-magnitude tool, because k is rarely known better than that.
  • A barrier works only if it reaches something tight. A core that stops on gravel can leak more than no core at all; joined to rock by a cutoff, it cuts the leak about 2,500 times.
  • A drain makes a dam leak more and stand safer: it takes the water pressure out of the downstream half, so the grains there grip with their full weight, and it lets the water out through a filter.

    Hint

    How much longer does the water's path get, if it goes down one side of the wall and up the other?

    The water goes under it. The cutoff reaches 7.5 m down, so the path grows from 102 m to about 102 + 2 × 7.5 = 117 m, and Darcy's law predicts about 13% less water; the lab gives 9%. The water still reaches the toe with most of its head, so the exit gradient there falls only a little. The Corps of Engineers' manual puts it bluntly: theory and model tests show a cutoff must reach at least 95% of the way through a uniform pervious foundation before seepage falls appreciably (, para. 9-4).

  4. In the lab, lower the reservoir to 60% and read the seepage, then raise it to 95%. Does the leak grow in proportion to the depth of water? What happens to the safety against piping?

    Hint

    Darcy's law makes the flow proportional to the head. Compare the two ratios: depth at 95% over depth at 60%, and leak over leak.

    Very nearly. The depth goes from 14.4 m to 22.8 m, a ratio of 1.58, and the leak from 1,652 to 2,657 L/day per metre, a ratio of 1.61: a little more, because a fuller reservoir also wets more of the fill. The safety against piping falls from 2.26 to 1.07, since the exit gradient grows with the head too. That is why engineers read piezometers and weirs against the reservoir level.

  5. Our dam of one fill stands on the clay foundation. A toe drain and a chimney drain both remove its seepage face. The toe drain leaks 49.9 L/day per metre with a safety against piping of 6.7; the chimney leaks 110, more than twice as much, with a safety against piping of 183. Which would you build, and why?

    Hint

    The lab's fill is the same in every direction. Is a real fill, compacted in layers, likely to be?

    The chimney, and the extra 60 litres a day per metre are the price. A fill compacted layer by layer passes water more easily along its layers than across them, and a loose or badly bonded layer can carry water over a toe drain and out of the downstream slope. A chimney cuts across every layer, so whatever comes through the upstream half is caught. The Bureau of Reclamation calls an inclined filter drain with a horizontal blanket almost standard for this reason, citing loose lifts, poor bond between lifts and inadvertently pervious layers (, sec. 6.3(c)). The lab's uniform fill cannot show this, which is a reason to read the lab as a guide and not a guarantee.
