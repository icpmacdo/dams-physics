Every number in this book has been a calculation: a pressure, a leak, a grip, a factor of safety, each worked out for a dam whose soil you chose. ended on a dam that passed its calculation and failed anyway. Real dams are also watched and governed by law, and some fail in ways no calculation in this book would catch.

On Friday 11 June 2010 a hiker on Mount Kobau, high above the Okanagan valley south of Oliver, British Columbia, found a small lake full to the brim and running over a dirt road, fast enough to cut a channel across it. He reported it at the tourist booth in Osoyoos. The message passed from the booth to the police, and from the police to the Ministry of Forests, where it ended as a voice mail about a washout on a minor road. Nobody heard it until it was too late., p. 10, the province's review by its Deputy Solicitor General.

The road ran along the crest of a dam. Testalinden Dam was a bank of silty sand and glacial till about 2.5 m high, finished in 1937 to store irrigation water, built with horses and a horse-drawn scraper to no engineer's design. That spring a deep snowpack was melting in the year's first hot spell. On the afternoon of Sunday 13 June, the water flowing over the bank cut through it. The lake ran down a steep, narrow gully toward the valley floor 1,500 m below and arrived as a mudflow at least ten times the volume of the water that started it. It destroyed or badly damaged five homes and buried 200 m of Highway 97. Nobody was hurt.The dam, the snowmelt and the mudflow: , quoting the field investigation in , which found no rain recorded in the three days before (the province's review, written a month after, spoke of heavy rain). The date, the year built, the toll: , pp. 7, 11 and 12.

None of it surprised the people whose job was to know. The province's files record water seeping near its outlet pipe as early as 1942. By 1960 a road had been built across it, and its spillway, the channel that lets a flood past (), had been filled with earth and a pipe laid through it. In 1977 an inspector wrote that "the dam is in deplorable condition and should, in my (the inspector) opinion, be breached or rebuilt." In 1988 another inspector added, "No attempt has been made to carry out our instructions." The review found warnings going back to the 1960s, and no sign that the repairs were made or that anyone held the owner to them., pp. 3 and 7–9; the quotations are from the inspections of 1977 and 1988.

Every fault on that list is in this book: water seeping along a pipe, a spillway too small for a flood, a freeboard of a foot or less. Nothing in the dam's calculations was wrong, because it never had any. What failed was the arrangement around it: inspections and letters, with nobody made to act on them. The other half

Testalinden failed in the commonest way an embankment dam fails: water came over the top. counted the record. Of the large embankment dams that failed in service up to 1986, about a third failed because water poured over the crest, and about half once the failed spillways and gates are counted in., its table 3, failures in operation: overtopping 34.2%, with spillway and gate failures 47.0%. This book has spent its chapters on the other half, and the title page gives it one sentence:

Soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and the water between them can take that press away.

It carried us from the beach to the workbench, but it does not reach Testalinden. Water running over a crest does not need to take the grains' press away: it drags them off the surface, and each one it takes uncovers the next. So the sentence needs an honest second half:

And water that pours over the top can wash the whole heap away, however hard its grains press.

Other omissions deserve a line each. Earthquakes do more than liquefy loose sand: shaking can crack a core and settle a crest. The flood a spillway must pass comes from rain and snowmelt records that a changing climate makes harder to trust; one lesson drawn from Testalinden is to recheck the spillways of dams built before those records existed. Watching the water you painted

For the half this book does cover, the calculation is where a dam's life starts. A real dam's fill is never quite what was sampled, the ground under it was seen only down a few boreholes, and drains silt up over the decades. So engineers build instruments into a dam, and each measures a quantity you have already computed. Here they are on our dam, the workbench's 24 m section on clay with the chimney and blanket drain that used to dry it; the water level inside it and the slip circle are the solver's:

Notice that each instrument reads an ingredient of a calculation. A piezometer is the straw of made permanent: it reads the water pressure u at one point, as the bars along the slip surface did in . The measuring weir reads the leak of . The inclinometer, a grooved tube down which a probe measures the tilt, shows where the ground is moving sideways, and at what depth. No instrument reads the factor of safety: it exists only in the calculation, and the readings are what engineers feed it.

So the question an engineer asks of a rising piezometer is how much safety it costs. For our dam, has supplied what is needed: on its critical circle the factor of safety is 1.116, the water carries 0.353 of the slices' weight (the ratio engineers call ru), and the same circle without water stands at 1.741. In 1960 Alan Bishop and Norbert Morgenstern showed that for a given slope the factor of safety falls very nearly in a straight line as ru grows:The Morgenstern of that paper () is Norbert Morgenstern, who fifty-five years later chaired the panel that investigated Mount Polley (, p. i).

FS ≈ FS0 − n · ru

FS0 the factor of safety with no water pressure · ru the share of the slices' weight the water carries, Σu·b ÷ ΣW · n the safety each unit of ru costs, set by the slope's shape and soilWhy nearly straight: in Bishop's sum the water enters only by taking u·b off each slice's weight before friction acts. If every slice lost the same share of its weight, the grip, and with it the factor of safety, would fall in a straight line as that share grew, except that the factor of safety also sits inside each slice's mα. That small feedback bends the line slightly.

What a rising piezometer costs (illustrative)

Given
Our dam on clay, as left it: FS = 1.116 with ru = 0.353, and FS0 = 1.741 on the same circle with no water. The slices on that circle weigh, on average, 154.1 kN for each square metre of their bases. A metre of water in a piezometer is 9.81 kPa.
Find
The safety lost for each 0.1 added to ru, and the rise in the piezometers along the slip that adds it.
The idea
Draw the line of through the two points. Raising the water pressure on every slice by the same amount adds that amount, divided by the slices' weight per square metre, to ru.

Check. Do it without the straight line. Rerun Bishop's sum on the same circle with every slice's water pressure 15.41 kPa higher: the solver's slices give 0.943, against 1.116 − 0.177 = 0.939 from the line. The two agree to the second decimal.

What it means. A dam like ours designed to 1.5 holds 0.5 above failure, and a season's rise of a metre and a half in its piezometers would spend more than a third of that. Our dam, at 1.116, would go below 1. So an engineer watches each piezometer's trend against the reservoir, and decides in advance the reading at which something must be done.

Peck's bargain

Most people take a dam that has stood for decades as proof that it is safe. Testalinden stood for seventy-three years and Mount Polley for eighteen, and both failed. Instruments let an engineer do better than wait. Ralph Peck, Terzaghi's co-author, set out the approach in his Rankine Lecture of 1969: the observational method. You design for the ground you think most likely, and before you build you decide what you will measure, what readings would mean the ground is worse than you assumed, and what you will do then. It is a bargain with ignorance: you need not know everything about the ground at the start, provided it tells you in time and you have a response ready.; the method as the tailings standard defines it, citing Peck: , glossary. Peck and Terzaghi wrote together.

Both conditions failed at Mount Polley, though its designers had invoked the method from the start. Its few instruments stood beyond the toe, because the steep slope could not be reached, and too far apart to cover the place where it broke. More fundamentally, as the panel wrote, "piezometers cannot measure pore pressures induced by undrained shearing because the location of the failure surface on which to measure them cannot be known in advance." Had they warned, there was no way to build a buttress in time. With the undrained failure never recognized, the panel concluded, the method had been reduced to "mere trial and error"., p. 77 (the method, the instruments' reach, the quotation, no time to respond), p. 80 (no instruments near the breach) and p. 107 (trial and error).

The lesson is now in the international standard for tailings facilities, published in 2020. It asks for the observational method in full for failures that are not brittle, and adds: "Brittle failure modes are addressed by conservative design criteria." A brittle failure, like that of Mount Polley's clay sheared too fast for its water to get out (), gives little warning: the soil loses strength once it starts to move. Against it, the only defence is margin built in before anyone is watching., Requirement 7.2.

Check yourself Why would even a piezometer on the slip surface give little warning of an undrained failure?

The pressure that weakens the soil appears only as it shears. By the time the piezometer reads it, the slip is already under way, and a soil that loses strength as it moves leaves no time to respond.

The law around the dam

An instrument nobody reads, or a reading nobody acts on, does nothing, and Testalinden had warnings on file for decades. Closing that gap is what regulation is for. In British Columbia a water dam answers to the Dam Safety Regulation, and a dam on a mine to Part 10 of the Health, Safety and Reclamation Code for Mines. Both have been rewritten since these two failures.

In 2010 Testalinden was rated "Low", which meant an audit about once in ten years; within two weeks of the failure the province had assessed 628 dams. The regulation of 2016 sorts dams into the Canadian Dam Association's five consequence classes, from low to extreme, by what a failure would do downstream, to lives, property and the environment. The class, not the dam's height, sets the duties. A dam of high consequence or above is checked on site weekly unless its manual says otherwise, inspected formally at least once a year, run from a written manual with an emergency plan, and given a dam safety review by an engineer every seven or ten years. Under today's scale Testalinden would have been significant, not low.The rating and the 628 dams: , p. 15. The class today, and the match with the Canadian Dam Association: . The classes and duties: , Schedules 1 and 2, ss. 8, 9 and 20.

Mount Polley rewrote the mining code. In July 2016 the province announced limits on the steepness of a tailings dam's downstream slope, a minimum factor of safety, flood and earthquake criteria, and an independent review board for every mine with a tailings facility. Every tailings facility and dam on a mine has an engineer of record, and if that engineer reports a safety problem the mine leaves unfixed, the manager must tell the chief inspector within 72 hours, or the engineer must. Some rules read like the panel's findings turned round. A downstream slope steeper than 2H:1V (two across for every one up) needs the engineer of record's justification and the regulator's approval; Mount Polley's stood at 1.3H:1V, and the panel found that a 2:1 slope would have avoided the failure. The factor of safety in operation must be at least 1.5. And the facility's manual must say what is to be done as readings worsen, where the piezometers of Mount Polley's failed embankment had no trigger levels at all.; , ss. 10.4.1, 10.4.3, 10.4.4, 10.5.8, 10.5.9 and its table 10-5, and 10.6.6. The slope: , p. iii; no trigger levels, p. 79.

The same ideas now cross borders. The Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management of August 2020 asks every operator that adopts it for an engineer of record, an accountable executive answering to the chief executive and, where a failure would be very high or extreme in consequence, an independent tailings review board. None of it makes a dam safe by itself. It puts named people and outside reviewers where Testalinden had letters in a file., Requirements 8.4, 8.7 and 9.1.

Check yourself Two earth dams are each 10 m high. One holds a farm pond above an empty valley; the other holds the same pond above a town. Which needs the more frequent review?

The one above the town. The consequence class, which sets how often a dam is inspected and reviewed, follows what its failure would do downstream, not its size.

What to remember

  • Grains pressing on one another, and the water between them taking that press away, explain about half the ways embankment dams fail. Water pouring over the top explains most of the other half.
  • Instruments read the ingredients of the calculation: piezometers read u, weirs read q, inclinometers find the slip. Nothing reads the factor of safety itself.
  • For our dam each tenth added to ru, about a metre and a half of water in the piezometers along the slip, costs about 0.18 of the factor of safety.
  • The observational method works when a failure gives warning and there is time to act. An undrained, brittle failure gives neither, so it must be met with margin in the design.
  • Regulation is part of the dam: a class set by what lies downstream, a named engineer, reviews, and readings with actions attached to them.

Questions

  1. Inspectors visited Testalinden in 1961, 1977, 1978, 1985, 1988 and 1992. List what they found. Which of those faults would a yearly walk along the crest still have shown in 2010, and what would it have taken to prevent the failure?

    Hint

    The opening pages and the case file name most of the faults. Then ask what the inspections lacked, not what they missed.

    A spillway reduced to a 12-inch pipe under a road; water rising to within a foot of the crest, so almost no freeboard; seepage near the outlet pipe; an outlet valve nobody could reach, so the lake could not be let down. A walk along the crest would have shown nearly all of them, as the inspections did. What was missing was action: an owner obliged to repair the dam, lower the lake or breach the bank as the letter of 1978 asked, and a regulator able to make that happen; a rating that matched what lay below; and a sign on the dam, so the hiker's report would have said "dam" and not "road". The review's twelve recommendations included the signs and a stronger regulation (, pp. 4–5 and 7–8).

    Run backwards: margin, then ru, then kPa, then metres of water.

    The margin is 1.628 − 1.5 = 0.128. As ru: 0.128 ÷ 1.77 = 0.0723. As water pressure: 0.0723 × 83.4 = 6.03 kPa. As head: 6.03 ÷ 9.81 = 0.61 m. Bishop's full sum on the drained circle gives 0.64 m. The alarm sits about two-thirds of a metre above today's reading. The drained dam's critical circle is shallower than the homogeneous dam's, so the same metre of water carries a larger share of its slices' weight.

    Hint

    At 85% the water breaks out 8 m up, the dam leaks 44.4 L/day per metre, and its factor of safety is 1.12.

    With 22.8 m of water the seepage breaks out 11 m up the slope instead of 8, the leak rises from 44.4 to 58.1 L/day per metre, and the factor of safety falls from 1.12 to 1.02. Go back to the beach where this book began, on an evening when the tide is coming in. The sea covers the firm, dark middle of the beach, and the sand there, already full of water, hardly changes. Then it reaches the castle you built from damp sand, whose walls stood straight up, held by the water clinging where the grains touch (). The first wave fills every gap, the pull is gone, and the walls slump into a low heap. The grains are the same grains; only the water has changed.

Pick up a handful before the next wave comes in: grains pressing on one another, and the water between them. Every earth dam in this book was made of that, and so is the ground under every dam you will ever stand on.
