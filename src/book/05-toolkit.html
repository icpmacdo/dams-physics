The toolkit: a self-check

The book uses arithmetic, a little trigonometry and no algebra beyond turning a formula round. These eight questions cover all of it, and each uses a number you will meet later. Answer in your head or on paper first, then open the answer.

1 · Powers of ten The workbench's rockfill has a permeability of 10⁻⁴ m/s; its clay, 10⁻⁹ m/s. How many times more easily does water pass through the rockfill?

10⁻⁴ ÷ 10⁻⁹ = 10−4 + 9 = 10⁵, a hundred thousand times. Dividing powers of ten subtracts their exponents: −4 − (−9) = 5.

2 · A slope as an angle Both faces of our dam slope at 2H:1V, two metres across for every metre up. What angle does each face make with the ground?

The tangent of the angle is rise over run: tan β = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5, so β = tan−1 0.5 = 26.6°. On a calculator, tan−1 is often marked atan or arctan, and it must be set to degrees.

3 · The area of a section Our dam is 24 m high, with a crest 6 m wide and both faces at 2H:1V. What is the area of its cross-section?

Split it into a rectangle under the crest and a triangle on each side. Each face runs 2 × 24 = 48 m across, so each triangle is ½ × 48 × 24 = 576 m². The rectangle is 6 × 24 = 144 m². In all, 1,296 m²: that many cubic metres of fill for every metre of dam, and the same 1,296 one-metre squares you will paint in the workbench at the end of the book.

4 · Mass and weight What does a 100 kg mass weigh, in kilonewtons?

Weight is mass times gravity: 100 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 981 newtons, or 0.981 kN. A tonne weighs 9.81 kN, and a cubic metre of water has a mass of one tonne, so it weighs 9.81 kN. That number turns up on almost every page.

5 · Pressure A tonne of water stands on one square metre. How hard does it press, in kilopascals?

9.81 kN spread over 1 m² is 9.81 kN/m², and a kilopascal (kPa) is exactly one kilonewton per square metre: 9.81 kPa. A column of water one metre deep presses that hard on its base; ten metres press 98.1 kPa.

6 · A log axis On an axis whose ticks read 10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁵, 10⁻⁴, what value sits exactly halfway between the first tick and the last?

10⁻⁵, the middle tick. On a logarithmic axis each step multiplies by the same factor, here ten, so halfway between 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁴ is the value ten times the first and a tenth of the last. The tempting answer, 5 × 10⁻⁵, sits much nearer the 10⁻⁴ end. The book needs one for permeability, which runs from 10⁻² m/s for gravel to 10⁻⁹ m/s for clay.

7 · Turning a formula round A formula says q = k · i · A. If q/A = 7.0 × 10⁻⁵ m/s and i = 0.70, what is k?

Divide both sides by i · A: k = (q/A) ÷ i = 7.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.70 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s. These are the numbers of the sand in Darcy's pipe in , and the formula is his law.

8 · Significant figures The seepage lab opens with 2,366 litres a day seeping past each metre of dam. Write that to two significant figures.

2,400. Keep the first two digits, 2 and 3, and let the next one, 6, round the 3 up. The lab works with typical soil properties, not measured ones, so two figures are as many as its answer deserves.
