This chapter takes the simplest water dam of all, a block of concrete that holds its lake by weight alone, and finds the block's weakness in it.

After this chapter you can

  • work out the weight, the push, the uplift and the factor of safety against sliding of a concrete gravity dam, with its drains working and clogged;
  • explain how a grout curtain and a line of drains spend the water's head before it reaches the base, and why the grip of the rock matters as much as the weight;

On 14 March 1884 the reservoir behind the new dam at Bouzey, in the Vosges hills of eastern France, was filling for the first time. The dam was a wall of stone masonry 22 m high, built to store water for the Canal de l'Est, and it stood on sandstone laid down in flat beds.Height, foundation and the events of 1884 and 1895: , pp. 16–17. The canal: , p. 104. With the water a little over 3 m below the crest, a 135 m length of the wall slid as much as 34 cm downstream on its foundation. It sheared off the key that anchored its heel in the rock, and the leaks below the dam jumped to 230 litres a second.

The wall moved no further. In 1888 and 1889 the engineers thickened its lower third with a buttress of new masonry on the downstream side and sealed the crack along the heel; a drainage gallery was proposed and turned down.The buttress: , p. 16; the 4 m crest, its Fig. BF-1. The gallery that was proposed and set aside: , p. 104. The upper two thirds kept their thin original section, 4 m thick at the crest.

On 27 April 1895, with the reservoir 55 cm below the crest, the upper wall broke away along 170 m of its length. The break ran almost level through the masonry about 10.5 m below the crest, well above the foundation, which this time did not move. The water went down the valley of the Avière through Bouzey and the villages below it. Accounts of the dead differ; the figure most often given is 87.The break: , pp. 16–17. The toll of 87: , p. 76; the same article gives 70 on p. 104, and , p. 17, "more than 100".

Engineers argued over the cause: the mortar, the thin upper wall. But masonry cannot lose its strength the way the loose wet sand of did, and nothing had shaken it. So what pushed the wall along its foundation in 1884, and what broke it in two in 1895? The answer that came out of Bouzey is the cushion of water from , at the base of a dam and in the cracks inside one.

The block, grown into a dam

Take the triangle of , 50 m tall and 40 m wide at its base, and lean it against a wall of concrete 8 m thick and 60 m tall, whose top carries a road. That makes the gravity dam of this chapter, 48 m wide at its base, drawn to scale in . Fill its reservoir to 57 m, 3 m below the crest, and let the river below it, the tailwater, run 5 m deep.Not every big water dam is concrete. British Columbia's tallest, Mica on the Columbia, is earthfill, 244 m high and nearly a kilometre thick at its base. Revelstoke, downstream, is a concrete gravity dam 175 m high (, slides 7 and 8; ).

Each metre of the dam is a slab of concrete 1,480 m² in section, and concrete weighs 23.5 kN/m³, so each metre of dam weighs 23.5 × 1,480 = 34,780 kN, or 34.78 MN. The reservoir pushes on its upright face with ½ × 9.81 × 57² = 15,936 kN (), and the river pushes back on the toe with 123 kN. The net push, F, is 15,814 kN, or 15.81 MN.

Weight holds a gravity dam the way it held the block in , by pressing on what is underneath: the dam is that block on level ground. The push F tries to slide it downstream; the rock grips back with the press times tan φ, where φ is the friction angle between concrete and rock. Take φ = 45°, so tan φ = 1, and count no glue. The factor of safety against sliding, the grip on offer divided by the grip needed, is then 34,780 ÷ 15,814 = 2.20: the opening question's dam, more than twice as heavy as the push on it.

But the block in also had a thin cushion of water under its base, and the question about the cushion showed what that does to a grip. The dam stands on rock, and rock is cracked.

Check yourself With only 30 m of water behind it, is the same dam about twice as safe against sliding?

Far safer than that. The push grows with the square of the depth, so at 30 m it is only ½ × 9.81 × (30² − 5²) = 4,292 kN, and the weight is 8.10 times that. A gravity dam is in most danger in its last few metres of water.

Water under the base

One could assume a dam this heavy would hardly notice water under it. Concrete is 2.4 times as heavy as water, and the water in a crack is a film millimetres thick. To test the idea, go back to the hole in the sand of , which fills from below because the water in the gaps is joined to the sea. The rock under a dam is cracked by joints and by the partings between its beds, and the reservoir fills them in the same way. At the heel, where the base meets the reservoir, the water in the cracks has the full pressure of the 57 m above it, 9.81 × 57 = 559 kPa. The film holds almost no water, but showed that a thin layer of water presses as hard as the depth it hangs from.

At the toe the water in the cracks is at the river's pressure, 49 kPa. In between it creeps from high head to low, and if the rock is the same all the way across, it loses its head evenly with distance, as the water did in the uniform sand of . So the pressure under the base falls in a straight line from heel to toe, pushing up on every square metre of the base. That push from below is called uplift, U. Before the numbers, a guess:

Nothing is done to stop the water: no drains, no seal. What share of the 60 m dam's weight does the uplift take away?

About two fifths, 42%. The comparison that matters is per square metre of base. The dam is on average only 30.8 m of concrete deep over its 48 m base, pressing the rock with 725 kPa. The water under it presses up with 304 kPa on average, the mean of 559 and 49. The next figure draws it to scale.

Notice that the uplift arrow is nearly as long as the push, although the cracks hold very little water: the uplift is pressure spread over 48 m of base. The wedge under the base does in the dam what the cushion did in . It carries part of the weight, so the dam presses on the rock with less, and the grip shrinks with the press. The factor of safety becomes

FS = (W − U) · tan φ ÷ F

W weight of the dam · U uplift on its base · F net push of the water, all per metre of dam (kN or MN) · φ friction angle between dam and rock. This is on level ground, with W − U the press left.

What it means. Nothing about the concrete changed. Water in the rock took away two fifths of the press that gives the dam its grip, and with it most of the margin the weight seemed to promise.

Drains, and what happens when they clog

The weight is fixed, and so is the lake. What an engineer can change is the water under the base, with two rows of holes. Near the heel runs a grout curtain: holes drilled deep into the rock in a line along the dam and pumped full of cement grout, to seal as many cracks as they can reach. A few metres downstream runs a line of drains, drilled into the rock from a gallery, a tunnel along the dam inside it, close to the base. Water that reaches a drain rises into the gallery and is led away, so the pressure at the drain line can be little more than the gallery's own.Foundation drains as 3-inch (76 mm) holes on 10-foot (3 m) centres, the rule below taken as conservative when they reach 40 to 50 per cent of the dam's height: , §8.10, p. 321.

How much do drains take off? Nobody can see into the rock, and the answer depends on which cracks the holes happen to cross. Designers start from a rule. The US Bureau of Reclamation's rule assumes that the head at the drain line is the tailwater's, plus a third of the difference between the reservoir and the tailwater:

hd = ht + (hr − ht) ÷ 3

hd head at the drain line · ht head of the tailwater · hr head of the reservoir, all in metres above the base. On either side of the drain line the pressure is taken to fall in a straight line.

In the language of , the water reaches the heel with 52 m of head to lose before it gets out at the toe. Uniform rock would spend it evenly; the curtain and the drains make it spend two thirds of it in the first 6 m.The one-third rule: , §8.10, pp. 320–321. The US Army Corps of Engineers is more cautious: its drains remove 25 to 50 per cent of the straight-line pressure above the tailwater's at the drain line, up to 67 per cent with tests to justify it (, para. 3-3.d(1)(c)). With no glue counted, a common target for this check is a factor of safety of 1.5 under the worst ordinary loads, a convention that leaves room for what nobody can measure, such as the real head in the cracks.1.5 for the worst static case when no cohesion is counted, 1.3 for the probable maximum flood: , p. 3-27, the table of factors for a no-cohesion analysis. The dam passes with its drains working and fails with them clogged.

But drains clog. Water seeping through rock and grout dissolves calcium out of them, and in the drain holes the calcium comes out again as a crust, like the scale in a kettle; silt and bacterial slime add to it.Drains are usually "plugged with calcium deposits, sediment, or biological growth": . Watching drain efficiency for clogging: , §3-2.4.3.2. A drain that passes no water lowers no pressure, and the wedge creeps back toward the straight line. That is what the gallery is for: engineers walk it to measure what each drain gives, read gauges set in some of the holes, and clean or redrill the holes that have gone quiet. A falling flow from a drain is as much a warning as a rising one.Uplift lifts more than dams. On 7 February 2017 the fast flow down the concrete chute of Oroville Dam's main spillway, in California, drove water through cracks and joints into the ground under a slab, probably faster than the drains there could carry it away. The forensic team concluded that the uplift most likely beat what the slab's weight, its anchors and the water on top could hold; the slab lifted out, and the flow tore into the poor rock beneath (, pp. S-1 and 33).

Check yourself Drains clog and a grout curtain needs no cleaning. Why not seal the rock with grout and leave out the drains?

Grout seals only the cracks it reaches, and a curtain ages. Water that finds a way past it arrives under the base with nearly the reservoir's head, and with no drains the wedge is the straight line again. The US federal regulator of hydropower dams allows no reduction in uplift for grouting alone., §3-2.4.3.2.

How hard concrete grips rock

Every number so far leaned on tan φ = 1, as the figure's readout says. The Bureau of Reclamation's guide gives a friction coefficient of 1 for concrete in a first design, and a federal regulator's worked example of a gravity dam uses 45°.Coefficient of internal friction 1.0 for concrete, for preliminary designs: , §8.6, p. 316. But a dam can also slide on a joint or a bedding plane a few metres down in the rock, which is only as rough as the rock's own partings, and those may be polished smooth or filled with soft material.

The middle third

Sliding is one way for the push to win. The other is tipping: the push turns the dam about its toe with a moment, as showed. Engineers check it by adding the weight, the push and the uplift into one force, the resultant, and asking where its line crosses the base.

Where the resultant crosses the middle of the base, the rock is pressed evenly. Move it downstream and the toe is pressed harder and the heel less, until the heel is not pressed at all: the pressure under the base, taken to change in a straight line, is then a triangle with its point at the heel, which balances a third of the way in from its tallest side (). So while the resultant stays between a third and two thirds of the base from the heel, in the middle third, the whole base stays pressed onto the rock. For the 60 m dam that is 16 to 32 m from the heel; the readout of puts the resultant at 0.56 B, 26.7 m, with the drains working and 0.62 B with them clogged. The same straight-line picture gives the pressure at the heel:

pheel = (V ÷ B) · (4 − 6x ÷ B)

pheel pressure of the dam on the rock at its heel (kPa) · V the downward force on the base (kN per metre) · B base width (m) · x where the resultant crosses the base, from the heel (m). At x = B/2 it gives an even press, V/B; at x = 2B/3, zero.

The middle third was the rule of the day at Bouzey. Lévy pointed out that it only keeps the joints from tending to open at their upstream ends, which is not enough: a crack can open anyway, from heat or a bad joint, and the reservoir fills it. A crack no thicker than a sheet of paper carries the full pressure of its depth (), which pushes its faces apart so that it grows.Lévy, an engineer and member of the Academy of Sciences, read his note on 5 August 1895. He compared the danger to a boiler without a safety valve, and proposed a valve: a guard wall on the upstream face with wells behind it, so that any crack opened into a well and drained instead of holding pressure. The wells would empty into a drain along the dam, whose flow would warn of what was happening inside (, pp. 288–289). Drains in the body of the dam later did the same job (, p. 104). So Lévy asked for more: at the upstream end of every joint, the masonry must press harder than the water presses there, and then water that finds a crack is squeezed back out.The usual condition and Lévy's new one: , p. 289. What to remember

  • A gravity dam is the block on the ramp, on level ground: its weight presses it onto the rock, and the rock's grip, (W − U) · tan φ, holds it against the water's push.
  • Water in the cracks under the base presses up with the reservoir's pressure at the heel and the river's at the toe. With nothing to stop it, it carried 42% of the 60 m dam's weight and cut its factor of safety from 2.20 to 1.28.
  • A grout curtain and a line of drains make the water spend most of its head near the heel; working drains raised the factor to 1.70. Drains clog, and the gallery is there so they can be measured and cleaned.
  • The friction angle multiplies everything: at 35° the same dam fell to 1.19.
  • The resultant must cross the base inside the middle third, or the heel stops being pressed. Lévy asked for more after Bouzey: the masonry at every joint's upstream end must press harder than the water there, so that water is squeezed out of a crack instead of into it.

  1. In one sentence, say how the uplift under a gravity dam is the water cushion under the block of .

    What did the cushion take away from the block, and what does the friction depend on?

    Both carry part of the weight on water, so the block or the dam presses on what is under it with less, and the friction, which comes from that press, shrinks while the push or pull trying to move it stays the same: W − U in is the "press left over" of .

    No: at 59 m it is still 1.57.   4. Clog the drains and raise the reservoir to 59 m. Where does the resultant cross the base, and is the heel still pressed?

    Hint

    The middle third runs from 0.33 B to 0.67 B. What happens to the heel pressure in as x approaches 2B/3?

    At 0.66 B, inside the middle third but close to its end, so the heel is still pressed, but barely. With V = W − U = 19,712 kN, gives 23 kPa at the heel, against an even press of 411 kPa. The factor of safety against sliding is 1.16. A crack opening at the heel would let in the full reservoir pressure and push the resultant further downstream.

  5. The US Army Corps of Engineers assumes that drains remove only 25 to 50 per cent of the straight-line pressure above the tailwater's at the drain line. With the reservoir at 57 m, what does the 60 m dam's factor of safety come to at each end of that range, and would you accept the Bureau of Reclamation's one-third rule for this dam without measurements?

    Hint

    With no drains the straight line stands at 50.5 m at the drain line; take 50 or 25 per cent of its height above the river's 5 m away.

    At 50 per cent the head at the drains is 5 + 0.5 × 45.5 = 27.75 m and the factor of safety 1.61; at 25 per cent, 5 + 0.75 × 45.5 = 39.13 m and 1.45, below 1.5. The one-third rule amounts to removing 62 per cent here, above the Corps' usual range and close to the 67 per cent it allows only when tests justify it (, para. 3-3.d(1)(c)). With the result this sensitive, a careful designer would want pressure gauges in the drain holes to show what the drains actually achieve, and would keep watching them.

  6. Lévy said that the masonry at the upstream end of every joint must press harder than the reservoir's water presses there. What does that prevent, and why is the middle-third rule alone not enough to prevent it?

    Hint

    Think about a crack that has already opened, and what pressure the water in it carries ().

    It prevents a crack, once open, from being pushed wider by the water in it: if the masonry presses the crack shut harder than the water pushes it open, water is squeezed out instead of forced in. The middle-third rule only keeps the face from being pulled apart by the weight and the push; it allows the face to be pressed with almost nothing, and any crack then fills with the reservoir's full pressure and spreads. At Bouzey's break the Bureau of Reclamation's back-calculation found the upper wall failing even the middle-third rule, with its face in tension ().

It is ground rock, sand and silt pumped in wet, and it can lose its strength altogether. What happens when a dam has to be built up, a few metres at a time, on top of the very waste it holds?
