Thus, it appears that for an identical sand, it can be assumed that the volume discharged is (directly) proportional to the head and inversely proportional to the thickness of the sand layer that the water passes through.

Henry Darcy, Les fontaines publiques de la ville de Dijon, 1856, in English as quoted by Simmons

So far. Still water presses harder the deeper you go, 9.81 kPa for every metre, and turned that into the push on a dam. Water in the ground is rarely still. Under a dam it creeps through the gaps between the grains. This chapter asks which way it goes, how fast, and what it does to the grains on the way.

After this chapter you can

  • find the total head at a point from a piezometer reading, and say which way the water there will flow;
  • use Darcy's law to estimate a flow, and how long a drop of water takes to cross a layer of soil;
  • explain why a thin layer of clay takes almost the whole drop in head, and how hard moving water pushes on the grains it passes.

Go back to the beach an hour or two after high tide, while the sea is still going out. Near the bottom of the beach, where the sand flattens toward the water, it is dark and shining, and thin streams of water run out of it and braid their way down the last few metres to the sea. They keep running for hours. Nothing is raining on the beach and nobody is squeezing it, yet the water keeps coming.

It comes out because the beach is full of it. At high tide the sea soaked into the sand and filled the gaps between the grains. As the tide falls, the water inside the sand falls too, but far more slowly, and it is left standing higher inside the beach than the sea outside. Where that buried water surface meets the sloping sand, it leaks out, and the streams are the leak.On a falling tide the water table in a beach comes apart from the sea and leaks out over a seepage face: , who tracked it on a beach in North Queensland.

That leaves two questions, and they are the questions of this chapter. What pushes the water out of the sand, when the sea it came from has gone? And why does it take hours?

Most people say water flows from high pressure to low pressure, the way air rushes out of a punctured tyre. It sounds right for the beach. But the rule fails in the simplest place there is. In a still lake the water at the bottom is at a far higher pressure than the water at the top: found 294.3 kPa thirty metres down, and zero at the surface. If water flowed from high pressure to low, the deep water would rise toward the surface and the lake would stir itself, forever. It lies still. So pressure alone does not decide where water goes. Whatever does decide it must be the same at every point of a still lake.

Head

To tell which way the water will go, engineers use a simple measure. Imagine pushing a clear straw down into wet ground and watching how high the water climbs inside it. The level the water reaches, measured from some fixed height such as sea level, is the total head at the bottom of the straw. Water in the ground creeps through the gaps between the grains, from where its head is high to where it is low.

The level in the straw adds two things. The first is how high the bottom of the straw is, its elevation head, z, measured from the fixed level, which is called the datum. The second is how far the water pressure at the bottom of the straw can push water up it, its pressure head. A pressure u holds up a column of water u ÷ γw tall, where γw = 9.81 kN/m³ is the weight of a cubic metre of water: one metre of column for every 9.81 kPa. Together:

h = z + u / γw

h total head (m) · z elevation head, the height of the point above the datum (m) · u water pressure at the point (kPa) · γw unit weight of water, 9.81 kN/m³. The datum can be any level you like, as long as you keep it: only differences in head move water.

Now the still lake makes sense. Go one metre deeper and you lose a metre of elevation head, but the pressure grows by 9.81 kPa, which is exactly one more metre of pressure head. The two trade evenly, so the total head is the same everywhere in still water, and with no difference in head nothing flows. The figure puts straws into a still lake, and then into the beach as the tide goes out:

Notice that in panel A the three straws reach very different depths, and the water in all of them stops at exactly the lake's surface. The pressures at their tips differ, but the heads don't, and nothing moves. In panel B the straws step down: the head inside the beach is highest far from the sea and lowest where the water comes out. That difference in head is what pushes the water out of the sand. The beach's water is still higher than the sea, and so it runs downhill to it, through the sand.

Two consequences surprise people. Groundwater can flow uphill, as long as it is heading toward lower head; a spring that bubbles up out of a hillside is water rising toward a point of lower head. And water can flow from low pressure to high. Follow a drop in panel B from close under the water table down and along through the sand: it goes deeper, into higher pressure, and all the while toward lower head. The rule, every time, is head.

Check yourself The middle straw in panel A has its tip 4 m into the lake bed, 12 m below the lake's surface. What water pressure does the tip feel, and why does the water in the straw stop at the lake's level?

9.81 × 12 = 117.7 kPa, a pressure head of 12 m. The tip is 8 m above the datum, so its head is 20 m, the same as the lake's surface: a straw shows the head, and in still water that is the surface everywhere.

The straws are real

Engineers sink thin pipes into dams and the ground beneath them and measure the water level inside. They are called piezometers, and reading them, along with measuring the water that seeps out, is one of the main ways engineers know what the water inside a dam is doing.A real straw takes time to fill. Water has to flow in through the ground to raise the level, and in tight ground that can be slow. Juul Hvorslev worked out the delay in 1951. For one fine-grained soil, his estimates ran from 193 days for a 2-inch cased boring with soil in its foot to 0.4 seconds for a small pressure cell set in a pocket of sand, which reads the pressure directly without waiting for a column to fill., his figure 14 and p. 37. Each one gives the head at a single point, a pinprick of knowledge inside a mountain of earth, so a dam carries many of them and engineers read them for as long as it stands.

Inside an earth dam they show what panel B shows. The water line in is a water table like the beach's: it starts at the reservoir's level on the upstream face and falls as it crosses the dam. A piezometer near the upstream face stands high, one near the downstream face stands low, and the water soaking into the dam runs between them, losing head as it goes. maps that water through a whole dam. Some ground is a sieve, some is a wall

Head says which way the water goes. How fast it goes depends on the ground, and above all on the size of the gaps. Gravel has gaps you can see. Clay is made of flat flakes hundreds to thousands of times smaller than a grain of sand, and the gaps between them are tiny and twisting. The same difference in head that sends water racing through gravel barely moves it through clay. To say how much, engineers need a number for the ground, and that number comes from an experiment run in a hospital courtyard in Dijon.

Darcy's law

In 1855 and 1856 Henry Darcy, the engineer who had built the water supply of Dijon in France some fifteen years before, ran water down through sand packed in an upright column 2.5 m tall and measured how much came out at the bottom.Darcy's water reached Dijon's reservoir at the Porte Guillaume on 6 September 1840, and he waived the fees the town owed him for it. The sand experiments, 35 of them, were run with the engineer Charles Ritter, using sand from the river Saône, and the mercury in their gauge shook with the water hammer of the street fountains at the hospital: Darcy's own water supply, rattling his own experiment. He published them in 1856 as Note D, an appendix to his book on the fountains of Dijon (; the story is told by , pp. 1025 and 1030–1031).The column, 2.50 m tall by Darcy's text (his plate marks 3.5 m) and 0.35 m across, and the dates: , pp. 1025 and 1030–1031. He found a simple rule. The flow grew in proportion to the difference in head between the top and the bottom of the sand, and shrank in proportion as the sand was made thicker: double the head difference and the flow doubles; double the thickness and it halves. What mattered was how steeply the head dropped along the way: the head lost for every metre the water travels. That slope is called the hydraulic gradient, written i. The flow through each square metre of ground is the gradient times a number that belongs to the soil, and that is Darcy's law:

q = k · i · A, so q / A = k · i, with i = Δh / L

q flow (m³/s) · A area the water flows through (m²) · q/A flow through each square metre (m/s) · k permeability (m/s) · i gradient · Δh head lost (m) · L length of the path (m)

Read the second form with i = 1, a metre of head lost for every metre travelled, and it says what k is: the flow through each square metre of ground under that push. That is why it has the units of a speed, metres per second. Engineers call it the permeability of the soil.Strictly, k in m/s is the hydraulic conductivity, and permeability is a property of the gaps alone, in m² (, section 2.3). Dam engineers say permeability for both, and so does this book. k depends on the water too: at 5 °C water is half again as viscous as at 20 °C, 1.52 against 1.00 mPa·s (), so the same sand passes cold water about two thirds as fast. The figures in this book use 10⁻² m/s for gravel, 10⁻⁴ for sand, 10⁻⁷ for silt and 10⁻⁹ for compacted clay.

Notice that the water in the tubes lines up in a straight slope from one tank to the other. The sand is the same all along the pipe, so every metre of it takes the same share of the head. Notice too that the readouts follow Darcy's rule: raise the left tank and the gradient and the flow grow together; lengthen the pipe and both shrink. Here is the arithmetic behind the readouts, and one more number the figure does not show:

How fast does water move through sand?

Given
Darcy's pipe as the figure opens: sand with k = 10⁻⁴ m/s, whose gaps take up 0.35 of its volume; the left tank at 2.00 m, the right at 0.60 m; a pipe 2.0 m long.
Find
The gradient; the flow through each square metre of pipe; how fast a drop of water moves, and how long it takes to cross the pipe.
The idea
Darcy's law gives the flow through each square metre (). But the water can only travel through the gaps, so to carry that flow it has to move faster than q/A.
  1. Δh = 2.00 − 0.60 = 1.40 mthe head at each end is its tank's level
  2. i = 1.40 ÷ 2.0 = 0.70head lost for each metre of pipe
  3. q/A = 10⁻⁴ × 0.70 = 7.0 × 10⁻⁵ m/sDarcy's law: cubic metres a second through each square metre
  4. In litres: 0.070 L/s, and 0.070 × 60 = 4.2 L/mina cubic metre is 1,000 litres
  5. v = 7.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.35 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/sthe same flow squeezed through the gaps, about a third of the area
  6. Time: 2.0 m ÷ 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/s = 10,000 slength of the pipe over the drop's speed
  7. 10,000 ÷ 3,600 = 2.8 hours3,600 seconds in an hour

Each square metre of pipe passes 4.2 litres a minute. A drop moves at 0.2 mm a second and takes 2.8 hours to cross the 2 m of sand.

Check. Swap the sand for silt, whose k of 10⁻⁷ m/s is a thousand times smaller. The flow should fall a thousandfold, to 7.0 × 10⁻⁸ m/s, which is 6.0 litres a day through each square metre: what the figure's readout shows with Silt.

What it means. Even through clean sand, losing seven tenths of a metre of head for every metre it travels, the water moves only about 0.7 m an hour. Seepage is slow, and the soils a dam is made of are slower still.

The last lines of that example hold a distinction worth keeping. The flow through each square metre of ground, q/A, is what a dam engineer adds up to find a leak. The speed of the water itself, the seepage velocity, is that flow divided by the gap fraction, n (engineers call it the porosity), and it is what tells you how long water takes to get anywhere.Why does head leave out the speed of the water? For a river or a pipe, engineers add a third term, the height the water's speed could lift it, v²/2g. For the drop in the sand pipe that is (2.0 × 10⁻⁴)² ÷ (2 × 9.81) = 2 × 10⁻⁹ m, two millionths of a millimetre. In the ground it is always that small, so it is dropped.

Back to the beach

That is enough to answer the second question from the beach. The sand there is like the pipe's, but the beach slopes gently, so its water table does too. Take the water table to fall 1 m over 20 m of beach, an illustrative gradient of 0.05. Then each square metre passes 10⁻⁴ × 0.05 = 5 × 10⁻⁶ m/s, and the water itself moves at that divided by the gap fraction, 1.4 × 10⁻⁵ m/s: about 5 centimetres an hour. A drop that soaked in 20 m up the beach needs about 16 days to reach the sea. The beach cannot empty in one low tide, or anything like it. It leaks steadily through the hours the tide is out, and the sea comes back and fills it again long before it is drained.

A metre of clay

Sand is the fast end of a dam's materials. The slow end is clay, and a guess first:

A one-metre block of compacted clay has water pressing on one side, with the water level 1 m higher than on the other side. How long does a drop of water take to get through the metre?

About eleven years, 11.1 by the figures' numbers. Through gravel, the same push gets a drop across in about 30 seconds. The race below runs four materials side by side. Its clock has to speed up enormously before the clay moves at all.

Given
A metre of each material (L = 1 m) under a gradient of 1. Gravel: k = 10⁻² m/s, gap fraction 0.30. Sand: 10⁻⁴, 0.35. Silt: 10⁻⁷, 0.40. Clay: 10⁻⁹, 0.35.

Check. Clay's time over gravel's is 3.5 × 10⁸ ÷ 30 = 1.17 × 10⁷. If gravel's 30 seconds were drawn a millimetre long, clay's bar would be that many millimetres, 11.7 km: the figure caption's twelve kilometres.

What it means. The gap fractions hardly differ; the permeabilities differ ten million times, and they decide the race. In a dam it is the choice of soil, far more than its thickness, that decides where the water is held back.

That enormous range is what makes it possible to build a dam out of soil at all. A dam needs a part that water can barely get through, and parts that let water out quickly, side by side. Clay does the first job. Sand and gravel do the second.

Layers in a line

But a dam's clay is usually a narrow strip inside a much wider bank of sand and rock. How can a strip a few metres thick hold back the head of a whole reservoir? Darcy's pipe can show it. Go back to it and put a plug of clay in its middle:

The pipe is the same length and the tanks are at the same heights, but now nearly all the head is lost across the short piece of clay. The tubes in the sand on either side read almost the same as the tank next to them. The same flow has to pass through every part of the pipe, because water can't pile up anywhere in between. The tight clay needs a far steeper gradient than the sand to push that flow through, so the clay takes almost the whole drop. Each piece of the pipe needs head in proportion to its length divided by its k, which works like a resistance:

The plug as resistances in a line

Given
The 2.0 m pipe with a clay plug 0.4 m long in its middle (k = 10⁻⁹ m/s) and sand on either side of it, 1.6 m in all (10⁻⁴ m/s); head difference 1.40 m.
Find
The share of the head the plug takes, and what it does to the flow.
The idea
The same flow passes every piece, and each piece needs head in proportion to L ÷ k, its resistance. Resistances in a line add.
  1. Sand: 1.6 ÷ 10⁻⁴ = 16,000 smetres over metres per second leaves seconds
  2. Clay: 0.4 ÷ 10⁻⁹ = 4.0 × 10⁸ sa fifth of the length, but a hundred thousand times tighter
  3. Together: 4.0 × 10⁸ + 16,000 = 4.00016 × 10⁸ sresistances in a line add
  4. Clay's share: 4.0 × 10⁸ ÷ 4.00016 × 10⁸ = 99.996%each piece takes head in proportion to its resistance
  5. k of the whole pipe: 2.0 ÷ 4.00016 × 10⁸ = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ m/sits length over its total resistance
  6. q/A = 5.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.70 = 3.5 × 10⁻⁹ m/s, 110 L a yearDarcy's law for the pipe as a whole; the gradient is still 0.70
  7. 7.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 3.5 × 10⁻⁹ = 20,000the flow without the plug, from , over the flow with it

The plug takes 1.39994 m of the 1.40 m of head; all the sand together loses 0.056 mm. The flow falls 20,000-fold, to 110 litres a year through each square metre.

Check. The share depends only on the resistances, so a material nearer clay's k should take a bigger share of the drop. Silt's 1.6 m has a resistance of 1.6 ÷ 10⁻⁷ = 1.6 × 10⁷ s, a total of 4.16 × 10⁸ s, and the plug's share falls to 4.0 ÷ 4.16 = 0.9615, the figure's 96.15% with Silt.

What it means. In a line of layers, the tightest one sets the flow and takes almost all the head, however thin it is. The others are left with almost none.

This is how a dam with a clay core works. A core of clay, much narrower than the dam around it, takes almost the whole drop in head, from the reservoir level down to the downstream side. The sand and rock downstream of it carry almost none of the drop, so the water pressure in the downstream half of the dam stays low. shows the zones that make this work.Water running along layers, instead of across them, meets them side by side, and then the most permeable layer carries most of the flow. Earth fill is spread and rolled in layers, and soil laid down by nature comes in beds, so both pass water more easily along their layers than across them. Hvorslev warned that because of such layering the horizontal permeability of a soil “often is many times greater than the vertical” (, p. 37).

Check yourself Suppose the plug were moved from the middle of the pipe to its left end. Would the flow change? Would the tubes read differently?

The flow would not change: the resistances add the same way in any order, so it stays 110 litres a year. The tubes would: all the sand would now be downstream of the plug, so every tube in the sand would read almost the right tank's level.

Water that pushes on grains

So far the water has been the thing that moves, and the grains have only been in its way. But every metre of head the water loses has gone somewhere. Water losing head is shoving its way past grains, and what it shoves, it pushes.

Take a slab of soaked sand one metre square and one metre thick, with water flowing straight up through it and losing 1 m of head on the way, a gradient of 1. If the water were still, the pressure under the slab would be higher than the pressure on top by the weight of the water between and no more, and the soaked sand would get only the lift of being under water. Flowing water needs a metre more head at the bottom to push itself through, so the pressure under the slab is higher than the still-water rule by another 9.81 kPa. That extra pressure, on one square metre, is an extra 9.81 kN pushing the slab up. The water does not rise any faster for it; it hands the push on to the grains, dragging on them as it squeezes past. In general, water that loses head at a gradient i pushes on the grains it passes, in the direction it flows, with a seepage force of

j = i · γw

j seepage force on each cubic metre of soil (kN/m³) · i gradient · γw unit weight of water, 9.81 kN/m³

This is also where the pore pressure of stops following its neat rule. Where water flows upward, the pressure grows faster with depth than 9.81 kPa per metre; where it flows downward, slower. The difference is the push on the grains. How big is it, set against what holds the grains down?

Nearly enough to float it

Given
Soaked sand weighing 20 kN per cubic metre, grains and water together (the sand of ); water at 9.81 kN/m³, flowing straight up through it at a gradient of 1.
Find
The seepage force on each cubic metre, and how it compares with the weight of the grains that is left once the water around them holds part of it up.
The idea
Under still water, soaked sand is buoyed up by the weight of the water it displaces, so the grains press down with the difference. Water flowing up pushes them up by i · γw on top of that ().
  1. j = 1 × 9.81 = 9.81 kN/m³upward, the way the water flows
  2. Grains' weight, under water: 20 − 9.81 = 10.19 kN/m³the soaked weight less the water's lift
  3. 9.81 ÷ 10.19 = 0.963the push up as a share of the weight down

Water flowing up through sand at a gradient of 1 pushes the grains up with 96% of the weight they have left under water. It nearly floats them.

Check. At a gradient of zero the water is still and the push is zero: the grains press down with their full 10.19 kN/m³. The push grows in step with the gradient, so it would match their weight at a gradient of 10.19 ÷ 9.81 = 1.04, only a little steeper than 1.

What it means. Grains that are pushed up nearly as hard as they weigh hardly press on one another at all. shows why that matters: a grain's grip on its neighbours comes from that press.

The same push works in any direction. Water seeping down through the upstream part of a dam presses its grains together harder. Water seeping out of a slope drags its grains outward and down, and that is what the moving water does at the foot of the beach, where the streams come out. In the heap with water draining out of its faces stood at 19° instead of 34°, and the drag is why. does the sum.

Harder. The force acts the way the water flows, here downward, adding 0.5 × 9.81 = 4.9 kN to each cubic metre's press. Water flowing down presses soil together; water flowing up lifts it.

What to remember

  • Water flows from high total head to low, where head is elevation plus pressure head, h = z + u/γw. In still water the head is the same everywhere, so high pressure at depth moves nothing.
  • Darcy's law: the flow through each square metre is k · i. Permeability runs from 10⁻² m/s for gravel to 10⁻⁹ for compacted clay, and a drop moves faster than k · i, because only the gaps carry it.
  • In a line of layers the tightest takes almost all the head: a clay plug a fifth of the pipe's length takes 99.996%. A core holds back a reservoir the same way, and a metre of clay takes about a decade to pass a drop.
  • Moving water pushes on the grains it passes, i · γw on each cubic metre. Flowing up at a gradient of 1 it carries 96% of the weight the grains have left under water, so they hardly press on one another: the water has taken their press away.

Questions

  1. Piezometer A has its tip 10 m above the datum, and the water in it stands 25 m above the datum. Piezometer B has its tip 2 m above the datum, with water to 22 m. Which way does the water between them flow? Which tip feels the higher pressure?

    Hint

    The water level in each pipe is the head at its tip. The pressure head is the height of that level above the tip.

    The heads are 25 m at A and 22 m at B, so the water flows from A toward B. But B feels the higher pressure: its tip has 22 − 2 = 20 m of water above it, against 15 m at A, so 9.81 × 20 = 196.2 kPa at B and 9.81 × 15 = 147.2 kPa at A. The water flows from lower pressure to higher, because it flows toward lower head.

  2. The pressure on the bed of a deep lake is far higher than at its surface. Why doesn't water flow up out of the lake bed toward the surface?

    Hint

    Push a straw into the lake bed. Where does its water stand?

    Every point in still water has the same total head. Going deeper trades elevation head for pressure head metre for metre, so a straw anywhere in the lake, or in the soaked bed under it, fills to the lake's surface (, panel A). With no difference in head there is no gradient, and Darcy's law gives no flow.

  3. With sand and the clay plug in Darcy's pipe, raise the right tank from 0.60 m to 1.80 m. What happens to the share of the head the plug takes, and to the flow?

    Hint

    The share depends only on the resistances of the pieces. The flow depends on the head difference too.

    The share stays at 99.996%, because the resistances haven't changed. The head difference falls from 1.40 m to 0.20 m, so the flow falls by 1.40 ÷ 0.20 = 7 times, from 110 to about 16 litres a year through each square metre.

  4. How long does a drop of water take to cross 2 m of the figures' silt under a gradient of 1?

    Hint

    Time is the gap fraction times the length, over k times the gradient. Silt: k = 10⁻⁷ m/s, gap fraction 0.40.

    0.40 × 2 ÷ 10⁻⁷ = 8.0 × 10⁶ s, which is 93 days: twice the race's 46 days for one metre, because at the same gradient the drop moves at the same speed and has twice as far to go.

  5. The core of is built twice as thick, 10 m, holding back the same 30 m of head. How do the leak and the time a drop takes to cross it change?

    Hint

    Doubling the thickness halves the gradient. What does that do to the flow, and to the speed of a drop that also has twice as far to go?

    The gradient halves to 3, so the leak halves, to about 7.8 litres a day per metre of dam. The drop moves at half the speed over twice the distance, so it takes four times as long: 4 × 9.24 = 37 years.
