All the measurable effects of a change of the stress, such as compression, distortion and a change of the shearing resistance are exclusively due to changes in the effective stresses.

Karl Terzaghi, The shearing resistance of saturated soils, 1936

After this chapter you can

  • work out the factor of safety of a block on a ramp, with and without water under it;
  • apply σ′ = σ − u to a column of soil, and say why a lake on top changes nothing;
  • derive the critical gradient, and the steepest face a sand can hold dry, under still water and with water seeping out of it.

A wet floor is slippery. So is a wet road, a wet rock in a stream, a wet bar of soap. Everyone learns from the soles of their feet that water greases things. Most people say the same of sand: wet sand is slippery sand, and a bank that slumps after rain has been greased.

asked you to keep that answer in mind, and it is already in trouble. The damp castle has water in it and stands with upright walls, steeper than the dry heap beside it. If water greased the grains, the castle would be the first to fall.

There is also a direct test. In 1962 two engineers, H. M. Horn and Don Deere, slid polished pieces of minerals over one another, first oven-dried and then soaked in water. Clear quartz, the commonest mineral in sand, gripped with a friction of 0.10 dry and 0.23 wet: more than twice as hard with water on it., their friction coefficients for three surface-moisture conditions: clear quartz 0.10 oven-dried, 0.10 air-dried, 0.23 saturated.Not every mineral behaves like quartz. In the same tests the flaky sheet minerals went the other way: muscovite mica fell from about 0.43 dry to 0.23 soaked, and chlorite from 0.53 to 0.22 (). Clay is made of flakes of this family, which is one reason clay comes back later in this book as a different kind of soil.

So water isn't greasing the grains. What it does instead was worked out by Karl Terzaghi, the engineer who founded soil mechanics in the 1920s, and the rest of this book stands on it. By the end of this chapter it explains why water can make the castle stronger, why a heap under still water keeps its slope, the 19° face that left unexplained, and the number every later chapter uses to say whether a slope will stand. To see it, start with something simpler than sand.

Friction comes from pressing

Put a book on a plank and slowly tilt the plank. The book stays put until the slope gets steep, then it slides. Friction holds it, and friction comes from the book pressing on the plank. Press twice as hard and it takes twice the force to slide it. The friction on offer is always a fixed fraction of the press, and that fraction is what Horn and Deere measured.

The fraction sets a steepest angle for each pair of surfaces. Tilt beyond it and the book slides, whatever it weighs. Engineers call it the friction angle, written φ, and the fraction is its tangent, tan φ. For sand as a whole it is about 34°, the same angle as the dry heap in , because the surface of a heap is a ramp of sand resting on sand. Polished quartz grips far less than that: at Horn and Deere's dry 0.10, a polished block would slide off a polished ramp tilted past about 6°., clear quartz oven-dried; the angle whose tangent is 0.10 is 5.7°.That dry 0.10 says as much about the surface as about the mineral. Later tests at MIT, in a vacuum chamber, found smooth quartz gripping with anything from about 0.1 to 1.0 depending on how clean its surface was, while rough surfaces varied much less (). None of the tests found water making quartz slippery. Real grains are rougher than a polished block, and they also interlock: to move, they have to roll up and over one another. Both lift sand to its 34°.

The weight drops out of that angle, and a block on a ramp shows why. Put a block of weight W on a ramp tilted at an angle a. Gravity pulls it straight down, and the ramp feels that pull in two parts. The part pressing straight into the ramp is W cos a, and the part pulling the block down the slope is W sin a. Friction can offer up to the press times tan φ. So the block holds as long as W cos a · tan φ is at least W sin a. The weight is on both sides and cancels: the block holds while tan a is less than tan φ, and slides when the ramp passes φ, heavy or light. That is why the dry heap of keeps one slope however much sand you pour on it.

A designer needs more than "holds" or "slides". The dam in had been checked and found to hold; how comfortably it held was a number, and that number is the factor of safety, the friction available divided by the friction needed:

FS = N′ · tan φ ÷ (W · sin a)

N′ the press left between the block and the ramp (kN) · tan φ the friction fraction · W sin a the pull down the ramp (kN). For a dry block N′ = W cos a, and the factor of safety is tan φ ÷ tan a.

Above 1 the block holds. Below 1 it slides. At 1.5 the ground could lose a third of its grip before anything moved, and about 1.5 is what dams are designed to, to leave room for everything nobody can measure exactly.1.5 is a convention, not a law of nature. The US Army Corps of Engineers asks a new embankment dam for 1.5 under steady seepage with the reservoir full, 1.3 at the end of construction, and 1.1 to 1.3 right after a fast drawdown of the reservoir; it says such values "continue to be based on experience" (, p. 3-1 and its table of minimum factors on p. 3-2). The lower values are for conditions that last a short time. Every later chapter of this book uses the same definition, applied to a dam's slope instead of a block.

Now slip a thin cushion of water under the block's base. Make a guess before you see it:

A block rests on a ramp tilted to 25°, holding comfortably. A thin water cushion sits under its base. You pump up the water pressure in the cushion. The block's weight, shape and surface stay exactly the same. What happens?

It slides. The water carries part of the block's weight, so less weight is left pressing the block onto the ramp. Friction comes from that press, so it shrinks too, while gravity pulls the block down the slope as hard as ever. Try it below.

Start with the dry block, before any water. Notice that its two bars stand in the ratio of the factor of safety: 12.2 kN of friction available against 8.5 kN needed, so 1.45. Then notice what the water touches. As the cushion's pressure rises, the press left over falls and the friction bar shrinks with it, kilonewton for kilonewton times tan 34°. The bar for the friction needed doesn't move at all, because the water pushes straight up from the ramp and has no part along it. The block slides when the two bars meet, though its weight, its shape and its surface never changed. Here are the readouts by hand:

Check yourself Two dry blocks sit on the same 25° ramp, one twice as heavy as the other. Which has the larger factor of safety?

Neither. Doubling the weight doubles the press and the pull alike, and a dry block's factor of safety, tan 34° ÷ tan 25° = 1.45, has no weight in it.

Soil is thousands of little blocks

Now shrink the block down to a grain of sand. A handful of soil is a crowd of grains, each resting on its neighbours at a few points of contact. Every contact is a tiny block on a tiny ramp, held by friction, and that friction comes from how hard the grains press on each other. The strength of soil is the friction of all those contacts added up.The press is not shared evenly. In experiments with a layer of plastic disks that glow between polarizing filters where they are squeezed, the load travels along branching chains of disks while their neighbours carry much less, and when the pile is sheared the chains line up with the push (). Soil mechanics works with the average over many grains, which is what σ′ is.

But fill the gaps with water and something has to carry the water's pressure. To see where it goes, picture a level surface one metre square, deep in soaked sand, that threads its way through the gaps and crosses the grains only where they touch. Everything above that surface, grains and water together, presses down across it. That is the total stress, written σ. The contacts between grains are tiny, so the water in the gaps meets almost all of the surface, and it presses on all of it with its own pore pressure, u. The water therefore carries u of each square metre's σ, and only the rest is left for the grains to carry, through their contacts, by pressing on one another. Terzaghi called that rest the effective stress:Karl Terzaghi, an Austrian engineer, worked the idea out while teaching at Robert College in Constantinople (now Istanbul), in a laboratory he built partly from cigar boxes and kitchenware, and published it in Erdbaumechanik in 1925 (; ). At the first international conference on soil mechanics, at Harvard in 1936, he put it in the sentence that heads this chapter (, p. 54).

σ′ = σ − u

Friction comes from pressing, so the most shear stress the grains can resist, their strength, is that press times the friction fraction:

τ = σ′ · tan φ′

σ total stress, the weight of everything above, per square metre (kPa) · u pore pressure (kPa) · σ′ effective stress, how hard the grains press on one another (kPa) · τ shear strength, the most shear stress the soil can carry on a plane (kPa) · φ′ the soil's friction angle, with a prime because it goes with the effective stress; 34° for this sand, tan φ′ = 0.6745

The water does to the grains exactly what the cushion did to the block: it takes over part of the load they would otherwise carry by pressing on one another, and it adds no grip of its own.

To use the rule you need σ at a depth, the weight of everything above it, and for that you need the soil's unit weight, the weight of one cubic metre. Damp sand weighs about 18 kN per cubic metre. Soaked, it weighs about 20, because water has filled the gaps that air held. Below the water table the water in the gaps joins up like a lake, so in still groundwater u grows by 9.81 kPa for every metre below the water table, the rule of . The figure below stacks ten metres of this sand and lets you move the water table, from below the column to three metres above the ground:

Notice first what happens as the water table rises from below the column to the ground. The sand gets heavier, because water is replacing air in its gaps, yet the effective stress at 10 m falls from 180 to about 102 kPa. That is 43% of the soil's strength gone, and no grain had to move. Below the water table each extra metre of sand adds 20 kPa to the total stress and 9.81 to the pore pressure, so the grains feel only the difference, 10.19 kPa a metre: the sand's buoyant unit weight, written γ′. Above the water table they feel all 18.

Notice second what happens once the water rises above the ground. Every extra metre of lake adds the same 9.81 kPa to the total stress and to the pore pressure, so the effective-stress line stops moving. A lake sitting on soil doesn't press its grains together any harder, however deep it is. Here are the numbers:

Lighter and stronger. σ falls from 200 to 192 kPa, but u falls further, from 98.1 to 58.9, so the grains press with 133.1 kPa instead of 101.9.

One line for the strength

Every strength in this chapter so far has been a press times the same fraction. So if you plot strength against effective stress, every state of this sand, wet or dry, deep or shallow, lands on one straight line through zero, rising at the friction angle:

Notice that the water never moves the line. Flooding slides the column's point down along it, to a smaller press and so a smaller strength; the sand's friction angle is the same throughout. That is the whole effect of still water on sand. Engineers call this line the strength envelope. Some soils, clays among them, keep a little strength even when the grains are barely pressed, called cohesion and written c′; their line starts at c′ instead of zero, τ = c′ + σ′ tan φ′. Clean sand has none.

Water that flows up

Still water, however deep, only takes away the press that its own pressure accounts for. But ended with water that flows, and flowing water drags on the grains it passes: water losing i metres of head for every metre it travels pushes on the grains in each cubic metre with i × 9.81 kN, in the direction it flows.

Turn that flow upward, as it does where water that has passed under a dam comes up again on the far side. Soaked sand presses down with its buoyant unit weight, 10.19 kN per cubic metre. The water flowing up through it pushes up with i × 9.81. Raise the gradient and the upward drag eats into the downward press, until at one gradient they cancel. There the grains still touch but no longer press on one another, the effective stress is zero, and the sand has no grip at all. That gradient is the critical gradient:

ic = γ′ ÷ γw = (γsat − γw) ÷ γw

γsat unit weight of the soaked soil (kN/m³) · γw unit weight of water, 9.81 kN/m³ · γ′ buoyant unit weight, γsat − γw

Find

The water's upward drag, i × 9.81 per cubic metre, against the grains' buoyant weight ().

What it means. No sand anywhere is much stronger than this against water rising through it. Where seepage comes up at the downstream foot of a dam, a gradient near 1 makes the ground heave or boil, and follows what happens next.

The beach face, worked out

Everything so far has been level ground. A slope adds a pull, and that brings back the two wet heaps of . A heap under still water stands about as steep as a dry one. The water carries part of each grain's weight, so the grains press on each other less, but the same buoyancy lightens their pull down the slope by the same share, and the angle depends only on the ratio of the two. The heap slumps to about 19° only where water drains out through its face, because the flowing water drags the grains downhill as well, the drag of . promised the sum that turns that drag into degrees.

It needs one more idea. A beach face, or the long face of a dam, is so long and even that every patch of it is like every other. Engineers call it an infinite slope, and its great convenience is that one grain on the face, or one cubic metre of it, stands for the whole. Take that grain as a block on a ramp of sand and draw its forces in the three states:

Notice that panels A and B have the same shape. Under still water every arrow shrinks by the same share, because buoyancy takes the same fraction off the grain's weight wherever it points, and so the angle stays at 34°. Notice then what is new in C. The grain weighs what it did in B, and its press into the face is no bigger, but a second arrow has joined the pull: the drag of the water flowing past it. Friction comes only from the press, so the face must flatten until the press's friction catches up. Here is the sum promised:

tan β = (γ′ ÷ γsat) · tan φ′

What it means. The 19° in was right; only couldn't say why yet. Soaked sand weighs about twice as much as water, so water seeping out of a face roughly doubles the pull on its grains and roughly halves the tangent of the steepest slope. The downstream face of an earth dam, where seepage comes out, is this face at the scale of a hill, and the drains of are there to keep the water from reaching it.

Check yourself Water seeps out of the face of a sand with heavier grains, soaked at 22 kN/m³ instead of 20, with the same friction angle. Does its face stand steeper or flatter than 19°?

Steeper. Its buoyant weight is a larger share of its soaked weight: γ′ ÷ γsat = 12.19 ÷ 22 = 0.554, so tan β = 0.554 × 0.6745 = 0.374 and β = 20.5°. The heavier the grains against the water, the less the water's drag counts.

The sentence, proved

Turn back to the title page and read the sentence there. You can now prove it:

Soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and the water between them can take that press away.

The first half is : strength is the grains' press times a fixed fraction, and nothing else. The second half is : whatever pressure the water has, it subtracts from that press. Every result in this chapter is one of the two halves at work. Water in the cushion took 31% of the block's press and slid it. Flooding the column took 43% of the grains' press. Water flowing up at a gradient of about 1 takes all of it. Water seeping out of a face adds a pull and no press, and the face falls from 34° to 19°.

The castle of seemed to contradict the sentence, because there the water makes the sand stronger. It obeys it too. In damp sand the water hangs in small rings between the grains, and the curved skin of each ring pulls, so the water's pressure is below the air's: u is negative. Subtract a negative and σ′ = σ − u is larger than σ. The water adds to the press instead of taking it away, by the same rule with the sign turned over (in damp sand only part of that pull counts, because the water touches only part of each grain). puts a number on that pull and shows why it can hold up a wall a few tens of centimetres tall and never a 30 m one.

The sentence doesn't cover everything. Water that pours over the top of a dam washes it away whatever its grains are doing, and takes up that story. But for what soil does with the water inside it, this is the rule the rest of the book applies.

What to remember

  • Friction comes from pressing: the grains' strength is their press times tan φ′, and the factor of safety is the friction available over the friction needed. A dry block, or a dry heap, slides at φ whatever it weighs.
  • Water pressure takes part of the grains' press and none of the pull: σ′ = σ − u. Under the 25° block, 31% was enough to slide it.
  • A rising water table makes soil heavier and its grains press less; a lake on top adds to σ and u alike and changes nothing.
  • Water flowing up at the critical gradient, about 1, takes the grains' whole press away.
  • Water seeping out of a face adds a pull and no press, so the steepest face falls to tan β = (γ′/γsat) tan φ′: 19.0° for the beach.

  5. Below the downstream foot of a dam, water rises through the sand of the column (20 kN/m³), losing 0.6 m of head for every metre it climbs. What share of the grains' press is left?
