At half past ten on the night of 3 August 2014, the night shift's logbook at the Mount Polley mine, in the hills of central British Columbia, recorded three things about the tailings dam: "Good berm, good slope, no visible cracks." (A berm is a bank or ledge of earth.), the timeline of the night, p. 10. The mine is about 65 km northeast of Williams Lake: , p. 1. At a quarter to twelve another worker drove along the top of the dam, across the stretch that was about to fail, and back again, and noticed nothing unusual.

At a quarter to one, the water in a small pond below the dam, which collects what seeps through it, began to rise. By one o'clock it was rising fast. At a quarter past one the lights went out in the electrical shop and the mill shut down. At five past two an operator found that the dam had been breached.The same timeline, p. 10, from the mine's reports and the panel's interviews.

What came out was more than water. A tailings dam holds back tailings: the rock that is left once the copper and gold have been taken out of it, ground to sand and silt and pumped in as a slurry. Over the next sixteen hours the gap kept widening, and between 21 and 25 million cubic metres of water and tailings came through it, about ten million of them water from the pond., p. 13; the range is the mine owner's estimate. Spread a metre deep, that would cover 21 to 25 square kilometres.

It washed away a stand of forest and ran two ways. Some went into Polley Lake, where the debris plugged the lake's outlet and raised its level by about a metre. The rest went down Hazeltine Creek, a narrow stream, and scoured it into a mudflow 150 metres wide that ran 7 kilometres to Quesnel Lake, one of the deepest lakes in the province., p. 13; on who was below the dam, p. 160. Nobody was at work below the dam that night, or out on the lake or the creek, and nobody was killed.

A dam that passed

The dam had been watched. Its first, low embankment, the starter dam, was built in 1996, and it had been raised in stages ever since, a few metres at a time, as the mine filled it; the ninth stage was being finished that summer. By that night it stood about 40 metres high where it failed, its crest 1 metre short of the height it was permitted to reach, and that evening the pond stood 2.3 metres below the crest (taken, as the review panel took it, at the top of the dam's core, the watertight zone inside it): its freeboard. Engineers designed each raise and checked it, the dam's engineers inspected it once a year, and the province's geotechnical inspectors, engineers who specialize in soil and rock, visited the site., pp. 5 and i; , pp. 3, 5 and 47.

The checks said it was safe. When the side of the dam that later failed was checked during the raise of 2007 to 2011, its calculated factor of safety was 1.7; its designers required 1.3., p. 64; for 2012, p. 79. A factor of safety is the strength holding a slope up divided by the push trying to bring it down, so 1.7 meant 70 per cent more strength than the slope needed. In 2012 its engineers judged the numbers high enough that the instruments measuring water pressure in that side needed no alarm levels.

So how does a dam that passed its calculations fail in a night? The province's Chief Inspector of Mines, in his own investigation, put the puzzle plainly: "The failure of the dam took place suddenly, without any warning signs.", p. 13. The province, with the Williams Lake and Soda Creek Indian Bands, set up a panel of three engineers to find out why. It was chaired by Norbert Morgenstern, whose name is on one of the standard methods of checking whether a slope will stand, and one of its members, Steven Vick, had written a standard textbook on tailings dams., pp. i and 1; the textbook is . They had the site drilled and sampled and the samples tested, read the design reports of eighteen years, and reported in January 2015.

Their answer is in the last chapter of this book, and by then you will be able to follow it: to see what they found, and why the calculations said the dam was safe.
