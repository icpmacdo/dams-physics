After this chapter you can

  • design a section that passes the leak, piping and slope checks, on both of its faces;
  • weigh what each fix costs, in fill, clay or water, against the safety it buys;
  • say what the slope check leaves out, and why a dam can pass it and still fail.

When the review panel opened Mount Polley's design files, it found that the side of the dam that later failed had been checked the way checks a slope. Trial slip surfaces were drawn through it, the grip the ground could offer along each was set against the weight trying to slide it, and the weakest surface gave a factor of safety. During the raise of 2007 to 2011 that number was 1.7, where the designers required 1.3. The Chief Inspector of Mines found that, on the engineers' understanding of the ground, the calculated factor of safety of that embankment was 1.5 or more., p. 64 (1.7 against 1.3); , p. 2 (1.5 or greater). By the rules of the trade the dam was safe, and on the night of 4 August 2014 it gave way ().

Before finding out what that number missed, design a dam yourself and run the same kind of check on it. This chapter hands you a workbench wired to the tools of the last four chapters, and five missions, each a brief of the kind engineers are given. The last is Mount Polley, rebuilt, and your check will pass it too.

A workbench with three checks

The workbench is our dam, the 24 m embankment of , drawn on a grid of 1 m cells. You paint the cells with five materials, choose the ground it sits on, and set the reservoir. Then the page tests your design with two calculations.

The first is the seepage solver of the lab in . It finds where the water inside your dam settles, how much leaks through, and how hard it pushes on the soil where it comes out.

The second asks whether the downstream slope would slide. It tries more than two thousand curved surfaces through the slope (1,960 on a grid, then a few hundred more homing in on the weakest), each a possible slip surface, and on a foundation with a weak layer it also tries flat-bottomed blocks that slide along the layer. On each it adds up, slice by slice as in , the grip the soil can offer (friction, plus the cohesion of a clayey fill) against the pull of the soil's weight trying to slide it downhill. Friction depends on how hard the grains press together, so wherever the seepage solution puts water pressure on a surface, the grip there drops.

Grip divided by pull is the factor of safety, the number of the block on the ramp in and of the slices in . Below 1 the slope slides. With the reservoir full, dam engineers usually want 1.5 or more, to cover what they can't know about the soil., table 3-1, p. 3-2: 1.5 for long-term steady seepage in a new earth or rockfill dam; 1.1 to 1.3 after a rapid drawdown. A third verdict, the upstream slope after the reservoir is emptied fast, waits for the fourth mission. The page reports the three checks in the language of the chapters that built them:

Leaks

Seepage in litres per day for each metre of dam, the flow of . Some is normal. A lot means lost water, or polluted water for a tailings dam.

Pipes

Where water leaves unprotected soil, it pushes on the grains, as in . Rising out of level ground it lifts them; out of a slope it drags them along the face, and the same number is a guide to how hard. Safety against piping is the push that would float sand away divided by the push it gets. For water rising out of level ground the US Bureau of Reclamation wants at least 3 for an existing dam and 4 for a new one (about 2 to 2.5 where the soil is well measured); the missions here ask for 3 at every exit., section 8.2.2.2, p. 8-7. The number warns of sand boils; it can't see every kind of internal erosion.

Slides

The lowest factor of safety of any slip surface at least 2 m deep, as in . Shallower slips are surface damage that maintenance fixes.

Mission 1: dry the slope

The first brief is the dam that opened : compacted fill on clay, standing at 1.12 with water breaking out 8 m up its downstream face. There you chose a fix on paper. Here you paint it. Before you do, it is worth asking whether you could have known where the water would come out without any solver at all. Dam designers had a rule of thumb for it long before computers:, section 6.3(c), p. 190, after Justin, Hinds and Creager: in a completely homogeneous dam, seepage "will emerge on the downstream slope regardless of its flatness", to "roughly one-third the depth of the reservoir pool". in a dam of one material, water held long enough behind it will break out on the downstream slope at about a third of the reservoir's depth, however flat that slope is.

What it means. Flattening a dam of one material keeps its face wet; the water comes out at the same height and has further to go to the toe. That is why the Bureau of Reclamation's manual says the fully homogeneous section "should seldom be used" (, p. 190), and why the first fix to try is a drain.

Check. The flatter slope adds weight on the toe and lengthens the slip, but the water still breaks out 8 m up the face () and still carries part of the weight on the slip surface. The drain removes that water instead, and on the dam as built the water carried 0.353 of the weight of the soil on the weakest slip.

What it means. No fix is free. The drain's cost is paid in water: it collects what reaches it, so the dam now leaks 2.47 times as much, 110 L/day per metre against 44.4, and leads it away where it can do no harm.

Check yourself You paint a block of filter sand inside the downstream half of the fill, but not joined to the toe. Will it help as a drain does?

Barely. Water that collects in a drain has to leave it; a patch with no way out fills up, and the water in it sits at the same pressure as the fill around it. The workbench treats such a patch as permeable soil, and drains only sand joined to the toe along the base.

Mission 2: hold the water

The second brief is the opposite problem. A dam of rockfill on sand and gravel stands well but leaks like a sieve: 32,490 L/day for each metre, 93% of it through the dam itself. The brief asks for 100 L/day or less without losing the slope or the piping safety, and with no more than 350 m³ of clay per metre. One could assume that a clay core, the watertight wall of , settles it. Here is what happens when you paint one:

Check. The core passes water a hundred thousand times more slowly than the rockfill it replaces (10⁻⁹ against 10⁻⁴ m/s), yet the leak fell only sixfold. A mismatch that large means the water had another way round, and the workbench's verdict names it: of the cored dam's leak, 99.98% passes under the dam, through the gravel.

What it means. Before choosing a fix, find where the water goes. The cutoff is useless on its own (the water would still pour through the rockfill) and nearly everything once the core is in.

Mission 3: tailings on a budget

The third brief comes from a mine. It wants at least 70% of the dam built of cycloned tailings sand, the coarse part of its own waste (), because that is far cheaper than hauling fill from a borrow pit. Built of tailings sand alone at 2:1 the dam scores 0.64 and slides. The sand has no cohesion, and water seeping out of a slope of it takes about half its grip (). The answer is the first mission's drain with a flatter face: a chimney and blanket drain inside tailings sand at 2.5:1 scores 1.63 with 90% of the dam in sand. Its price is again paid in water, and here it is a large price: the drain collects 7,980 L/day per metre, polluted water that a real mine must catch below the dam and pump back.

Mission 4: empty it fast

Every brief so far tested the downstream slope with the reservoir full. The fourth tests the other face. A reservoir sometimes has to be emptied in a hurry, to reach a damaged outlet or to take the load off a dam in trouble, and showed what that does: while the water stands against the upstream face it presses the slope in place, and when it goes, the water trapped in fill and clay stays, pushing out with nothing pushing back. This is rapid drawdown, and for such a short-lived event engineers accept a lower factor of safety, 1.1 to 1.3 depending on the case;, table 3-1, p. 3-2: 1.1 from maximum surcharge pool, 1.3 from maximum storage pool. the mission asks for 1.2. Empty the dam of fill and its upstream slope scores 0.94.

The obvious remedy is a coat of rockfill on the face, since rockfill drains as fast as the reservoir falls. Paint a skin 5 m thick and test: the factor of safety goes down, to 0.89. The weakest slip runs deeper instead, behind the skin, through fill that still holds its water. A free-draining zone helps only if the slip cannot get round it. Rockfill over the whole upstream part of the dam, from the face in to 3 m short of the crest, scores 1.24; carried in to the crest's edge it scores 1.32; stopped 7 m short, 1.14, not enough. All rockfill drains best of all, 1.41, and leaks 29,621 L/day per metre, which is why the brief caps the leak at 1,000.

Now flip the view back to the downstream slope. The upstream shell has made it worse, 1.03 against 1.12: the reservoir's water now crosses the rockfill without losing head and reaches the fill with nearly all of it. So the shell solves one problem by handing the next zone a harder one. Combine it with the first mission's drain (start from Fill + drain, then paint the upstream shell) and the dam passes everything at once: downstream 1.63, upstream after a fast drawdown 1.30, a leak of 240 L/day per metre and a piping safety over 50. A free-draining shell upstream, a fill that holds the water in the middle, a drain that catches it downstream: you have rebuilt the zoned dam of from its jobs.

Check yourself The downstream drain does almost nothing for the upstream slope after a drawdown (1.05 against 0.94). Why?

A drawdown's danger is the water trapped in the upstream fill once the reservoir has gone. A drain under the downstream half lowers the water there, far from the upstream slip, which still runs through fill that holds its water.

Mission 5: Mount Polley

The last brief is the dam of the Prologue, scaled to the workbench: a clay core between rockfill shells, on clay, with a weak layer in the ground below. In the real foundation the weak layer lay 8 to 10 m under a dam almost 40 m high, about a fifth of its height down;, p. 135 (the layer) and p. 75 (the height). the workbench's lies 4 to 6 m under a 24 m dam. The layer has no cohesion and a friction angle of 19°, against 28° for the clay around it. The brief asks for a downstream factor of safety of 1.5. The dam starts at 1.21. Decide first how you would spend your money:

In a drain was the best of three fixes. This dam stands on a weak layer at 1.21. Which change lifts it most?

The flatter slope: 1.21 becomes 1.56. The drains move it to 1.215, almost nothing, and the cutoff to 1.28. The layer is weak because of what it is, not because of water a drain could reach: it lies sealed in clay below the dam. What holds the sliding block back is weight bearing on the layer beyond the toe, and a longer stretch of layer to shear, and a flatter slope supplies both.

By the workbench's rules this is a dam you could build, and the real panel's verdict points the same way: had the downstream slope been flattened as the original design proposed, it found, the failure would have been avoided. Hold on to the passing number all the same, and ask which strength it used.

What the number can't see

Most people would stop here, with a passing number. The workbench gave the layer a drained strength, the grip σ′ tan φ′ of a soil whose water keeps pace with the load and leaves as the grains are squeezed. Mount Polley's designers did the same for the clays they knew of under the dam: silts and clays laid down on the bed of a lake fed by melting glaciers, which engineers call glaciolacustrine. After a field program in 2011, their checks gave these soils a peak friction angle of 28°, the same angle the workbench gives its clay foundation. The layer that failed, a higher one, was not in the checks of the side that failed at all., p. 69 (the 28° peak angle after the 2011 field program) and p. 76 (only the lower of two such units was in the Perimeter Embankment's stability analysis). The workbench's 19° stands in for the layer's weakness. Neither is the strength the layer had on the night it failed.

The dam had pressed that clay harder than anything in its past. A clay in that state is loose for its load, like the loose sand of , and when it is sheared it tries to contract. Over the years of raising, the load came on slowly enough for its water to seep away, and the panel found the loading had been essentially drained. A slide is fast. In water took 11 years to cross a single metre of clay; sheared in minutes, the clay's water could not leave; its pressure rose, the press between the grains fell, and the grip fell with it. That grip is the clay's undrained strength, and gave its rough size for soft clays. The panel's laboratory tests on samples of the layer measured it directly. On average:, pp. 76 and 135 (normally consolidated, so contractive, under the dam), p. 101 (a loading path essentially drained), table 5.3.1, p. 49 (peak undrained strength ratio, average 0.24 over all tests), p. 98 (after strain weakening, perhaps 0.13).

Notice that the gap grows with the press. The higher the dam, the more strength the drained line promises and the more of it vanishes if the clay is sheared fast. The clay was also strain-softening: in the panel's tests its grip peaked and then fell as it kept moving, so its peak strength held only until it began to move. A slide that starts on such a clay weakens the ground it slides on, which is how a dam that had stood for years could go in a night.Why check drained strength at all? Because it is the right check for loads that last: over years a clay's water does leave, and a slope must stand on the strength it has then. The undrained check is a separate calculation, run with the clay's measured strength ratio and its stress history. The two answer different questions, and a design needs both wherever a clay can be sheared fast.

Your workbench passed the dam at 3:1, and with a flatter slope the real dam would probably have stood. But the number that approved it was computed for a strength the layer did not have when it mattered. The panel was blunt about where the fault lay: "the dominant contribution to the failure resides in the design." Its foundation investigations had not found a continuous layer near the breach, nor recognized that it could fail undrained., p. iv and p. 105 (the quotation), p. iv (2H:1V would have avoided the failure).

Check yourself The real layer lay 8 to 10 m under a dam about 40 m high; the workbench's lies 4 to 6 m under 24 m. Is the workbench's at the same share of the height?

Nearly. 8 ÷ 40 = 0.20 and 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25; 4 ÷ 24 = 0.17 and 6 ÷ 24 = 0.25. Scaled to its height, the workbench's layer starts a little higher.

A layer has to be found first

A calculation can only weigh the ground it is told about. Finding out what that ground is, layer by layer and how strong each is, is the work of site investigation: mapping the geology, drilling boreholes and testing what comes up. A borehole samples a column of ground about a hand's width across. Under the side of Mount Polley that failed, the panel found the critical soil at depths of about 8 m, and only four locations where holes went deeper than 8 m and the soil was tested. None was where the breach occurred., pp. 28 and 80. The Chief Inspector counted three deep sampled drillholes along the 2 km of that embankment: , p. 2. The layer was there, a few metres thick, and the drilling passed it by.The epigraph is the title of a paper Karl Terzaghi published in 1929, in a collection on geology and dams issued by the American Institute of Mining and Metallurgical Engineers (). Eighty-five years later, a layer a few metres thick under a 40 m dam was such a detail. After the failure, British Columbia's engineering association wrote guidelines for investigating dam foundations, in answer to one of the panel's recommendations., preface: written in response to the panel's Recommendation 6, and applying to all dams in the province.

Then there was the slope on top. The design had called for a downstream slope of 2H:1V, but for years the outer face had been left at the steepest angle tipped rockfill will stand, about 1.3H:1V. (You measured about 1.4 across for 1 up in , from the crest's edge to the toe; the face itself stood at 1.3H:1V.) The panel put the two causes in one sentence: "If constructing unknowingly on the Upper GLU stratum, and not recognizing the potential undrained failure constituted loading the gun, building with a 1.3H:1V angle of repose slope over this stratum pulled the trigger." Here the Upper GLU is the panel's name for the layer. That night the crest dropped at least 3.3 m, more than the 2.3 m by which it stood above the pond. The pond spilled over the lowered crest and cut the breach., p. 75 (an unbuttressed 1.3H:1V slope, almost 40 m high), p. 108 (the quotation), p. 137 (the crest's drop and the pond 2.3 m below the core). The Chief Inspector of Mines listed among the causes a "misplaced faith in the Factor of Safety"., p. 5.

Notice how little of the drawing the layer takes up, and how much of the slide runs along it. Everything above the layer is the dam the checks were written for; the layer is the detail they were never shown.

What the check leaves out

The workbench is honest about its own blind spots, and so should you be. Material strengths and permeabilities are typical values, not design values. The sandbox does not check earthquakes, the liquefaction of loose saturated sand (), or whether each zone is a proper filter for the one beside it. In a real dam, clay painted straight against rockfill would wash into it, which is why real cores have filters.

Four more limits matter to anyone who would trust its numbers. It checks no clay for its undrained strength, as Mount Polley shows. It skips the end-of-construction case, when a clay core or foundation is still carrying the weight of fresh fill in its water, for which designers want 1.3., table 3-1, p. 3-2. Its exit gradients right at the toe depend on the 1 m grid, so the piping safety there is a guide. And it shows one cross-section: a real dam is a kilometre or more of sections, each on its own ground, and the weakest may be the one nobody drew.

What to remember

  • A design is a plan for the water: a core and a cutoff to hold it back, a drain to let it out before it reaches the downstream slope, a free-draining shell where the reservoir may fall away. Each keeps water from taking the grains' press where a slip would run.
  • Every fix costs something, in fill, clay or water. A drain buys safety cheapest because it removes water instead of adding weight, and pays for it in leak.
  • A factor of safety is only as good as the strength fed to it. A drained check assumes the water can leave; a clay sheared fast keeps its water, and Mount Polley's gave less than half its drained grip.
  • No calculation weighs ground nobody sampled. The layer had to be found before any strength could be measured.

Questions

  1. The workbench passes the Mount Polley section at 3:1, with a factor of safety of 1.56. Give two reasons the real dam could still have failed at that number.

    Hint

    Ask which strength the check used, and what the check was told about the ground.

    First, the check uses a drained strength, and the real layer failed undrained: the panel measured an average undrained strength ratio of 0.24 against the drained tan 28° the designers gave such clays (, pp. 49 and 69) = 0.532, so the fast grip was 0.45 of the slow one, and it fell further once the clay moved (strain softening). Second, the check knows only the ground it is given; at Mount Polley the layer had not been found where the breach occurred, so no check, drained or undrained, included it. A third answer is also fair: the workbench's layer angle is a stand-in, and the real layer's properties had never been measured before the failure.

  2. Meet Hold the water with the least clay you can. What sets the floor in the workbench, and what would set it in a real dam?

    Hint

    Clay passes water a hundred thousand times more slowly than rockfill, so in the model even a thin wall works if it has no gap. Then think about the gradient across a thin wall.

    In the workbench the floor is continuity: a clay wall 2 m wide from the cutoff to above the water, 46 m³ per metre, leaks 9.8 L/day and passes every goal, with a factor of safety of 1.81; any gap, and the water finds it. In a real dam the floor is set by what the model leaves out. The whole head is lost across the wall, a gradient of about 20.4 ÷ 2 = 10.2, which would drive the core's fines into the rockfill without filters on both sides (); a thin core also cracks more easily as the shells settle round it. The panel noted that Mount Polley's core had been cut from 8 m to 5 m wide, making it more vulnerable to cracking (, p. 75).

  3. The workbench lets you paint clay straight against rockfill. What would really happen, and which chapter said so?

    Hint

    Compare the size of the clay's particles with the gaps between rocks, and think about water crossing the boundary.

    Water leaving the clay would carry its fine particles into the large gaps of the rockfill, and the core would erode from its downstream side, internal erosion of the kind describes. Real cores sit between filters and transitions graded so that each zone holds back the one beside it (). At Mount Polley the filter and transition were each about 1 m wide, and the panel found many samples of the filter too coarse; it did not find that piping or cracking caused the breach (, pp. ii and 75).

  4. In Empty it fast, start from Leaky (all rockfill) and test. Why does its upstream slope score so much better than the dam of fill, and why does it still not complete the mission?

    Hint

    The workbench lets anything that passes water at 10⁻⁵ m/s or faster drain as the reservoir falls.

    It scores 1.41 against 0.94. Rockfill drains as fast as the reservoir falls, so no trapped water pushes the slope out after the reservoir's support has gone, and its grains keep their press; fill and clay keep their water. But the dam leaks 29,621 L/day per metre against the mission's cap of 1,000. (On sand and gravel the same dam scores 1.81 upstream; the mission fixes the foundation as clay.)

  5. Raise the reservoir of the dam of fill to 95% of its height. Where does the one-third rule say water will break out on the downstream slope? Then test it.

    Hint

    95% of 24 m, then a third of that.

    The reservoir is 22.8 m deep, so the rule gives 22.8 ÷ 3 = 7.6 m. The workbench says 11 m: the rule is a rough guide, and here it errs low.

  6. You may drill four more holes at Mount Polley before the dam is built. Where do you put them, how deep do they go, and what do you do with what comes up?

    Hint

    Where would a weak layer hurt most? How deep can a slip under a 40 m dam reach? What test tells you a clay's fast strength?

    Put them under the planned dam where it will be highest and its slopes steepest, in a line across the footprint from the upstream toe to beyond the downstream toe, because that is where a slip would run along a layer; and space them by the geology, closer where the glacial deposits change from place to place. Take them deeper than any slip the dam could cause, through all the glacial soils to firm ground. Recover undisturbed samples of every fine-grained layer and test them for their stress history and their undrained strength, not only their friction angle. Then run the stability check with that undrained strength as well as the drained one. Four holes cannot cover kilometres of dam; their job is to tell you where the next forty should go.
