This chapter watches a tailings dam grow, and asks why the direction it grows in matters so much.

After this chapter you can

  • sketch the three ways to raise a tailings dam, and work out the fill each one needs and where its crest ends up;
  • explain why an upstream raise can fail with no earthquake, years after the last raise was built;
  • estimate how fast a tailings deposit rises, and name the levers that keep it safe (rate of rise, a wide beach, a pond kept away from the crest) and the alternatives to a wet deposit.

On 30 September 2020 Brazil changed its national law on dam safety. Most laws about building say what a structure must achieve: how strong, how safe, how often inspected. This one did something rarer. It named a way of building and forbade it: no mining dam in Brazil may be built or raised by the upstream method, a montante in Portuguese. Then, so that nobody could argue about what the words meant, the next paragraph defined the method, in translation: a way of building a dam "in which the containment dykes rest on the tailings themselves, or on sediment previously discharged and deposited.", Art. 3, adding Art. 2-A to Law 12.334 of 2010. §1 reads: "Entende-se por alteamento a montante a metodologia construtiva de barragem em que os diques de contenção se apoiam sobre o próprio rejeito ou sedimento previamente lançado e depositado." The translation is ours.

The law gave every mine with such a dam until 25 February 2022 to take it out of service as a dam.The Portuguese word is descaracterização, "decharacterization": draining, reshaping or removing the deposit until it no longer works, or fails, as a dam. The next paragraph lets the regulator extend the deadline where the work cannot technically be finished in time. It was not the first word on the subject. Less than a month after the collapse at Brumadinho in January 2019, Brazil's mining agency had already banned the method by resolution, with the same definition.The deadline: , Art. 2-A §2. The resolution: ANM Resolution 4 of February 2019, as summarized by . Chile had turned against it half a century earlier.

A lawmaker does not usually care whether a wall steps one way or the other as it grows. So why would a country write the word "upstream" into its laws? The answer is in what the wall stands on, and to see it we have to watch one grow.

A dam that grows with the mine

A water dam is finished the day it is built. A tailings dam never is while the mine runs. The mill sends out more ground rock and water every day, so the dam has to rise with the deposit behind it. It begins as a starter dam of soil and rock dug from pits nearby, and the tailings are piped out over its crest as a slurry. As showed, the slurry sorts itself on the way: the coarse sand settles near the pipes and builds a gently sloping beach, while the fine grains and the water run on to a pond. When the deposit nears the top, the dam is raised, and then raised again. Each raise can step toward the pond, away from it, or straight up, and that direction names the method:

  • Upstream: the crest steps toward the pond. Each raise is a small dyke built on the tailings beach, often from the beach's own sand.
  • Downstream: the crest steps away from the pond. Each raise is built over the previous outer slope, on compacted fill and natural ground.
  • Centreline: the crest rises straight up. Each raise straddles the top of the beach and the outer slope.

Check. Raising a downstream dam by a little adds a strip of fill as wide as the whole base, because the outer face moves out from top to bottom. Halfway up the last raise, at 62 m, the base is 8 + 4 × 62 = 256 m wide, and 256 × 12 = 3,072 m³, the same as step 6.

What it means. Fill means trucks, time and money, and a downstream dam pays for each new metre across its whole width while an upstream dam pays for one small dyke. That is the pull toward upstream, and it grows as the dam does.

What it means. Of the three, only the upstream dam has to trust the tailings to carry it. Whether it holds is a question about the beach, and no amount of care with the dykes can answer it.

What the wall stands on

Most people say a dam fails when its wall breaks, so the way to make it safe is to build the wall strong. For an upstream dam that answer looks in the wrong place. Each dyke is compacted fill, small and sound. Under it lies the beach, and the beach was never built at all. It was laid down from a slurry, grain by grain, the way a wave lays down the loose sand at the water's edge in , and nobody rolled it afterward. Near its surface, in the sun and wind, it drains and can look as firm as the damp sand of a castle. A few metres down it sits below the water table of the deposit, the phreatic surface, and there every gap is full of water.

How high that water stands under the dykes is not fixed. It depends on where the pond is. Here is one upstream dam drawn twice, with the pond kept far from the crest and with the pond close to it:

Notice that the only difference between the two panels is the water. The dykes, the beach and the tailings are the same. In B the dykes stand on loose grains with water in every gap, and as showed, that is a material whose strength depends on what happens to that water when something pushes on it.

Something always does. Each new dyke adds its weight to the beach beneath it. The weight of the sloping dam presses the tailings under it outward, toward its open face. Nobody needs to shake anything. So the question to settle is what loose, soaked grains do under a push that is slow and steady:

A new dyke is placed, slowly, on a beach of loose tailings with water in every gap. The water can only leave slowly. What happens to the grains under the dyke?

The water takes over. Pushed, loose grains try to shuffle into the gaps between them and pack tighter. To do that, the water in those gaps must get out of the way, and here it can't leave fast enough. So the grains cannot move closer, and the weight they were carrying passes to the water. The water pressure rises, the grains press on one another less, and their grip goes with the press, as showed in the sandbox. Nothing has to break, and nothing has to shake.

A slow push is enough

Put the box back into the dam. Below the phreatic surface, the beach under an upstream raise is loose sand and silt with water in every gap; it is contractive, so a push makes it try to pack tighter. Whether the water can get out of the way in time depends on how far it has to go and how easily it moves, and put a clock on that: the time grows with the square of the distance (). A sample in a box drains in seconds. A deposit tens of metres thick, laced with fine silt from the pond, does not drain in the seconds or minutes it takes a slope to start moving. For that moment the tailings are undrained, the water takes the load, and the grains stop gripping. The deposit flows as a heavy liquid, and the dykes on it go with it. That is static liquefaction: liquefaction with no earthquake at all.

The trigger can be small, and it can come late. Dam I at the Córrego do Feijão mine near Brumadinho was raised upstream in ten raises over 37 years, to 86 m. Its last raise was built in 2013, and tailings stopped arriving in July 2016. On 25 January 2019, with no sign of trouble on any of its instruments, its slope collapsed in under ten seconds, and about three quarters of its tailings flowed out within five minutes. The expert panel found flow liquefaction of the tailings inside the dam itself. They were loose, saturated and brittle, and the water level in them had stayed high near the toe after deposition stopped. What tipped them was a combination of slow creep and the loss of suction in the drier tailings above the water table after heavy rain at the end of 2018., Executive summary, pp. i–ii, and p. 16 (raises, height, dates). The toll is in . The growing had stopped. The loose, soaked tailings were still there.

An earthquake is a faster push of the same kind, and Chile, where the ground shakes often, learned it first. After the El Cobre tailings dams failed in an earthquake in 1965 and buried the village below, Chile wrote its first rules for tailings dams, in 1970. They covered dams with people living within reach downstream; among other things they kept the pond as far from the sand wall as possible and had the wall's sand raised outward, over the crest, and they have been read ever since as shutting out the upstream method for those dams. Chile's rules of 2007 say it in one line, which translates as "the use of the upstream method is prohibited."El Cobre in 1965: . The rules: , Arts. 30, 31 and 44; , Art. 14(h). Stava, in Italy, failed with no earthquake at all.On 19 July 1985 the upper of two tailings dams above the village of Stava, in the Italian Alps, burst. Its mud poured into the lower basin and on down the valley, about 180,000 m³ of it, and 268 people died. The upper dam was still being raised, by the upstream method. Luino and De Graff put first among the causes the mistaken assumption that the tailings would consolidate fairly quickly.

That answers the question this chapter opened with. A country writes "upstream" into its law because no rule about the wall can make an upstream dam safe. The danger is in what the wall stands on: loose, saturated tailings that a slow push can turn to liquid, and that stay that way after the raising stops. So the law names the one thing it can name, the place the wall is allowed to stand.

Check yourself A mine proposes to build its upstream dykes of crushed rock instead of beach sand, so that the dykes themselves are strong. Does that make the dam safe?

No. Stronger dykes still stand on the same loose, soaked beach. If the tailings under them liquefy, rock dykes go down with them as surely as sand ones.

How fast it may rise

Upstream dams are still built where the law allows them; most South African tailings dams, for one, are raised this way. The ones that stand are kept standing by keeping the tailings under them drained. A long beach keeps the pond, and with it the high water, away from the crest, as in panel A of . The pond itself is kept small: its water is drawn off to a separate dam as soon as it can be, not stored against the tailings., §4.8, p. 18. The third lever is time: the rate of rise, how many metres a year the surface of the deposit climbs.

Time matters because every metre of new tailings is a load on the tailings below. Below the phreatic surface that load is carried at first by the water, and it passes to the grains only as the water seeps away and the grains settle closer, the slow process called consolidation. Rise slowly and each metre has drained and settled before the next arrives. Rise fast and the water pressure builds up from one season to the next, the tailings stay loose, and the phreatic surface climbs with them.

So the deposit runs a race between two clocks. One is the time it takes to add a metre. The other is the time the water below takes to get out, and by that grows with the square of the distance the water must travel, so it grows as the deposit thickens; the fine, silty tailings laid down toward the pond are the slowest of all. Nobody can work out the second clock exactly for a real deposit, layered as it is, so the industry limits the first one instead. South African guidance puts the limit for most upstream dams at about 2 to 2½ m a year, up to about 6 m a year for dams whose outer wall is built of coarse, free-draining sand, and says plainly that the figure is a judgement based on experience., §4.6, p. 15. At 2 m a year, each metre has half a year to drain before the next one covers it.

The rate of rise itself is plain arithmetic: the volume the mill sends out in a year, spread over the area of the deposit,

r = M ⁄ (ρd S)

r rate of rise, m a year · M dry tailings sent out, tonnes a year · ρd tonnes of solids in each cubic metre of settled deposit · S area of the deposit's surface, m²

Check. The order of magnitude: 13 million m³ is a block 1 km by 1 km by 13 m, and spread over two such squares it stands 6½ m deep.

What it means. The rate of rise is set by the mine's output and the size of the valley, not by the dam engineer. A mine that grows its output, or a deposit that narrows as it climbs, can outrun its own tailings. The engineer's choices are a bigger footprint, a slower mine, or a dam that does not stand on the tailings at all.

Raising away from the water

The other two methods do not ask the tailings to carry the dam. A downstream raise stands on compacted fill and on the ground beyond the old toe. Because each raise covers the whole outer face, a sloping core and drains can be carried up with every lift, as in a water dam, and the downstream panel of draws them. Such a dam can hold back a pond, and it rides out earthquakes best. Its costs are the ones counted, and land: its toe marches out 4 m for every metre of rise, 192 m over the four raises of the figure, and that ground has to be set aside from the day the mine opens.

A centreline raise splits the difference. Its crest stays put, so pipelines and roads along it never move, and a vertical drain can rise with every lift. A small wedge of each raise rests on the top of the beach, 9% of the fill in the figure, so that part of the beach must be sandy, drained and compacted. It can be built of cyclone sand: the slurry is spun in a hydrocyclone, which flings the coarse grains to its wall and out at the bottom while the fines and most of the water leave at the top., §4.4.4, p. 12. The coarse sand drains freely and can be compacted, which is what a wall needs.

Check yourself Tailings behind a downstream dam rise 6 m a year. Is that rate a danger to the dam?

Far less than it would be to an upstream dam. The limit exists because each upstream raise stands on the last few years of tailings. A downstream raise stands on compacted fill and the ground beyond the toe, and the tailings behind it only lean on it, as a heavy liquid would; that is a load it is designed to carry. South African guidance says the rate of rise is not relevant behind a fully engineered earthfill or rockfill wall (). The wall must still be raised fast enough to stay ahead of the tailings.

Between centreline and upstream lies the modified centreline raise. Each raise steps its crest a little toward the pond, so more of it rests on the beach than in a centreline raise, and the core leans slightly upstream, supported on that side by a zone of tailings. Mount Polley, in British Columbia, was designed to be raised this way., pp. 8 and 53. The panel adds that as built, one early raise was entirely upstream construction and later ones crept upstream (pp. 55 and 69). Hold that thought; it comes back in .

Less water in the deposit

Every method so far stores the tailings wet and builds a wall to hold them. The other road is to take the water out. Thickened tailings leave the mill with less water and stand on steeper beaches than a thin slurry does (). Filtered tailings, sometimes called dry stack, go further: filters take out enough water that the tailings can be carried by truck or conveyor and spread and compacted like earthworks. The review panel that investigated the Mount Polley breach set out the case for them from first principles of soil mechanics: take away the surface water, keep the tailings unsaturated with drainage, and "achieve dilatant conditions throughout the tailings deposit by compaction"., §9.3.1–9.3.2, pp. 121–123, which also gives cost as the main reason filtered tailings are not used more widely. Each of those is a way of keeping the grains pressing on one another: no pond to soak them, no water in the gaps to take the load, and grains packed dense enough to swell, not collapse, when they are sheared. Even then the compaction is not optional; a loose, dry stack that later gets wet can flow too. The catch is cost: filtering is more expensive than piping a slurry, and most filtered operations so far have been small.

What to remember

  • A tailings dam grows with the mine, by upstream, centreline or downstream raises. To the same height, upstream needs the least fill (2,496 m³ per metre in , against 9,792 downstream) because it builds each raise on the beach.
  • An upstream raise stands on loose tailings with water in every gap. Pushed, even slowly, the grains try to pack tighter, the water cannot leave in time and takes the load, and the grains stop gripping. No earthquake is needed, and the danger lasts after the raising stops.
  • A slow rate of rise gives the water time to leave the tailings; a long beach and a small pond keep it away from the dykes. How fast the deposit rises is set by the mine's output and the area the deposit covers.
  • Downstream and centreline raises stand on dense, drained fill, so the grains under the dam keep pressing; filtered, compacted tailings take the water out of the deposit itself.
  • What decides the danger is the state of the fill, loose and wet or dense and drained, not what it holds back: Coquitlam is a water dam that needed the same cure.

  3. Why could the dam at Brumadinho fail with no earthquake, more than five years after its last raise?

    Hint

    Ask what the end of raising changed, and what it did not change, about the tailings inside the dam and the water in them.

    Ending the raising stopped adding load, but it did not make the tailings dense or dry. They stayed loose and saturated, the water level near the toe stayed high, and they were brittle, so a very small strain could set off a large loss of strength. Slow creep, and the loss of suction in the drier tailings above the water table after heavy rain, supplied that strain. With the water unable to leave in the time the slope took to move, the tailings were undrained, the water took the load, and the dam liquefied from within ().

    Hint

    How was it built, and what state does that leave the fill in?

    Because it was built by hydraulic fill, which leaves soil loose and saturated, the same state as the beach under an upstream raise. The cure was the same too: a dense, drained, compacted dam against its toe that does not depend on the old fill.
