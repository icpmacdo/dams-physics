So far. A soil grips only as hard as its grains press on one another, and water pressure in the gaps takes part of that press away: σ′ = σ − u. A lake standing on top changes nothing, because it adds to the weight and to the water pressure alike. All of that assumed the water could come and go as it pleased.

After this chapter you can

  • put a number on the grip of damp sand, and say why a sand wall stands a quarter of a metre and never thirty;
  • predict from how sand is packed whether shearing makes it swell or shrink, and so explain the pale footprint, the sinking foot and liquefaction;
  • estimate how long water takes to leave a layer of sand or clay, and so tell a drained loading from an undrained one.

Go back to the beach at low tide and walk down to the firm sand in the middle, where the sea was an hour ago. Put your weight on one foot. A pale ring spreads round it, as if the sand had dried where you stood, and fades in a second or two. Lift the foot and the print turns dark and shiny for a moment, wetter than the sand around it.

Now step down to the edge, where each wave lays a fresh sheet of sand. Stand still and it holds you. Wiggle your feet and it goes soft and swallows them to the ankle.

Both sands are saturated: every gap between the grains is full of water. showed that water pressure in the gaps takes part of the grains' press away, and that still water standing on top changes nothing. By that account two wet sands pressed by the same foot ought to behave alike. They behave as opposites. Most people, asked what a foot does to wet sand, say it squeezes the water out, so the sand round the foot should look wetter. On the firm beach it looks drier.

left a clue. When its heap of wet sand was jiggled, the densely packed heap held and the loosely packed one flowed, so packing matters. Time matters too. Every sum in let the water flow in or out as fast as it liked, and under a moving foot it can't, not quickly enough. This chapter is about what water does when it is trapped. It starts with the simplest case, the damp castle, where the water pulls.

Water that pulls

The damp castle has only a little water in it, and that water gathers in tiny rings where the grains touch (the close-up in draws them). The surface of each ring is curved and stretched, like the skin of a soap bubble, and it pulls the two grains toward each other. So the water in a ring is at less than the pressure of the air around it: a negative pore pressure, which engineers call suction.

Put a negative u into and the effective stress goes up: σ − u is more than σ when u is below zero. The grains press together harder than their own weight could make them, and by more press means more grip. That extra grip does not depend on how much sand lies above, so it can hold grains on a vertical face, where weight alone would roll them off.

How much grip? Nobody can put a pressure gauge inside a ring of water a fraction of a millimetre across, but the grip can be measured whole. Shear damp sand in a box with nothing pressing on it and it still resists, with about 0.6 kPa for a sand of fine, angular grains.: sand of angular grains 0.1–0.4 mm across, sheared in a box; its cohesion rose with the water content and levelled off at about 600 Pa once the water was about 3% of the weight of the grains. Grip that is there with no press at all is called cohesion, written c. In damp sand it is borrowed from the water, so engineers call it apparent cohesion. It is the grip a suction of 0.6 ÷ tan 34° = 0.89 kPa would give if it pressed on every grain, the weight of about 5 cm of damp sand. Whether that matters depends on what it is set against.

A wall of soil with cohesion fails when a wedge of it slides off along a sloping crack: the block on the ramp of , with a little glue on the ramp. Check. The answer falls a little short of the 0.3 m castle, and the gap is fair: 0.6 kPa was measured on one sand, prepared one way. Finer sand has more contacts in each square metre, each with its own ring, so it grips harder, and a round tower is braced by its own curve. What the sum settles is the size: tens of centimetres, never tens of metres. And with no cohesion at all, dry sand, H = 0: no vertical face, only the 34° heap.

What it means. At the foot of the castle the weight already presses with 5.4 kPa, six times the 0.89 kPa the suction adds, so suction matters most near the top, where the grains carry almost no weight. At the base of a dam the grains press with hundreds of kilopascals. Designers leave suction out, as said, and a week of rain or drought would take it away in any case.

Dry the sand and the rings evaporate; flood it and they merge into one body of water at ordinary positive pressure. Either way the suction goes and the wall slumps.Treating suction as a negative u slightly overstates it. The rings wet only part of each grain, so only part of the suction presses the grains together. Alan Bishop wrote the effective stress of a damp soil with a factor χ between 0 (dry) and 1 (saturated) in front of the suction (). The measured cohesion used here already includes that factor. Suction needs air in the gaps. But the firm sand under your foot has no air in its gaps. It is saturated, and it stiffens anyway.

Loose and dense

The answer lies in how the grains are packed. Push on a heap of grains so that they must slide past one another, and what happens next depends on how they sat. Densely packed grains sit in each other's hollows. To slide, each must ride up over the one in front, so the heap swells as it shears. Loosely packed grains have hollows below them, and shearing drops them in, so the heap shrinks. Engineers call the first kind dilative and the second contractive.Osborne Reynolds named the swelling dilatancy in 1885. He filled a rubber bag with lead shot and water and sealed it, and the bag went rigid in whatever shape it had: to change shape the shot had to spread apart, and there was no more water to fill the room. Connected to a measuring vessel, the bag drew water in as he squeezed it out of shape. He explained the pale footprint in the same paper (, p. 475).

Here are both, in close-up, with every gap full of water:

Notice that in both drawings the water is the wrong amount for the new arrangement. The dense stack has grown more gap than it has water to fill. More water can only flow in through the narrow gaps from the sand around, which takes time. Until it arrives, the water already there is stretched to fill the larger space, and its pressure falls below what still water at that depth would have. Engineers call the difference from the still-water pressure the excess pore pressure, Δu; here it is negative. A negative Δu works like suction: by the grains press harder, and the sand under your foot stiffens.

The water the sheared sand pulls in has to come from somewhere, and the nearest supply is the water among the top grains. So the water surface is drawn down below the top grains and leaves them looking dry: that is the pale ring. Reynolds saw both supplies, the surface and the sand around, and saw why the surface wins at first: drawing water through the sand "requires time to accomplish"., p. 475 (p. 209 of the 1901 reprint). A second or two later the water from below arrives, the surface rises, and the ring darkens again.

Keep the words apart. Suction is the pull in damp sand, where air fills most of each gap. Negative excess pore pressure is the pull in saturated sand that is trying to swell faster than water can follow. Both make the grains press harder.

Check yourself You lift your foot out of the pale ring and the print turns dark and shiny for a moment. Why?

With the foot gone, the swollen sand settles back into its dense arrangement and shrinks. It now holds more water than its gaps need, and the extra is pushed up to the surface until it drains away.

The loose sand at the water's edge does the opposite. Each wiggle of your foot shears it, and its grains start to drop into the hollows below them. For the pack to shrink, water has to leave it, and water leaves sand only through the same narrow gaps. So the pack can't shrink yet. The water, squeezed between grains that are trying to close on it, rises in pressure: a positive Δu. It now carries part of the weight the grains were carrying, so they press on one another less, and grip less, and your foot sinks. Stand still for a few seconds and the water seeps away upward, the grains settle and press again, and the sand holds you once more.

A house on the same sand is a much bigger foot, and an earthquake shakes it for longer than you can wiggle. Make a prediction before reading on:

A house stands on loose sand that is full of water. An earthquake shakes the ground for a few seconds, too fast for the water to drain away. What happens to the house?

It sinks and tilts. Each shake lets loose grains drop toward the gaps below them; the water in those gaps can't escape in time, so its pressure rises and it takes the weight the grains were carrying. Grains that hardly press can hardly grip. Experiment 3 in the grain sandbox, , lets you watch it.

The question turns on its last words: too fast for the water to drain away. How fast is that?

A matter of time

Whether the water keeps up is a race between two clocks: how long the load takes to come on, and how long the water needs to move. If the water wins, its pressure stays at the still-water value while the grains rearrange, and engineers call the loading drained. If the load wins, the water is trapped, the soil can't change its volume, and the water pressure changes instead; that is undrained. The same sand can be either; what decides is how fast.

The water's clock can be worked out from things you already have. Picture a saturated layer L thick, with free-draining gravel at its top, and a load put on it suddenly. At first the water carries the whole of the new load as an excess pressure Δu. To hand it on to the grains, the layer must squeeze out enough water to let it shorten, and how far it shortens depends on its stiffness against squeezing, M, in kPa: a press of Δu shortens it by the fraction Δu/M of its thickness. That is a volume of water Δu·L/M under each square metre. Darcy's law, , says how fast it leaves: the excess is a head of Δu/γw spread over a path of length L, so each square metre passes k·Δu/(γw·L) cubic metres a second. Divide the volume by the rate. Δu cancels, and what is left is a time:Karl Terzaghi published the theory of how excess water pressure drains out of a layer of clay in 1923 (), two years before the book credits with effective stress. The rough sum here gives the form of his answer; his full solution adds a factor close to 1 for most of the pressure to drain.

t ≈ L² ⁄ cv
cv = k · M ⁄ γw

t time for most of the excess pressure to drain (s) · L the longest path the water must travel to a free-draining face (m) · cv the coefficient of consolidation (m²/s) · k permeability (m/s) · M the soil's stiffness against squeezing (kPa) · γw = 9.81 kN/m³

Notice the square. A layer twice as thick has twice the water to lose along a path twice as long, so it takes four times as long. And notice the k. Sand lets water through a hundred thousand times more easily than clay, so for the same stiffness its water leaves a hundred thousand times sooner. The slow squeezing out of water under a new load is called consolidation.

What it means. Sand is drained under anything slower than a few minutes and undrained under anything quicker: a footstep, an earthquake. Clay is undrained under almost everything except the slow work of months and years.

The same. Water in the middle of the 4 m layer has 2 m to go either way, so L is 2 m and t = 2² ÷ 0.08807 = 45.4 days. Drained through one face only, it would take four times as long.

The sandbox as a laboratory

The two clocks are hard to watch in real sand: the grains are small and the water is hidden. The sandbox below shows both. Every grain in it is simulated as a disc that pushes, rubs and rolls on its neighbours, and the water in the gaps is a pressure that rises when the pack is squeezed and falls when it swells. To shear the grains is to push the top sideways so that they slide past one another. The Drain switch sets the race: Open lets water flow in and out of the box freely, which is drained; Shut traps it, which is undrained.

Notice two things the sandbox does that the rest of this chapter has only described. In Experiment 2 the dense sample's strength rises to a peak and then falls as it keeps swelling: once its grains have climbed out of each other's hollows, the pack is no longer dense, and it grips like loose sand. And in Experiment 4 the extra water pressure falls and then stops falling, near −100 kPa*. Water can be pulled only so hard before it boils into bubbles of vapour, which is called cavitation, and the model stops it there.Why about −100? Pressures in this book are gauge pressures, counted from the air's 101 kPa (). Lower the water's pressure to near zero absolute, which is about −100 kPa gauge (strictly, to its vapour pressure, a little above zero absolute), and dissolved air and vapour come out of it as bubbles. So a dense sand sheared with its water trapped can gain only so much strength. Laboratory tests meet the same ceiling, which is why they are run with the water under raised pressure, and the sandbox copies it.

Liquefaction

Push Experiment 3 to its end and the loose sand's trapped water carries nearly all the load. The grains still touch, but they hardly press, the effective stress falls toward zero, and the grip goes with it. The sand flows like a heavy liquid. That is liquefaction, and your wiggling feet do a small version of it at the water's edge. It needs three things at once: loose grains, every gap full of water, and a load that comes faster than the water can leave. An earthquake supplies the speed, but any load that comes on fast enough will do, even a small slip that sets the grains moving. Liquefaction with no earthquake is called static liquefaction, and meets it.

Water dams are not immune. Lower San Fernando Dam, north of Los Angeles, was begun in 1912 largely as hydraulic fill: soil sluiced into place with water, which settles loose and stays saturated. Early on 9 February 1971 an earthquake of magnitude 6.6 shook it hard for about 15 seconds. Within about 12 of them, the water pressure in the sandy fill near the base of the upstream slope had climbed so high that much of that fill had liquefied, and most of the upstream slope slid into the reservoir. What was left of the embankment stood about 1.5 m above the water, and that was with the water kept, since 1966, at least 2.9 m below its old maximum level. Some 80,000 people below the dam were evacuated for four days while the reservoir was pumped down, and nobody was killed at the dam.The slide and its cause: . The date, magnitude, about 15 s of strong shaking with high water pressures after about 12 s, the 5 ft (1.5 m) of freeboard left, the 80,000 evacuated for four days and the 9.6 ft (2.9 m) cut in the maximum operating level in 1966: .

Dense sand behaves differently. Once its grains start to slide they must climb over one another, the pack must swell, its water is pulled thin, and the grains press harder. That is why the densely packed heap of held when it was jiggled. Engineers who want an embankment that will not liquefy compact its fill densely, layer by layer, as describes.In 1936 Arthur Casagrande sheared loose and dense samples of sand side by side and found that both ended at the same density, which he called the critical void ratio: sand looser than that shrinks as it shears, and sand denser swells (). Two years later the upstream slope of Fort Peck Dam in Montana, a hydraulic fill dam, slid during construction and killed eight men. The board that investigated blamed weak shale and bentonite under the dam, and thought liquefaction of the fill may have carried the slide further ().

This is why mine waste worries engineers so much. The ground-up rock a mine pumps out as slurry, called tailings, often settles loose and stays saturated for decades, and follow what that means for a dam that holds it back, or stands on it.

Clay, the slow soil

Everything so far was sand. Clay, the soil of the cores that made watertight, follows the same rule, σ′ = σ − u, on a far slower clock. Its flakes are so small that its water needs about a decade to cross a metre of it (11 years in ), and by a 2 m layer takes weeks to drain and a 10 m layer years. So almost any load on clay starts undrained.

Clay also grips less than sand. Its flat flakes slide on one another more easily than rounded quartz grains climb over each other: the workbench of gives its clay a friction angle of 26°, against 34° for the beach sand. Loaded slowly, so that its water keeps pace, a clay grips as says, with σ′ · tan φ′.

Loaded fast, it is another matter, and the reason is the loose sand's. A soft clay that has never carried more load than it carries now is loosely packed for its load, and when it is sheared it tries to contract. Its water can't leave in time, so the water pressure rises, σ′ falls, and the grip falls with it. The strength a clay shows when it is sheared too fast for its water to move is its undrained strength, su. Engineers who worked back from failures of soft clay under embankments, footings and excavations found the average strength the clay had actually supplied along the slip, and Gholamreza Mesri showed in 1989 that laboratory tests give the same answer:: su = 0.22 times the largest effective stress the clay has ever carried, which for a clay never loaded more than now is its present σ′; first from field failures, then confirmed from laboratory tests.

What it means. The same clay, in the same place, has two strengths, and which one counts depends on how fast it is asked for.

Clay, loaded fast, can be weaker than the same clay loaded slowly. Of everything in this chapter, that is the sentence the last chapter of the book needs most.

Undrained isn't always weaker. A clay that was once pressed harder than it is now, by ice or by soil since worn away, is densely packed for its load, and behaves like dense sand: sheared fast, it tries to swell, its water pressure falls, and for a while it is stronger. The danger then comes later, as water seeps in and the extra strength seeps away. And any clay that has slid a long way along one surface loses more still, because its flakes line up along the slip; the grip left is its residual strength.

Check yourself A dam is raised over a 2 m layer of the example's clay, with sand beneath it, by one metre every month. Is each raise drained or undrained for the clay?

Each raise goes on in days, far quicker than the 45 days the layer needs, so each starts undrained. But a month is not much shorter than 45 days, so most of each raise's water pressure has seeped away before the next. Over the life of the dam the clay is largely drained; for any one fast event, such as a slip, it is undrained.

Where Part I comes back

Soil holds as well as its grains press on one another, and water in the gaps, pushing or pulling, changes that press. Every later chapter leans on that pair:

  • · A lake can't lose strength. Tailings can. A water dam holds a liquid; a tailings dam holds a loose, saturated deposit that can liquefy.
  • · A concrete dam is the block on the cushion. Water under its base carries part of its weight, and the friction that stops it sliding goes with it.
  • · An upstream raise stands on tailings. If the tailings under it liquefy, the raise has nothing left to stand on.
  • · The core takes the head so the downstream shell keeps its grip. Low water pressure behind the core means a high effective stress and a strong downstream slope. The upstream shell stays soaked, so it relies on draining freely.
  • · Drains put the press back. Lowering the water inside a dam lowers the pore pressure, so the grains press harder.
  • · Sand boils: water flowing up can lift the grains. When its push on them equals their weight under water, the effective stress is zero and the sand boils.
  • · A slope's water can't always leave as fast as the reservoir does. Let a reservoir down quickly and the upstream shell's water pressure lags behind, with nothing outside to balance it.
  • · Your design is judged on this. The stability check weighs the friction the grains can supply against what's needed, using the water pressures from the seepage calculation.

What to remember

  • Water can pull as well as push. In damp sand its rings press the grains together, but only with a fraction of a kilopascal: enough for a wall a quarter of a metre tall, never one of thirty.
  • Sheared, densely packed grains must swell and loosely packed grains must shrink. Where the water can't follow, the dense sand's water is pulled thin and its grains press harder; the loose sand's water takes their weight and they press less.
  • Whether the water follows is a race between the load and the drainage time, t ≈ L²/cv: seconds to minutes for sand, months to years for clay. Drained if the water wins, undrained if the load does.
  • Liquefaction is the loose case taken to its end: loose, saturated and loaded faster than it can drain, the grains stop pressing on one another and the soil flows.
  • Clay, loaded fast, can be weaker than the same clay loaded slowly.

Questions

  1. Explain, in the terms of this chapter, why the firm sand goes pale under one foot while the sand at the water's edge swallows the other.

    Hint

    Which sand is dense and which loose? What must each do when it is sheared, and what does the water do meanwhile?

    The firm sand is dense. Sheared by the foot it must swell, and the water it needs is drawn in from the surface, which sinks below the top grains and leaves them dry for a moment; meanwhile the stretched water pulls the grains together and the sand stiffens. The sand at the edge is loose. Wiggled, its grains try to drop into the gaps, the trapped water's pressure rises and takes their weight, they press less and grip less, and the foot sinks.

A reservoir of water has no strength to lose: it is a liquid already. Some dams hold back loose, saturated, ground-up rock that does have strength, and can lose it in seconds. What changes when the thing behind the dam can lose its own strength?
