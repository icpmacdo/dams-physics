The voids are the channels of flow for percolating water.

R. R. Proctor, Fundamental principles of soil compaction, 1933

Between 1964 and 1967, in the canyon of the Peace River in northern British Columbia, crews built a hill one layer at a time. Each layer of its middle was spread no more than 10 inches thick (6 inches from late August 1965), scratched up on top so the next would bond to it, and rolled four times by a 90-ton compactor on rubber tyres. Each layer was meant to come out at least 98 per cent as dense as the same soil packed in a standard laboratory test. When they stopped, the W.A.C. Bennett Dam stood 183 m high and ran 2.0 km across the canyon and the terraces on either side, about 44 million cubic metres of earth and rock., pp. 1–3: 600 ft high, 6,700 ft long, 57.5 million cubic yards of fill, built 1964 to 1967; the rules for placing the core are in its table of zones, p. 3.Bennett is far from the tallest dam of its kind. Shuangjiangkou, in China, a rockfill dam built round a core of gravelly soil, is 315 m high. It began storing water in May 2025 and connected its first generator to the grid in June 2026 (; ). It is reported as the world's tallest dam, a title it is expected to hold until Rogun, in Tajikistan, reaches its planned 335 m, which was projected for 2033.

From outside, a dam like this is one long slope of earth. Cut it across and it is seven materials. The panel of engineers that reviewed Bennett in 2012 lists them as zones, from the middle outward. Zone 1 is the core, there to hold the reservoir back. Zones 2 and 3, a transition and a filter, are each described as a filter to the core. Zone 4 is a drain. Zone 5 is a bedding layer for the riprap, the armour of large rocks on the upstream face. Zone 6, the random shell, "provides the weight for the stability of the dam". Each went in by its own rules: the core in the thinnest layers at a water content held close to one chosen value, the transition and filter in thicker layers under a vibrating roller, the shell with coarser streaks allowed because they help water out., the table of zones, pp. 3–4, quoted for Zone 6.

Keep an eye on Zone 2, the transition. It sits between the core and everything coarser, and of all the zones its job is the hardest to see in a drawing. is where it earns its keep.

Why go to that trouble? The canyon had plenty of soil. Why not build the whole hill out of the tightest soil in it?

Where it stood in the silt dam. Through any one material the head falls steadily from the lake side to the far side, however tight the material is: that is Darcy's pipe from with nothing but clay in it. Making the whole dam a hundred times tighter cuts the leak a hundredfold and makes the water take far longer to arrive. How tight the soil is sets how much water gets through; where the water ends up standing is set by the dam's shape and the lake's level.

The film puts its answer in one line: a dam does not have to be watertight; it has to keep the water pressure out of the part that holds it up. Every zone in this chapter serves that idea. showed that soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and that water in the gaps takes part of that press away. The part of an embankment that holds it up against the lake is the downstream half. So the first question about any design is where the water will stand inside it, and only the second is how much of it will get through.

Four jobs

A core alone does not make a dam, because the water it lets through has to go somewhere and the core has to be held in place. Every embankment with a core hands out the same four jobs to different soils.

Hold the water back. That is the core's job: compacted clay, or glacial till, the unsorted mix of clay, silt, sand and stones that a glacier leaves behind. Bennett's core is glacial soil of that kind, a non-plastic mix of silt, sand and gravel., p. 31 ("non-plastic silt sand gravel", and a glacial soil tested as similar to it) and p. 48 (the moraine materials used in the core). Where the ground beneath is gravel or cracked rock, the barrier has to go down into it too. A cutoff trench carries the core down through loose river deposits to rock, and under the trench a grout curtain, lines of holes drilled into the rock and pumped full of cement grout, closes the rock's cracks.Rock is tested for grouting by pumping water into a section of a drill hole. One Lugeon is an inflow of one litre a minute into each metre of hole at a pressure of 1,000 kPa, equivalent to a rock permeability of about 1.3 × 10⁻⁷ m/s (, p. 5).

Let the water out. Whatever gets through the core must leave the dam without building up pressure in the part that holds it up. Drains of clean sand and gravel do that: a chimney drain standing against the downstream side of the core, a blanket along the base, and a toe drain that carries the water out to where it can be measured.

Stop the soil. But water leaving fine grains for coarse ones carries the fine grains with it, so a drain laid straight against a core would slowly wash the core away. Filters and transitions sit between them and step the grain size up gradually, each zone fine enough that the grains of the zone inside it cannot wash into its gaps, and coarse enough to let the water through. This is the thinnest zone in the dam, and its job is the subtlest; is about it.

Add weight. The shells of rockfill or sand and gravel are most of the dam. They hold the core in place against the push of the reservoir and hold themselves up by friction, which is to say by the press of their grains on one another. Over the upstream shell goes riprap, against waves and ice; along the top, the crest carries a road and stands high enough above the highest flood to leave some freeboard.

Read a cored dam from the middle outward and you meet the jobs in the same order every time: tight in the centre, coarser and freer-draining toward the faces. Each zone is coarser than the one inside it, so the water can get out, and fine enough to hold that zone's grains in. Engineers number the zones in that order too. Zone 1 is the barrier, Zone 2 the filters and transitions under or beside it, Zone 3 the main rockfill, though each dam adapts the scheme: Bennett's run from 1 to 6, core outward.The convention, as Cooke and Sherard set it out and Cruz quotes it: , p. 57.

Water leaving the core would carry its fine grains into the big gaps between the rocks, and the core would wash away from the inside. The filter and the transition step the grain size up so that no zone can wash into the next.

Two shells, one soaked

The two shells look alike in a drawing, and they lead different lives. The downstream shell is the part the core protects. Behind our dam's core the water stood 0.65 m deep, so above that the gaps in the downstream shell hold air, the water pressure in them is zero, and the grains press on one another with their whole weight.

The upstream shell gets no such protection. It sits under the reservoir, and every gap in it is full of water at whatever pressure the lake's depth puts there. One could assume that a soaked shell must be a weak one, since water in the gaps takes part of the grains' press away. It does take part of it, and the grains grip one another less for that; but as long as the water is still, the shell is no less safe. Buoyancy lightens each grain's press on the grains below it, and it lightens the pull that tries to slide the grains down the slope by exactly the same share. showed the result for a heap of sand: under still water it stands as steeply as it does dry. Check. The two faces are the same rockfill at different slopes, so their factors should differ only by the ratio of the slopes: 1.98 × 1.9 ÷ 2.2 = 1.71. And at a 1:1 face, 45°, the dry rockfill would have FS = tan 42° = 0.90: no heap of this rockfill stands that steep.

What it means. A soaked shell is safe while its water can come and go with the lake. Rockfill, at 10⁻¹ to 10⁻³ m/s, drains as fast as any reservoir can be lowered; a shell of fine fill would not, and its face would fall toward the trapped case. These are long, straight slopes; real slips are curved and run deeper, and checks them on our dam.

That trapped case has a name. Lowering a reservoir faster than its water can leave the upstream shell is rapid drawdown: the water that pressed on the face from outside is gone, the water inside is still there, and it pushes outward through the slope. It is the one event that turns a soaked shell from safe to dangerous. It is why designers prize an upstream shell that drains freely, and one reason the earth–rockfill section's upstream face is the flatter of the two.

Where the rule breaks

"Tight in the middle, coarse outside" is the rule for a dam with a core. The other three tabs of break it, each for a reason.

The concrete-face rockfill dam has no core. Its barrier is a slab of reinforced concrete laid over the upstream face, joined to the rock around its edges by a concrete plinth, with the grout curtain drilled from the plinth. Behind the slab the whole dam is rockfill, rolled hard so that it settles little and the slab does not crack. Its finest zones are directly under the slab, not in the middle: a cushion of crushed rock and sand gives the slab a firm, smooth bed and limits the leak if a crack opens. Water that passes the slab drops through the rockfill to the base and runs out, and the rockfill needs no filter against the slab because it has no fine grains to lose. The leaks it can take are large. At two big dams of this kind in Brazil, Barra Grande and Campos Novos, leaks of 1,000 to 3,000 litres a second were measured with no sign of trouble in the downstream slope., pp. 60–61, reviewing .

The concrete gravity dam has zones only in a looser sense: a richer, frost-resistant concrete on its faces, a lean mass concrete inside so that the heat of setting stays low, and drains and a gallery near the upstream face. None of them is there to stop water carrying grains away, because concrete has no loose grains. Its danger, as showed, is the water underneath it.

The tailings dam is built of its own waste. In the centreline dam on the last tab, sand split from the tailings by cyclones is spread and compacted on the downstream slope, a chimney drain keeps that sand from filling with water, and the finest waste settles out by the pond, on the upstream side, as far from the slope as it can be ( showed how such a dam rises). The materials are the mine's, and the aim is the cored dam's: keep the water away from the part that holds the dam up.

So "tight in the middle" is one way of doing something every dam on the four tabs does. Each decides where the water will stand, and keeps it out of the part that carries the load.

Check yourself A crack opens in a concrete-face rockfill dam's slab and the leak through the dam rises tenfold. Why would its engineers worry less than about a crack in a clay core?

The water that passes the slab falls into rockfill with no fine grains to lose, and drains out at the base without raising the water pressure in the dam. Water through a crack in a core can carry the core itself away, which is why a core needs filters and a slab does not.

How a heap becomes fill

So far every zone has been described by what it is made of. How it is put in place matters as much. Soil dug from a borrow pit and tipped into a heap is loose, the loose sand of : wide gaps, ready to settle when it is wetted or shaken, and inclined to shrink when it is sheared, handing its load to the water in its gaps. A dam of loose soil would settle unevenly and crack, and a soaked shell of it could lose its grip altogether. So every zone goes in as thin layers, each squeezed dense by rollers before the next is spread. That squeezing is compaction, and it is why Bennett's core went in no more than 10 inches at a time.

How dense can a soil be made? In 1933 R. R. Proctor, a field engineer with the water department of Los Angeles, published a way to find out.Proctor's first article () rested on laboratory tests of more than 200 soils, checked in the field on four dams his bureau had built in the two years before. He explained the dry side much as this chapter does below: in a very dry soil, films of water where the grains touch draw them firmly together, and a little more water releases that pull. Past that point he wrote of the water as having "an effect similar to that of lubrication". Pack a sample of the soil into a mould with a set number of blows from a standard hammer. Weigh it, dry it in an oven, and weigh it again. Then add a little water to the soil and repeat. A cubic metre of the packed sample weighs its bulk unit weight γ, grains and water together; its water content w is the weight of the water as a fraction of the weight of the grains. Take the water out and what is left is the weight of grain in each cubic metre, the dry unit weight:

γd = γ / (1 + w)

γd dry unit weight, the grains in each cubic metre (kN/m³) · γ bulk unit weight, grains and water (kN/m³) · w water content, weight of water ÷ weight of grains

The more grain packed into each cubic metre, the denser the fill. Plotted against the water content, Proctor's dry unit weights climb, reach a peak and fall again, here for an illustrative soil:

Notice that the curve has a single peak and falls away on both sides. On the dry side, the castle's grip from works against the roller: rings of water where the grains touch bind them into crumbs that resist being pushed into one another's gaps, and a little more water weakens the rings and lets the grains pack closer. On the wet side, water fills nearly every gap, and in the moment a roller takes to pass it cannot get out: the water carries the roller's press, as it carried the load in the undrained tests of , and the grains are pushed no closer. The peak is the optimum water content, and the peak's weight is the most this effort can achieve with this soil. A heavier roller, or more blows, gives a higher peak at a little less water.

A specification asks for a share of that peak. Its relative compaction is the dry unit weight reached on site divided by the laboratory maximum:

RC = γd / γd,max

RC relative compaction, usually given as a percentage · γd,max the peak of the laboratory curve for the same soil (kN/m³)

Bennett's core had to reach 98 per cent, at a water content between 2 per cent below the optimum and 1 per cent above it., the table of zones, p. 3 (the specification) and p. 9 (permeability against the water content at placing). The water content matters for more than density. The panel found that the same core soil, placed drier, with less of its gaps filled with water, let water through 60 to 140 times more easily once soaked than when it was placed wetter. The core's card in gives a second reason to keep a core near its optimum or a little wet of it: there the soil bends as the dam settles, where a drier, stiffer one would crack.

On site an inspector checks each layer: dig a small hole, weigh what comes out, measure the hole's volume (by filling it with sand of known density, for instance), and dry a sample for its water content. Those two numbers give γ and w, and with them the gaps. A cubic metre of solid grain would weigh Gs γw, where Gs, about 2.65 for quartz sand, is how many times denser the grain is than water (). So the volume that one unit of grain takes up, gaps included, is Gs γw / γd, and the gaps alone, the void ratio, are that less one:

e = Gs · γw / γd − 1

e void ratio, volume of gaps ÷ volume of grains · Gs specific gravity of the grains · γw unit weight of water, 9.81 kN/m³

What it means. Gaps that fill 0.29 of the volume are fewer than the third of the volume that allowed soaked soil. Sand packed this dense swells when it is sheared and draws water in (), so it holds. Compaction turns a loose heap that would hand its load to the water into fill that keeps it on the grains.

Check yourself A layer tests at 95% and well wet of the optimum. The contractor offers to roll it a few more times. Will that help much?

Not much. Wet of the optimum the gaps are nearly full of water, which cannot get out while a roller passes, so the water takes the extra press and the grains move little closer. Drying the soil toward the optimum, or digging the layer out, does more.

Each zone, then, is a soil chosen for one job, packed by rules written for that job, and set where its designers expected the water to be. The core expects the water to stop at it, the filter expects to meet whatever gets through, and the drain expects to carry it off.

What to remember

  • A dam need not be watertight. It has to keep the water pressure out of the part that holds it up, so that the grains there press on one another with nearly their whole weight. In our dam a clay core takes 96.8% of the head and the leak barely changes.
  • Four jobs, middle outward: hold the water back (core, cutoff, grout curtain), let it out (drains), stop the soil moving with it (filters and transitions), and add weight (shells). Each zone is coarser than the one inside it.
  • The downstream shell is kept nearly dry. The upstream shell is soaked and still strong, because still water lightens the grains' press and the pull on them alike, and rockfill drains as fast as the lake falls. Rapid drawdown of a shell that drains slowly is its danger.
  • "Tight in the middle" is the cored dam's way. A concrete-face rockfill dam puts its barrier on the face, a gravity dam is its own barrier, and a tailings dam builds itself from its waste, all to keep water away from what carries the load.
  • Compaction packs loose fill dense, so that it keeps its load on its grains. Each soil packs densest at its optimum water content, and relative compaction measures a layer against that laboratory best.

Questions

  1. Why can the upstream shell be soaked and still strong, and what one event makes it dangerous?

    Hint

    Compare what still water does to the press of a grain on the grains below it and to the pull that tries to slide it downhill. Then ask what happens if the water cannot leave.

    Under still water, buoyancy lightens the grains' press and the pull down the slope by the same share, so the face is as safe as when dry: 1.98 for the rockfill face of . Rockfill drains as fast as the lake falls, so its water stays still. The danger is rapid drawdown of a shell that drains slowly: the water left inside seeps out through the face, and the same face falls to about 1.10.

  2. A concrete-face rockfill dam has no core. Where is its barrier, and why does its main rockfill need no filter against the slab?

    Hint

    Ask what a filter is for, and whether the rockfill has anything a leak could carry away.

    The barrier is the concrete slab on the upstream face, sealed to the rock by the plinth, with a grout curtain drilled from the plinth into the rock below. Water through a crack in the slab enters rockfill that has no fine grains to lose and drains straight out at the base. A cushion of crushed rock and sand under the slab limits the leak, and a fine transition under the joint between slab and plinth catches silt washed into a gap there and seals it (the cards of ).

  3. Press Build on each tab of . Which dam puts its watertight element in only after the body of the dam is built, and what does that mean for inspecting it?

    Hint

    Watch the stage in which the barrier appears, and whether anything is built over it afterwards.

    The concrete-face rockfill dam: its slab goes on in the fourth of seven stages, once the rockfill is in place, and it stays on the face, where it can be seen and repaired whenever the reservoir is drawn down. An earth core rises with the shells, layer by layer, and is buried for good; its condition can be read only from instruments inside it and the water that seeps out of the dam.

  4. The earth–rockfill dam has filters on both sides of its core. Why does the downstream one matter more?

    Hint

    Which way does the water cross the core, most of the time?

    Seepage crosses the core from the reservoir side to the downstream side, so the grains it could carry leave through the core's downstream face; the downstream filter keeps them in, and doubles as the chimney drain. The upstream filter matters when the flow turns round, as it can after a fast drawdown, when water in the core flows back toward the lowered reservoir.

  5. An illustrative field test on a rolled layer gives a bulk unit weight of 21.0 kN/m³ at 14% water. The laboratory peak for the soil is 18.9 kN/m³. What is the relative compaction, and does the layer pass a 98% specification?

    Hint

    Take the water out first with , then divide by the peak.

    γd = 21.0 ÷ 1.14 = 18.421 kN/m³, and 18.421 ÷ 18.9 = 0.975, a relative compaction of 97.5%. It fails 98%, narrowly; it would pass a 95% specification.

  6. Bennett's core had to reach 98% of the laboratory peak and be placed between 2% below and 1% above its optimum water content (, p. 3). Why specify the water content as well as the density?

    Hint

    Two layers with the same density can still differ in how they behave once the reservoir soaks them.

    Because density alone does not decide how a core behaves when soaked. Bennett's panel found that core soil placed drier let water through 60 to 140 times more easily, once soaked, than the same soil placed wetter, and a dry, stiff core cracks more readily as the dam settles. Proctor showed the same thing in 1933: one soil packed to the same density at 6.2% and at 22% water was very hard in the first case and like soft putty in the second, and could become like the second once it soaked up water (). Too wet, on the other hand, and the soil cannot be packed dense at all, which is the wet side of .
