The Panel considers that too much was expected of the grout curtain, and that the design should have provided measures to render the inevitable leakage harmless.

So far. Every dam leaks, and followed the water through ours: where it gets in, which way it goes, where it comes out, and how drains and cutoffs decide all three. At the toe of our dam on sand and gravel, the water left the ground with a gradient of 0.79. This chapter asks what that water does to the grains it leaves behind.

After this chapter you can

  • explain a sand boil as the block of floating, and judge an exit gradient against the margin engineers want;
  • name the four ways water carries soil out of a dam, place a real failure in one of them, and say why a pipe, once started, speeds up;
  • size a sand filter for a sand, and say why a clay core needs a different rule.

On the morning of Saturday, 5 June 1976, the reservoir behind Teton Dam in eastern Idaho was nearly full for the first time. The dam was new: an earth embankment 93 m high (305 ft above the riverbed) across a canyon of hard volcanic rock, finished the November before and filling since October. Two days earlier, springs of clear water had appeared in the rock of the canyon wall, about 400 m downstream. The engineer in charge took them for a good sign: the ground around the reservoir was filling up as it should.: the dam, p. v and p. 1-1; the springs of 3 June, 1,300 and 1,500 ft downstream, p. 12-1, and the project engineer's testimony, p. 2-1.

Shortly after seven o'clock, the first people to arrive for work found muddy water running out of the rubble at the foot of the right abutment, where the dam met the canyon wall, at 20 to 30 cubic feet a second (0.57 to 0.85 m³ a second), and a trickle of cloudy water higher up the same junction. Both grew all morning. At about half past ten, with a loud burst, a large new leak broke out of the downstream face, from a tunnel about 1.8 m across, and the water began cutting a gully back up the face. At eleven a whirlpool opened in the reservoir, above the upstream slope. At 11:55 the crest began to fall in between the whirlpool and the head of the gully, and the reservoir went through. Eleven people died in the flood, by the count of the Bureau of Reclamation, which built the dam; the panel that investigated the failure associated fourteen deaths with it, directly or indirectly.The morning: , p. 12-1, pieced together by the panel from sworn testimony and photographs (pp. 2-1 to 2-15). Eleven deaths: , p. 1. Fourteen: , p. v and p. 1-1.

Teton leaked through its core. So, twenty years later, did the W.A.C. Bennett Dam on the Peace River in British Columbia, the dam whose zones opened . In June 1996 a hole opened in its crest, and the investigation that followed, reviewed by a panel of experts in 2012, found that seepage had carried part of its core away. Bennett is still holding back its reservoir. In both dams water found a way through the core and picked up soil on the way. The difference lay in where it could take that soil, and that difference is what this chapter is about.

Sand that floats

Start at the beach. met the soft sand at the water's edge as loose sand that a wiggle shakes into soup. As the tide goes out there is a second reason for it. The water held in the beach seeps back out at the foot of the face, and where it flows upward through the sand it takes part of the grains' press away. Push it up harder and it takes all of it.

put numbers on that. Water that loses i metres of head for every metre it travels drags on the grains in each cubic metre with i × 9.81 kN, in the direction it flows. A cubic metre of soaked sand presses down on the grains below it with its buoyant weight, about one water's worth. Turn the flow upward, raise the gradient, and the drag eats into that press until, at the critical gradient, the two cancel. The grains still touch, but they no longer press on one another, the effective stress is zero, and the sand has no grip at all. It is the block on the ramp of with so much water under it that it floats. For a typical quartz sand, grains 2.65 times as heavy as water with 0.65 m³ of gap for every cubic metre of grain, worked it out from the grains: (2.65 − 1) ÷ (1 + 0.65) = 1.00.

That is easy to see in a tube. Fill it with a metre of that sand, feed it water from below out of a tank, and let the water spill out of a spout at the top of the sand. Then raise the tank:

Notice that nothing about the sand changes from one tube to the next. The grains, the gaps and the water in them are the same; only the tank moves. In A the water stands still and the grains carry their whole weight under water. In B the water climbs through the sand, losing half a metre of head over its metre, and its drag takes half the grains' press. In C the drag matches the grains' weight, and the top of the bed lifts and churns. That is a sand boil, and it is what an engineer fears to find beyond the toe of a dam, where water that has passed under it rises out of the ground.

Check yourself A looser sand of the same grains has 0.8 m³ of gap for each cubic metre of grain. Does it boil at a lower or a higher gradient?

Lower. Its critical gradient is (2.65 − 1) ÷ (1 + 0.8) = 0.92: more of each cubic metre is water, so the grains weigh less under it and take less drag to float.

The gradient where seepage leaves the ground is the exit gradient, and engineers judge it against the critical gradient by a ratio, their margin against heave:

FSheave = ic / iexit

ic critical gradient, about 1 for sand · iexit the upward gradient where seepage leaves the ground

A slope in was judged at a factor of safety of 1.5. For an exit, the Bureau of Reclamation's design standard asks for at least 3 at an unfiltered exit of an existing dam, and 4 for a new dam or a repair, or about 2 to 2.5 where the soil and the water pressures are well measured., section 8.2.2.2, pp. 8-5 to 8-7, which also gives the warning about internal erosion quoted in the next paragraph. The margin is high because the number it divides by is shaky. Right at the toe the gradient has no settled value: the closer you look, the steeper it gets, so a computed exit gradient depends on how finely the ground was divided.Water flowing under a dam's base turns a sharp corner to come up beside the toe, and in the mathematics of a flow net the gradient at such a corner grows without limit. A grid of cells smooths the corner over one cell, so its answer depends on the cell's size. The seepage lab reads its exit gradient over the last half metre of ground. Natural ground is never as even as a model of it, and a lens of fine sand can sit where the model has gravel. And in fine sand under a roof, grains can be carried along at far gentler gradients than the one that floats them, as the next section shows.

The standard also says what the ratio does not measure: it flags sand boils, not every kind of internal erosion. Its own words are that it "is not a factor of safety against the initiation of internal erosion". A dam can boil without failing, and fail with no boil at all, as Teton did. A pipe that feeds itself

A boil is a warning, and grains are only part of what it carries. Each grain that comes up with the water leaves a hole the size of a grain behind it. The question is what that hole does next. Make a guess before you read on:

Water rising beyond the toe of a dam on sand has started to carry grains out, and a small hollow has opened under the edge of the dam. As the hollow works its way back under the dam, does it get easier or harder for the water to carry more sand away?

Easier. The water in an open hollow flows almost freely and loses almost none of its head there, so all the head between the reservoir and the toe has to be lost in the sand still ahead of the hollow. The longer the hollow, the shorter that stretch of sand, the steeper the gradient across it, and the harder the water pulls grains from its tip. The hollow becomes a pipe that feeds itself, and nothing in the sand slows it down.

To decide, count the head the water has to lose, and the distance it has to lose it over. Under the intact dam the water loses its whole head along the full path from the reservoir floor to the toe. What happens next depends on what holds the hollow open. The dam above it holds up its roof, so it stays open; water in it moves freely, and the hollow can grow back toward the reservoir, against the flow. Engineers call this backward erosion piping: it starts at the exit and travels upstream. The figure plays it from the first boil to the breach:

Notice which way the pipe grows. The water flows from the reservoir to the toe, and the pipe grows from the toe to the reservoir. It does its damage out of sight, under the dam, and the only sign above ground is at the exit: a boil, a cone of sand round a spring, water that runs cloudy. By the time the pipe reaches the reservoir, the water has a tunnel straight through, and the tunnel widens until its roof falls in. The arithmetic of a pipe that feeds itself is short:

A head budget for a growing pipe

Given
Our dam on sand and gravel: 20.4 m of head between the reservoir and the toe, spent along a path 102 m long under the dam's base. The lab's exit gradient at the toe, 0.786. An open pipe that loses no head along its length (illustrative: a real pipe loses a little).
Find
The average gradient on the path the water has left to cross, before the pipe and as it grows; and roughly what the water does at the pipe's tip.
The idea
The head is fixed by the reservoir. Whatever length of sand is left has to lose all of it (gradient = head lost ÷ distance, as in ).
  1. i = 20.4 ÷ 102 = 0.20the average gradient under the intact dam
  2. 0.786 ÷ 0.20 = 3.9how much steeper the water runs where its paths crowd together at the exit
  3. Pipe 50 m back: 20.4 ÷ 52 = 0.39the same head over the sand that is left
  4. At its tip: 0.39 × 3.9 = 1.5if the water crowds into the tip as it crowded into the toe: past the critical gradient
  5. Pipe 90 m back: 20.4 ÷ 12 = 1.7now even the average floats the sand

Halfway under the dam the average gradient has doubled, to 0.39, and the push at the pipe's tip is past the critical gradient. With 12 m of sand left, the average alone is 1.7.

Check. The average gradient reaches 1 when the sand left is as long as the head is high, 20.4 m: a pipe 81.6 m back, four fifths of the way under. Roughly halving the path roughly doubles the gradient, as step 3 found.

What it means. These numbers sketch a head budget and leave out how fast sand erodes, but the direction is certain: every grain that leaves makes it easier for the next one to leave. A pipe that has started will not stop by itself. The place to stop it is the exit, before it starts.

Four ways water carries soil

A boil at the toe is one way in. Teton showed nothing at its toe until its last morning; its trouble started deep inside, in the core. Engineers now sort the ways that erosion inside a dam can begin into four. The International Commission on Large Dams names them, and the panel that reviewed Bennett used the same four: concentrated leak, backward erosion, suffusion and contact erosion.; the four, with their definitions, in the terminology of , pp. 60–62. Here they are, each drawn on our dam at one scale:

Notice that only one of the four needs an exit at the ground surface. A concentrated leak (A) is water running through an opening that is already there: a crack, a gap beside a pipe through the dam, a loose layer, or open joints in the rock under a core. Its walls are scoured from the inside, and the opening widens. Backward erosion (B) is the pipe of the last section. Suffusion (C) happens in a soil whose grain sizes have a gap, coarse grains and fine ones with few in between: the coarse grains carry the load, the fine ones sit loose in their gaps, and seepage can wash the fine ones out and leave the skeleton standing. Contact erosion (D) happens where a fine soil lies against a coarse one and water runs along the boundary, as in a dam built on a layer of silt over open gravel.

A concentrated leak needs a crack, and a core is compacted precisely so that it has none. But a crack can open after the core is built. A core that settles more over a deep part of the valley than over a shallow one stretches, and the stretched part can crack. And water can open one by itself. Where something holds part of the core up, as the steep walls of a narrow trench can, the soil below is pressed together less than its depth would suggest. Teton's core was carried down into the rock of its abutments in such a trench, a key trench. If the water pressure in the soil rises above the least of those presses, the water can split the soil apart, the way a wedge splits wood. That is hydraulic fracture, and the panels that investigated both Teton and Bennett found it a likely way their cores first opened.

All four end the same way if nothing stops them: soil carried out of the dam, and a channel that grows. The statistics count them together, under an older name. Engineers often call every kind of internal erosion piping, though strictly the word means the backward erosion of the last section. Foster, Fell and Spannagle's 2000 study of 124 embankment-dam failures up to 1986 counted every kind as piping, through the embankment, through the foundation, or from one into the other., their table 3: failures to 1986, excluding failures during construction. Internal erosion of all kinds caused about as many failures as overtopping: 47% against 48% once spillway and gate failures are counted with overtopping. That is more than overtopping alone (34%). Some failures had more than one cause, so the shares add up to a little over 100%. Water pouring over the top of a dam is the other half of the story, the one told. The rest of this chapter is about the half that starts inside.Most clays resist erosion far better than sand, because their tiny flakes cling together: in laboratory tests a sand moves at an upward gradient of about 1, a clay often not until tens or hundreds (, p. 8-6). A few clays come apart into separate flakes in fresh water instead (, p. 78), and such a dispersive clay loses that defence. The pinhole test finds them: water is forced through a fine hole punched through a sample, and a dispersive clay turns it cloudy ().

Why a filter works

The first cure anyone reaches for is to stop the water. showed how hard that is under our dam, and Teton's designers tried it with a grout curtain, cement grout pumped into the joints of the rock under the core. Karl Terzaghi had proposed the opposite half a century earlier: let the water out, and keep the soil in.Teaching in Istanbul in the early 1920s, Terzaghi showed in simple laboratory tests that a layer of coarser sand with weight on top of it stops a fine sand from boiling, and patented the idea. He wrote about piping under dams and how to prevent it in 1922 (). The grain-size rules grew out of his consulting on small weirs in Europe; traces the story. A layer of the right sand where the water leaves the soil passes the water and holds the grains. A filter stops soil, not water.

How can anything let water through and not sand? The figure shows it at the scale of grains: on the left the grains of a core meet rockfill, on the right a filter of sand:

Notice that the filter does not need gaps smaller than every grain of the core. It needs gaps smaller than the core's coarsest grains. Those lodge in the filter's face, their own gaps are smaller still and catch the next size down, and so on: the soil builds its own filter against the real one, and the water goes on through.Three equal balls touching leave a gap through which a ball only 0.155 of their diameter can pass, about a sixth. A real filter has grains of many sizes, the small ones sit in the gaps of the large, and laboratory tests by Sherard and his colleagues found it acts like a sieve whose openings are about 0.11 times its 15% size (, pp. 275–276). A filter that does this is designed by grain size, and grain size is read off a curve.

Pour a soil through a stack of sieves, finest at the bottom, and weigh what each sieve holds. The grain-size curve plots, for each size, the share of the soil by weight that is finer. Engineers then name the soil by points on that curve: D15 is the size that 15% of the soil is finer than, D85 the size that 85% is finer than. D85 stands for the coarse grains that have to stop at the filter's face; D15, for the fine grains that decide how easily water passes. Here are a fine sand and a filter for it:

Notice that the filter's curve lies almost wholly to the right of the sand's: nearly all its grains are coarser than nearly all of the sand. That looks as if the sand could slip through, and it would, if the filter's openings were as wide as its grains. They are about a ninth of its D15. Terzaghi's two rules put the filter in its band:

D15F ≤ 4 · D85B and D15F ≥ 4 · D15B

D15F the filter's 15% size · D85B, D15B the base soil's 85% and 15% sizes (mm); the base is the soil the filter protects

The first rule keeps the soil in. A filter whose D15 is at most four times the sand's D85 has openings at most 0.11 × 4 = 0.44 of the sand's D85, so at least the coarsest 15% of the sand cannot get in, and the layer those grains build holds back the rest. The second rule lets the water out. Water passes a sand roughly in proportion to the square of its fine grains' size (its D15), so a filter whose fine grains are several times coarser passes water many times more easily, and the water leaves the soil without building up pressure in the filter. The agencies that design dams use these rules for sands and gravels, with 4 in the first and 3 to 5 in the second., table 5-2 (p. 84) for the retention rule by soil category; p. 84 for the permeability rule as the Bureau of Reclamation, the Corps of Engineers and the Natural Resources Conservation Service each write it; p. 88 for the square of D15.

Given
The sand of (illustrative): D15 = 0.20 mm, D85 = 0.45 mm. A graded filter's openings are about 0.11 of its D15.
Find
The range of D15 a filter may have; how its openings compare with the sand's coarse grains; how much more easily it passes water.
The idea
Apply both rules of , choose inside the band, then check the openings against the grains they must stop.
  1. Largest D15F: 4 × 0.45 = 1.8 mmkeep the soil in
  2. Smallest D15F: 4 × 0.20 = 0.8 mmlet the water out
  3. Choose D15F = 1.2 mminside the band, clear of both ends, since no pit delivers a curve exactly
  4. Openings: 0.11 × 1.2 = 0.13 mmthe smallest particle the filter stops
  5. 0.13 ÷ 0.45 = 0.29openings against the sand's coarse grains
  6. 1.2 ÷ 0.20 = 6the filter's fine grains against the sand's
  7. 6 × 6 = 36water passes a sand roughly as the square of its fine grains' size

Any filter with D15 between 0.8 and 1.8 mm will do. One at 1.2 mm has openings under a third of the sand's coarse grains and passes water about 36 times as easily as the sand.

Check. Even at the coarse end of the band the openings are 0.11 × 1.8 = 0.20 mm, under half the sand's 0.45 mm: the rule keeps a margin. And the band is 1.8 ÷ 0.8 = 2.25 times wide, which is the sand's own D85 over its D15: a sand with a wider spread of sizes leaves a wider choice, and a uniform sand a narrow one.

What it means. A filter works by grain size alone. Nothing in it is tight, and water passes it more easily than the soil it protects; it holds the grains because its coarse grains bridge the gaps and the soil's own coarse grains bridge the rest. Where water leaves a soil through such a layer, the grains at the exit keep pressing on one another.

Check yourself Would rockfill with a D15 of 30 mm hold the sand of ?

No. Its openings are about 0.11 × 30 = 3.3 mm, 7.3 times the sand's coarse grains, so the sand washes straight in: the left-hand panel of . A dam steps the size up through a sand filter and a coarser transition before the rockfill.

A clay core needs a different rule. Its D85 may be a few hundredths of a millimetre, and four times that would ask for a filter of very fine sand or silt, which would drain poorly. But clay does not wash out grain by grain from a sound core; it erodes from the walls of a crack, and what the filter must do is catch what comes out of the crack and seal it. James Sherard and Lorn Dunnigan tested filters in the laboratory against many core soils with a leak forced through them, in the 1980s, and found that ordinary filters of clean sand reliably stop erosion from concentrated leaks in fine silts and clays, while broadly graded glacial soils need a relatively fine one.; the rules as the agencies now write them in , table 5-2, p. 84, and the cake on the filter's face, pp. 275–276. The rules the agencies use today come from that work, sorted by how much of the core is finer than 0.075 mm: for a core more than 85% that fine, the filter's D15 may be up to nine times the core's D85; for one 40 to 85% that fine, it must be no more than 0.7 mm whatever the core. The eroded clay builds a thin cake on the face of such a filter, finer as it builds, and the crack stops leaking soil.

Two cores, two endings

Now Teton and Bennett can be set side by side. Here is Teton's core where it failed, on the right abutment, drawn to scale from the review panel's own section:

Notice the transition, the zone asked you to keep an eye on. It is many times wider than the filter and sits right against the core. The panel that reviewed Bennett in 2012 concluded that the settling of loosely packed core around the casing, as it got wet, could not by itself explain how far the first sinkhole had sunk: some of the core must have been carried away. That answers this chapter's question. Teton and Bennett both leaked through their cores, and in both the water carried core soil with it. At Teton the soil went into open joints in the rock, nothing stopped it, and in five hours the tunnel had taken the dam. At Bennett the soil went into a transition graded to hold it, and the leak sealed itself against the transition's grains. The water at Bennett went on through, as water in every dam does; the soil stopped, and the grains of the core went on pressing on one another.

What to remember

  • Water rising through sand drags its grains upward. At the critical gradient, about 1, the drag matches the grains' weight under water, they stop pressing on one another, and the sand boils. Designers want an unfiltered exit at a third or a quarter of that.
  • Grains washed out at an exit leave a hollow that works back toward the reservoir, and every metre it gains shortens the path and steepens the push at its tip: a pipe feeds itself.
  • Water starts carrying soil out of a dam in four ways: a concentrated leak through a crack, backward erosion from an exit, suffusion of fine grains through coarse ones, and contact erosion along a boundary between soils.
  • A filter stops soil, not water. Its 15% size is at most four times the protected soil's 85% size, so the coarsest grains lodge at its face and hold back the rest, and at least about four times the soil's 15% size, so water leaves freely. Clay cores need the finer, tested rules of Sherard and Dunnigan.
  • Teton's leak met open rock and Bennett's met a transition. In both the water went on through; only at Bennett did the soil stay, and the grains of the core keep pressing on one another.

Questions

  1. Once a pipe has started under a dam, why does it speed up instead of slowing down?

    Hint

    The reservoir fixes the head. Over what length of sand does the water have to lose it once the pipe is open?

    Water in the open pipe loses almost no head, so the whole head between the reservoir and the toe is spent on the sand still ahead of the pipe. As the pipe grows that stretch shortens and the gradient across it steepens: under our dam the average goes from 0.20 with no pipe to 0.39 with half the path eaten and 1.7 with 12 m left (). A steeper push carries grains away faster, which lengthens the pipe faster.

  2. A sand to be protected has D15 = 0.15 mm and D85 = 0.6 mm. Between what limits must its filter's D15 lie?

    Hint

    Use both rules of , with a factor of 4 in each.

    Retention: D15F ≤ 4 × 0.6 = 2.4 mm. Permeability: D15F ≥ 4 × 0.15 = 0.6 mm. So 0.6 mm ≤ D15F ≤ 2.4 mm; a filter near the middle of the band, about 1.2 mm, leaves room for the variation of a real pit.

  3. Which of the four ways of began the boil in , the failure of Teton, and the first sinkhole at Bennett?

    Hint

    Ask where each began: at an exit in the ground, or in an opening inside the dam?

    The boil is backward erosion: it begins at the exit beyond the toe and works back. Teton and Bennett were both concentrated leaks: water running through openings inside the core, at Teton along the base of the key trench and into open joints, at Bennett most likely through cracks opened by hydraulic fracture. At Bennett some of the transition was also prone to suffusion, which the panel thought let a little core erode before the transition held.

  4. Bennett and Teton both leaked through their cores into something coarser. Why did one survive?

    Hint

    Compare the gaps in what each core leaked into with the size of the core's grains.

    Teton's silt leaked into open joints in volcanic rock, gaps far larger than its grains, so nothing stopped the eroded silt and the tunnel grew to a breach in hours (). Bennett's core leaked into its transition, a soil graded to act as a filter for the core: the eroded grains lodged in it and the erosion stopped (). Both leaked water; only Teton let the soil go.

  5. In , switch the toe to Filter + weighting berm and play the sequence. What does the filter stop, and what does it let through? What is the berm's weight for?

    Hint

    Think of the tube of : what would a weight on top of the sand do to the press between its grains?

    It stops the sand and lets the water through: the sequence never gets past steady seepage. The berm's weight presses down on the ground at the exit, so the grains there press on one another harder, and the upward drag of the rising water has more press to overcome before the sand can heave. It is Terzaghi's first filter: sand with weight on top.
