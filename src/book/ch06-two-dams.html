A hydroelectric dam holds back water, and the water is the product. Its height above the turbines is stored energy, released on demand. A tailings dam holds back mine waste, called tailings: rock ground finer than flour, pumped out of the mill as a slurry with the water used to process it. Nobody wants it back. The job is to keep it in place, forever.

Both dams face the physics of . Water pressure grows by about 9.8 kPa for every metre of depth (p = ρgh), which comes to nearly 1,000 kPa, or 1 MPa, at the foot of a 100 m reservoir. And water seeps through anything that isn't perfectly tight.

What differs is almost everything else

Why tailings dams fail differently

Water has no strength to lose. A deposit of loose, saturated tailings has some, and can lose nearly all of it at once: it is the liquid beach from at the scale of a mine. Sheared, the loose grains try to pack tighter, the trapped water takes over their load, and the deposit flows like a heavy fluid. That is liquefaction. An earthquake can set it off. So can something much quieter, with no shaking at all (static liquefaction): a rising water table, slow creep, or a small movement of the slope.

  • 1985Stava, Italy. Two upstream-raised fluorite tailings dams collapsed one into the other; 268 people killed.
  • 1994Merriespruit, South Africa. An upstream-raised gold tailings dam overtopped after heavy rain and breached; the tailings flowed into a town, a flow one later study attributes to static liquefaction. 17 killed.
  • 2014Mount Polley, British Columbia, Canada. A tailings dam raised by the modified centreline method breached in the night, releasing about 25 million m³ of tailings and water. It was not a liquefaction of the tailings. Its cause is the subject of . No deaths.
  • 2015Fundão, Mariana, Brazil. An upstream-raised dam liquefied; 19 killed. Tailings travelled 663 km down the Gualaxo do Norte, Carmo and Doce rivers to the Atlantic.
  • 2019Brumadinho, Brazil. Dam I, upstream-raised, failed by static liquefaction in seconds; 270 killed. The disaster led to the GISTM and to Brazil banning upstream dams.
