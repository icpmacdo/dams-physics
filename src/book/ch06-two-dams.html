Part I built one idea from a beach, a bucket and a block on a ramp: soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and the water between them can take that press away, all at once when it cannot get out in time (). Part II puts the idea to work on real dams, starting with two that look alike in section and do opposite jobs.

At 12:28 in the afternoon on 25 January 2019, a video camera above an iron mine near Brumadinho, in the Brazilian state of Minas Gerais, was looking across the valley at the face of a dam. Dam I held about 12.7 million cubic metres of waste from the mine, the Córrego do Feijão, and nothing had been added to it for two and a half years. A drone had filmed it seven days before and seen nothing wrong. Its instruments had recorded nothing that mattered either: survey markers on the crest, tubes that measure movement deep inside it, radar watching the face, and gauges reading the water pressure within it., pp. i–ii (the time, the drone, the instruments, deposition ending July 2016), p. 5 (the camera) and p. 12 (the volume stored).

In the footage the crest sags first, along most of the dam's length. A fifth of a second later the face bulges outward, low down, just above the toe. Five seconds in, water spurts out near the toe. In under ten seconds the whole slope has collapsed, and what was a slope is a heavy liquid, moving. Within five minutes 9.7 million m³ of tailings, three quarters of everything the dam held, had poured out. The flow ran through the mine's canteen and offices, through houses and farms, and on down the valley to the Paraopeba River. It killed 270 people; two years later 11 of them had still not been found.The sequence, the volume and the flow's path: , pp. i, 3, 5–6. The toll, 259 deaths and 11 people missing: , p. 1.

When a water dam fails, water escapes. Here the dam itself went liquid. The ground-up rock behind its face had stood as a slope for decades, firm enough to walk on, and in seconds it had no grip left. A lake has no grip to lose; it is a liquid already. That difference is this chapter's question: what changes when the thing behind the dam can lose its own strength?

Two jobs

A hydroelectric dam holds back water, and the water is the product. Its height above the turbines is stored energy, released on demand. A tailings dam holds back mine waste, called tailings: rock ground to sand, silt and finer, pumped out of the mill as a slurry with the water used to process it. Nobody wants it back. The job is to keep it in place, forever.

There is a great deal of it. In April 2019, after Brumadinho, a group of investors asked the world's listed mining companies to describe their tailings facilities. The 107 companies that replied listed 1,743 of them, holding 44.5 billion m³; spread evenly over Manhattan, the authors worked out, that would stand 750 m deep. The same companies expected to add about 2.5 billion m³ a year., pp. 1–2.

Both kinds of dam face the physics of . Water pressure grows by 9.81 kPa for every metre of depth, which comes to nearly 1,000 kPa, or 1 MPa, at the foot of a 100 m reservoir, and water seeps through anything that isn't perfectly tight. Below, one of each is drawn in section, side by side:

Head drives the turbines, depth loads the dam

Start with the water dam, because its sums are short. A cubic metre of water weighs 9.81 kN. Let it fall H metres and gravity does 9.81 × H kilojoules of work on it, since a kilojoule is a kilonewton moved through a metre. The turbine catches as much of that as it can. Send Q cubic metres through every second and the power is the energy each cubic metre gives up, times the cubic metres per second, less what the machines lose:

P = η · ρ · g · Q · H

P power (W, watts; a megawatt, MW, is a million watts) · η efficiency, the share of the falling water's energy that the turbine and generator turn into electricity · ρ 1,000 kg/m³ · g 9.81 m/s² · Q flow through the turbines (m³/s) · H head, the drop from the reservoir surface to the tailwater, strictly the gross head (m)

The figure takes η = 0.90 for the turbine and generator together.H here is the gross head, surface to surface. Part of it is spent pushing the water through the intake and the penstock, so the turbine itself works on a little less, the net head. The figure uses the gross head, and folds the losses on the way into one efficiency. At its opening settings:

The power of a drop

Given
A turbine flow Q = 150 m³/s falling a head H = 84.8 m (the figure rounds it to 85); efficiency η = 0.90; water weighs 9.81 kN/m³. At the highest reservoir level the head is 92 m.
Find
The power, and the flow that would give 200 MW at the highest level.
The idea
Energy per cubic metre, times cubic metres per second, times the share the machines keep ().
  1. Each m³: 9.81 × 84.8 = 831.888 kJits weight times the drop; a kilonewton through a metre is a kilojoule
  2. Each second: 831.888 × 150 = 124,783 kW150 cubic metres every second; a kilojoule each second is a kilowatt
  3. Kept: 0.9 × 124,783 = 112,305 kW = 112.3 MWthe turbine and generator keep 90%; a megawatt is 1,000 kW
  4. At 92 m, per m³/s: 0.9 × 9.81 × 92 = 812.268 kWthe same three steps with one cubic metre a second
  5. Q = 200,000 ÷ 812.268 = 246.2 m³/s200 MW is 200,000 kW

The plant makes 112 MW, the figure's readout. With the reservoir at its highest, 200 MW takes 246 m³/s, inside the Turbine flow slider's range.

Check. Power is proportional to flow, so each cubic metre a second is worth 112,305 ÷ 150 = 748.7 kW at this head, and doubling the flow to 300 m³/s should double the power: the figure gives 224.6 MW.

What it means. A hydro plant sells head and flow in equal measure. The height of its water is what it earns from, and, as the next example shows, it is also what the dam has to hold.

The turbine in the figure turns at the same speed whatever the flow, and that is deliberate.Every generator on a grid has to keep step with the grid's alternating current, 60 cycles a second in North America, so it turns at one fixed speed; the rotors at Mica and Revelstoke turn at 112.5 revolutions a minute (). Open the gates wider and the water pushes the runner harder, so the generator delivers more power at the same speed. More flow means more push on the runner at the same speed.

One could assume that the H that drives the turbines is also the depth that loads the dam. But look at the left drawing again: H ends at the tailwater, and the dam's upstream face goes on below it, down to the heel on the rock. The water against the dam is deeper than the drop it makes past it. The figure's readouts use the depth, and a reader who checks them with H gets the wrong answer:

Depth, not head

Given
The figure at its opening settings: the water is 88.8 m deep at the heel and the head is 84.8 m. Water weighs 9.81 kN/m³.
Find

The idea
The load is the pressure triangle on the whole wet face (), and the face is wet all the way down to the heel.

What it means. The depth of water at the dam sets its load; the drop to the tailwater sets its power. The tailwater also pushes back on the downstream face, a push this figure leaves out; adds it, and the water underneath the dam.

A beach that sorts itself

The other half of the figure works differently. The mill sends its tailings out as slurry, and from the spigots along the crest it pours onto the deposit and runs downhill toward the pond. As the flow spreads and slows it drops what it carries, and it drops the biggest grains first. The coarse sand settles close to the spigots and builds the tailings beach, sloping gently away from the dam. The finest grains stay in suspension far longer and settle in the pond at the far end. A mine builds a beach, as the sea does, and sorts it the way a river sorts its bed. Given

Settling sorts the grains; Darcy's law then says how fast each kind drains. The crossing time is 's: gap fraction ÷ (k × gradient), here with a gradient of 1.
  1. Size: 0.1 ÷ 0.01 = 10the sand grain is ten times wider
  2. Sinking speed: 10 × 10 = 100speed grows with the square of the size
  3. Permeability: 10⁻⁵ ÷ 10⁻⁸ = 1,000how much more easily water passes the sand
  4. Sand: 0.4 ÷ 10⁻⁵ = 40,000 s = 11.1 ha metre of sand, at a gradient of 1

The sand sinks out of the slurry about 100 times faster than the slimes, so it settles by the dam, and it passes water 1,000 times as easily: water crosses a metre of it in about half a day, and a metre of slimes in more than a year.

Check. gave 46.3 days for silt with k = 10⁻⁷ m/s and the same gap fraction. A k ten times smaller should take ten times as long: 10 × 46.3 = 463 days.

What it means. The sorting does the dam a favour. It puts coarse sand that drains in hours next to the dam, where the deposit must be firm, and keeps the wet, slow slimes far away. The favour holds only while the pond stays at the far end of the beach.

Check yourself A mine lets its pond spread until the water lies against the crest. What now sits against the dam?

Water, and the slimes that settle out of it: the finest, wettest, slowest-draining tailings, where the dam most needs firm, drained ground. That is why tailings rules keep the pond far from the crest ().

What differs is almost everything else

The physics of the two dams is the same: pressure that grows with depth, and water that seeps from high head to low. Almost everything else about them differs:

Notice the two rows that decide most of what follows. A water dam is built once and then watched; a tailings dam is raised for as long as the mine runs and then has to stand indefinitely after it, earning nothing.The Global Industry Standard on Tailings Management was published in August 2020 by a review that the International Council on Mining and Metals, the United Nations Environment Programme and the Principles for Responsible Investment set up after Brumadinho (). It is the first standard meant to apply to tailings facilities anywhere, new or old. And when a water dam fails it lets out water, while a tailings dam lets out a deposit that may itself have turned to liquid.

Why tailings dams fail differently

Water has no strength to lose. A deposit of loose, saturated tailings has some, and can lose nearly all of it at once: it is the liquid beach from at the scale of a mine. Sheared, the loose grains try to pack tighter, the trapped water takes over their load, and the deposit flows like a heavy fluid. That is liquefaction. An earthquake can set it off. So can something much quieter, with no shaking at all (static liquefaction): a rising water table, slow creep, or a small movement of the slope. It does not lose quite all: a liquefied deposit keeps a small residual strength, and what is left of its grip decides how far it runs before it stops.

Every dam needs a door

Most people picture a dam failing because the water pushes it over or bursts through it. For embankment dams the commonest single way is simpler: the water goes over the top, which engineers call overtopping. The two totals are the source's own, and for the same reason they are not quite the sums of the rows above them. Statistics like these are counts of messy events sorted into boxes that overlap, and nobody should read the decimals as exact. Two things are plain all the same. About a third of these failures began with water over the crest, and about half did once the failed spillways and gates are added. Most of the other half is water inside the dam carrying its soil away, which takes up., failures in operation; the counts as reproduced in , which also gives the size of the record, 11,192 dams.

The sentence on this book's title page does not reach those overtopping failures. A flood pouring over an embankment does not need water in the gaps to take the grains' press away. It runs down the outer face fast, drags the grains off the surface and carries them away, and each grain it takes uncovers the next. A heap of earth can be cut through in a few hours; at Merriespruit an hour or two of water over the crest was enough. A concrete gravity dam can be built with its own crest as the spillway; an embankment cannot.Merriespruit: , p. 708. An overflow crest "may make up an entire gravity dam": , §9.8(c). So every dam needs a door, a way to let a flood past that is not over the crest.

The door is a spillway: a channel past the dam, usually at one end of it, with a crest, the sill the water pours over, set lower than the crest of the dam. In ordinary times the lake is kept at or below the spillway's crest. When a flood comes in faster than the turbines and outlets can pass it, the lake rises and begins to pour over the sill, and the higher it rises, the faster it pours. It goes on rising until the spillway passes water as fast as the flood brings it. The designer's job is to make sure that happens with the lake still below the dam's crest, by a margin called the freeboard. Here are both, to scale, for an illustrative embankment 30 m high:

Notice that the freeboard is measured from the flood level, not from the normal one. The 5 m between the lake's everyday level and the crest is not all margin: the flood spends 3 m of it just to make the spillway pass the water.

How much a spillway passes depends on how deep the lake stands over its crest. The sheet of water pouring over is a fixed share of that height, h, deep (about two thirds of it, over a broad crest), and it moves at a speed that grows with the square root of h, so the flow over each metre of crest grows as h × √h, which is h to the power 3/2:

Q = C · L · h3/2

Q flow over the spillway (m³/s) · L length of its crest, across the flow (m) · h height of the lake above the crest, measured upstream where the water is still level (m) · C a coefficient set by the shape of the crest (m½/s)

For a flat, broad crest, theory gives C = (2/3)3/2 × √g = 1.70 m½/s, which is the 3.087 that the Bureau of Reclamation's Design of Small Dams gives in feet. A rounded crest shaped to fit the falling water, over a deep approach, does better: the same book takes 4.0 in feet, 2.21 m½/s., §9.10, equation (3) (p. 365), §9.12(a) for 3.087 (p. 369) and §9.12(d) for 4.0 (p. 370). A coefficient in feet becomes one in metres on multiplying by √0.3048: 3.087 gives 1.70. The example takes a round value between the two:

What it means. The margin is counted in metres of water, and every metre of flood rise is a metre of freeboard spent. Because the flow grows faster than the rise, a bigger flood costs less extra height than you might fear; a flood the spillway was never designed for can still spend the rest.

Check yourself The flood turns out 10% bigger than the one the spillway was designed for. Does the lake rise 10% higher over the crest?

Less. With Q growing as h3/2, h grows as Q2/3: 1.12/3 = 1.066, so h goes from 3 m to 3.20 m, only 6.6% higher, and 1.80 m of freeboard is left.

Spillways can fail too, and when one does, the freeboard is tested in earnest.On 7 February 2017 the concrete chute of the main spillway at Oroville Dam, California, broke up while carrying a flood: water forced through cracks and joints lifted a section of the slab, and the weak rock beneath it washed out. With the main spillway damaged, the lake rose over the emergency spillway's weir for the first time in the dam's history, and the ground below the weir eroded so fast that about 188,000 people downstream were ordered to leave. The dam itself, an embankment 770 feet (235 m) high and the tallest in the United States, held (, pp. S-1, 7 and 26). Why water under a slab lifts it is a subject of . A tailings dam usually has no spillway at all. It stores the flood in its pond and pumps the water back to the mill, which works only if the pond has room. Suppose, for illustration, that a design flood brings 3 million m³ into a pond of 2 km²: the pond rises 3,000,000 ÷ 2,000,000 = 1.5 m. That 1.5 m, plus room for waves, has to be kept empty below the crest all year round, and kept while the dam is raised and the deposit climbs. Merriespruit's history records too little freeboard and often poor control of its pond., p. 707: "insufficient freeboard provision and often poor pool control". Water over the top of a tailings dam does what it does to any embankment, and then it uncovers what lies behind the wall: tailings that may be loose, saturated, and ready to flow.

What to remember

  • A water dam holds a liquid, which has no grip to lose. A tailings dam holds loose, saturated ground rock whose grains grip only while they press on one another, and the water in its gaps can take that press away in seconds.
  • The head H, reservoir to tailwater, drives the turbines: P = η·ρ·g·Q·H, 112 MW in the figure. The depth of water at the heel, a few metres more, sets the load on the dam: 38.7 MN on each metre.
  • Slurry sorts itself: coarse sand settles by the dam and drains fast, so its grains press firmly on one another; the slimes settle by the pond and drain about a thousand times more slowly. The sorting helps only while the pond is kept away from the dam.
  • Every deadly failure in the case table was of a dam raised upstream, standing partly on its own loose, wet waste, and twice nothing from outside set it off at all.
  • Water over the crest, with failed spillways and gates, began about half the failures of large embankment dams, and it washes grains away however hard they press. Every dam needs a spillway, and freeboard above the flood.

Questions

  1. At a turbine flow of 150 m³/s, drag Reservoir level from its lowest to its highest. By what factor does the power grow, and by what factor the water load on the dam? Why are they different?

    Hint

    The power follows the head H; the load follows the square of the depth at the heel.

    The power goes from 74.2 to 121.8 MW, a factor of 1.64, because H goes from 56 to 92 m.
