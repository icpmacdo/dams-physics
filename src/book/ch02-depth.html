So far. On the beach one sand behaved three ways, and only the water had changed. To say why, we need two facts about water first. This chapter is the first of them: how hard still water presses, and on what. adds the second, what happens when it moves.

After this chapter you can

  • work out the water pressure at any depth, and the push of a reservoir on each metre of a dam;
  • say where that push acts, and why doubling the depth makes it four times bigger and its tipping effect eight times bigger;
  • explain why a concrete dam is a triangle, and why water on an embankment's sloping face presses it down harder than it pushes it sideways.

Stand on the crest of a big dam on a still day and look upstream. The reservoir runs back between the hills further than you can see, millions of tonnes of water, and it is hard not to feel all of it leaning on the wall under your feet. Somebody had to decide how strong to make that wall, so the feeling is worth testing. Make a guess before you read on:

Two dams each hold back water 50 m deep. One holds a lake 10 km long. The other holds a slot of water only 2 m wide, with solid rock behind it. Which dam has to be stronger?

The lake is the natural guess, and it is wrong. Water pressure depends only on how far below the surface you are. Fifty metres down it is the same in a slot as in an ocean, so the two dams feel exactly the same push. The big lake is far heavier, but its weight rests on the lake bed. The size of a lake decides how much water escapes if the dam breaks. The depth decides the load on the dam.

Follow where the lake's weight goes. Cut the lake into upright columns, like slices of a cake still in its tin: each column stands on the lake bed and presses straight down on it. The bed carries the lake. Only the water touching the dam pushes on the dam, so the question becomes what decides how hard that water pushes.

You have felt the answer. Swim down to the bottom of a deep pool and, a few metres down, your ears start to hurt. The water above you has weight, and the deeper you go, the more of it there is. That weight is where water pressure comes from. The rest of this chapter turns it into a number, and then into the push on a dam.

A tonne on every square metre

Picture a column of water one metre tall standing on one square metre of a pool floor. It holds one cubic metre of water, which is a tonne. On Earth a tonne weighs 9.81 kilonewtons (kN),Mass and weight are different things. A tonne is 1,000 kg of stuff; gravity pulls on it with 9.81 kN, and that pull is its weight. Turned round, a kilonewton is the weight of about 102 kg. This book uses tonnes for how much water there is and kilonewtons for how hard it pushes. so the floor under that column carries 9.81 kN on its square metre. A force of one kilonewton on each square metre is one kilopascal (kPa), the unit of pressure in this book. Stack a second metre of water on top and the floor carries two tonnes, 19.62 kPa. Every metre of depth adds the weight of one more tonne on every square metre:

p = ρ · g · h ≈ 9.81 kPa × depth in metres

p pressure (kPa) · ρ density of water, 1,000 kg/m³ · g gravity, 9.81 m/s² · h depth below the water surface (m). With these units 1,000 × 9.81 × h comes out in pascals, newtons on each square metre; divide by 1,000 for kPa.

Notice what the rule leaves out. It names the depth and nothing else: not the width of the lake, not its length, not how much water lies beyond. Engineers call the pressure of still water hydrostatic, and in a moment we will see that it is also blind to direction.

Ten metres of water presses with about 98 kPa, roughly the pressure of the whole atmosphere above us.Galileo recorded that a suction pump could lift water no higher than about 18 braccia, some 10 m (Museo Galileo). In a letter of 11 June 1644 to Michelangelo Ricci, Evangelista Torricelli explained why: the air presses on the water outside the pipe, and that press can hold up a column only so tall. He showed it with mercury, which stood about three quarters of a metre high (Museo Galileo). The standard atmosphere, 101.325 kPa, balances 10.3 m of water, or 76 cm of mercury, which is 13.6 times as dense. That raises a fair question. The air presses on the lake's surface too, so shouldn't every reading include it? A sensor that counted the air would read 396 kPa thirty metres down, not 294.Sea-level air: 101.325 kPa and 1.225 kg/m³, . But the air presses as hard on the dam's downstream face, on the rock, on everything, and equal pushes from two sides cancel. So engineers leave it out and measure pressure above the air's, as a tyre gauge does. That is gauge pressure, and this book uses it throughout: the surface of still water is at zero.

Check yourself A lake high in the mountains has thinner air above it than a lake at sea level. Does a gauge 30 m down read less there?

No. It reads 294.3 kPa in both, because a gauge measures the press of the water above the air's. The thinner air lowers the pressure on the surface and on the gauge's back alike.

What it means. A pressure in still water is a property of one number, the depth. A gauge lowered to the foot of the lake dam and one lowered to the foot of the slot dam would read the same.

Sideways too

The column explains why a pool floor feels the water above it. It doesn't yet explain your ears, which face sideways, or a dam, which stands beside the water and not under it. One could assume that water presses mostly downward, the way a stack of bricks does, with a little left over for the walls. Bricks can do that because a brick holds its shape. Water can't. A liquid is a material that flows when anything tries to change its shape, however gently.

So suppose that at some point in still water the push from above were harder than the push from the sides. The water there would be squeezed out sideways, the way toothpaste leaves a tube, and it would start to flow. Still water doesn't flow; that is what still means. Therefore at any point in still water the push is the same in every direction: down, up, sideways and slantwise.A tighter version of the argument. Take a tiny wedge of still water with one face level, one upright and one sloping. The pushes on its three faces must balance its weight. Now shrink the wedge: the pushes shrink with the faces' areas, the weight much faster, with its volume. In the limit the weight drops out, and the pushes balance only if the pressure on all three faces is the same, whatever the slope. At the foot of a 50 m dam the water presses as hard on the concrete beside it as on the rock beneath it, 490.5 kPa each way.

That settles the opening question, and in 1586 the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin found a way to see it without any algebra.Stevin published it in De Beghinselen des Waterwichts (), the first systematic treatise on hydrostatics since Archimedes. He explained the paradox, as in the figure, by imagining parts of the water set solid (). Take the lake, at rest. Imagine that all of it except a slot 2 m wide against the dam sets solid, like jelly, without changing its weight or its shape. Nothing in the lake was moving before, and setting it solid gives nothing a reason to start, so every pressure stays what it was, including every pressure on the dam. They hurt the same. Both are 10 m down and both feel 98.1 kPa. The narrow shaft matters as little as the narrow slot.

The push on a whole dam

A sensor gives the pressure at one point. A dam has to hold the pressure over its whole face, and that pressure changes all the way down. Draw it as does, as arrows: at the surface the arrow has no length, and it grows by 9.81 kPa for every metre down to its longest at the foot. Together the arrows fill a triangle, and the push on each metre length of dam is the area of that triangle: half its height times its width at the bottom, ½ × H × (9.81 × H). The depth appears twice, once as the height of the wet face and once in the pressure at its foot. So the push grows with the square of the depth:

F = ½ · ρ · g · H²

F push of the water on each metre length of dam (kN per m) · H depth of water at the dam (m)

Make the water twice as deep and the triangle is twice as tall and twice as wide at the bottom, so four copies of the first triangle fit inside it. A dam holding 100 m of water takes four times the push of one holding 50 m.

Where the push acts

A push on a dam does two things. It tries to slide the dam downstream, and it tries to tip it over about its toe, the downstream foot (), the way you tip a bookcase by pushing near its top. For tipping, where the push acts matters as much as how big it is. Most of the triangle sits low down: the bottom third of the face carries 56% of the push.Why 56%: the push above any level grows with the square of its depth, so the top two thirds of the face carry (⅔)² = 4/9 of the whole, and the bottom third the other 5/9. Add up all the arrows and the whole push acts as though it were gathered at one point, the centre of the triangle. A triangle's area balances a third of the way in from its wide side, and the wide side of the pressure triangle is at the bottom, so the push acts H/3 up from the base. Its tipping effect about the toe, the moment, is the push times its lever arm, the distance from the pivot to the line the push acts along. For a sideways push that distance is its height above the base, H/3, wherever on the base the pivot is:

M = F · H/3 = ρ · g · H³ / 6

M tipping moment about the toe, per metre length of dam (kN·m per m, or MN·m per m; 1 MN·m is 1,000 kN·m)

Double the depth and the push grows four times while the height it acts at doubles, so the tipping effect grows eight times. Here are all three numbers for the dams of the opening question:

Check. Water weighs a tonne per cubic metre, so for water the push in tonnes is ½ × H × H: ½ × 50 × 50 = 1,250, which is what the figure's readout says.

What it means. The height of the water is what a dam pays for. Each extra metre of depth costs more than the one before.

Check. Where can a sideways push bigger than the water's weight come from? Compare the areas the water presses on. Each metre of the slot's floor is 2 m² and carries the 100 tonnes: 50 tonnes on each square metre, the pressure at 50 m. Each metre of the dam's face is 50 m² and carries on average half that pressure, 25 tonnes on each square metre, so 50 × 25 = 1,250 tonnes. The same pressure acts on a big face and a small floor. Nothing moves, because the rock pushes back on the water as hard as the dam does, from the other side.

What it means. Pressure is not weight. A thin layer of water can carry an enormous push as long as it is deep, which is why a narrow crack under a dam, filled from the reservoir, matters so much in .

By more. The push grows by (60/50)² = 1.44 times, the moment by (60/50)³ = 1.73 times.

Why a concrete dam is a triangle

Look at a concrete dam from the side and it is close to a triangle, narrow at the top and wide at the foot. One could assume the reason is that the pressure is greatest at the bottom, so the wall needs to be thickest there. That explains a thick foot, but not a thin top: a wall as thick as its foot all the way up would hold too. The full reason is about every level at once.

Cut the dam in your mind along a level line, z metres below the water's surface. Everything above the cut has to hold against the water above the cut, or it would slide or tip off along that line; so engineers check a concrete dam at levels all the way up, not only at its base. What holds it is its weight, pressing down on the concrete below ( shows how a press becomes a grip). The push of the water above the cut grows with z². For a triangle, the width at depth z is proportional to z, so the area above the cut, half its width times its height, grows with z² as well. Push and weight grow in step. Why the triangle keeps step

Given
A concrete section with an upright upstream face, 0.8z wide at depth z, water to its top. Concrete weighs 23.5 kN/m³, water 9.81 kN/m³.
Find
At any depth z: the weight of concrete above that level, the push of water above it, and how they compare, for sliding and for tipping.
The idea
Both are areas of triangles whose sides grow with z, so both grow with z². Tipping adds one more factor of z to each, through the lever arms.
  1. Weight above z: ½ × 0.8z × z × 23.5 = 9.40 z² kNarea of the triangle above the cut, times concrete's unit weight
  2. Push above z: ½ × 9.81 × z² = 4.905 z² kN with the depth to the cut
  3. Weight ÷ push = 9.40 ÷ 4.905 = 1.92z² cancels: the same at every level
  4. Holding moment: 9.40z² × ⅔ × 0.8z = 5.01 z³the triangle's centre of area, a third of its width from the upright face (its wide side), so two thirds of the width from the toe
  5. Tipping moment: 4.905z² × z/3 = 1.635 z³the push acts a third of the way up,
  6. Holding ÷ tipping = 5.01 ÷ 1.635 = 3.07z³ cancels too

Above every level of the triangle the concrete weighs 1.92 times the push, and holds 3.07 times the tipping moment.

Check. At z = 30 m: weight 8,460 kN, push 4,414.5 kN, and 8,460 ÷ 4,414.5 = 1.92. At z = 45 m: 19,035 ÷ 9,932.6 = 1.92.

What it means. A triangle gives every level the same margin, so no level is weaker than the rest and none carries concrete it does not need. What this leaves out, water seeping under the dam and pushing it up, is the subject of .

To see what the shape buys, compare a wall with straight sides that uses the same concrete. Down to 50 m the triangle holds 1,000 m³ of concrete for every metre of dam, so the straight wall is 20 m thick. Its weight above a cut grows only in proportion to the depth, while the push grows with the depth squared. Ten metres down it weighs 9.6 times the push above, far more than it needs. At the foot its weight is the same 1.92 times the push as the triangle's, but against tipping it holds only 1.15 times the moment where the triangle holds 3.07, because its weight sits closer to the downstream toe. The triangle puts its concrete where the push is.

Dams that hold back their water by their own weight in this way are called gravity dams, and real ones are close to this shape. Water on a sloping face

Most of the dams in the rest of this book are embankments, heaps of earth and rock whose upstream face leans back at a gentle slope. The still-water argument holds on any face, at any angle: the water presses equally in every direction, so on a surface it presses straight at it, at right angles. On a sloping face the arrows tilt. They point down into the slope as well as across it. Check. Every arrow is at right angles to the face, so the whole push must be too. Its angle from the vertical has a tangent of 2,041 ÷ 4,083 = 0.50, which is 26.6°, the same as the face's angle from the horizontal, 26.6°. At the other extreme, an upright face has no water standing on it and the downward part vanishes, as on the concrete dam.

What it means. On an embankment the reservoir mostly presses the dam onto its foundation, which helps it grip. The picture is exact for a watertight face, such as a concrete skin on rockfill; in an earth dam the water also soaks into the face, and follows it inside. shows what happens when the reservoir is let down fast: the help on the face goes, while the water inside the dam stays.

Water in the ground has pressure too

Dig a hole in the sand near the sea and it fills with water from below. The water in the hole settles at a level, and below that level every gap between the grains is full of water. That level is the water table.

The water in those gaps is joined up from gap to gap, like the water in a soaked sponge, so while it is still it obeys the same rule as the water in a lake. A metre below the water table it presses at 9.81 kPa, ten metres down at 98.1 kPa, and at every point it presses equally in every direction, on every grain around the gap. The pressure of the water in the gaps between grains is called pore pressure, written u. It is the water half of this book's one sentence: shows how it takes away the press of grain on grain that gives soil its strength.

The important word there is still. The water in a lake is still. The water in the ground under a dam almost never is: it creeps through the gaps from the reservoir toward the far side of the dam, and moving water does not keep to the rule of this chapter.

What to remember

  • Still water presses equally in every direction, and how hard depends only on the depth: 9.81 kPa for every metre. The size of the lake does not enter.
  • The push on each metre of dam is the area of the pressure triangle, ½·ρ·g·H², acting a third of the way up. Double the depth and the push grows four times, its tipping effect eight.
  • A gravity dam is a triangle because the weight above any level then grows with the square of the depth, as the push does, so every level is equally safe.
  • On a sloping face the water presses at right angles to the slope; on an embankment it presses the dam down harder than it pushes it across.
  • Water in the gaps of soaked ground follows the same rule while it is still. That pore pressure pushes neighbouring grains apart, which is how water starts to take away the grains' press on one another.

Questions

  1. A reservoir is raised from 40 m deep to 60 m. By what factor does the push on each metre of its dam grow?

    Hint

    The push grows with the depth squared, so compare 60² with 40².

    From 7.85 MN to 17.66 MN per metre, a factor of (60/40)² = 2.25. Half as deep again more than doubles the push.

  2. Predict the push on each metre of dam when the water is 70 m deep, then set the figure and check. How does it compare with the push at 50 m?

    Hint

    70² is close to 2 × 50².

    24.0 MN per metre, as heavy as 2,450 tonnes: 1.96 times the push at 50 m, because 70² = 4,900 is nearly twice 50² = 2,500.

  3. Your ears hurt 3 m down in a pool, but not after a 30 m ride up a lift in a tall building. Why?

    Hint

    Both are the same rule, , with a different fluid. Air at sea level weighs about 1.2 kg per cubic metre, water 1,000.

    Three metres of water changes the pressure by 29.4 kPa. Thirty metres of sea-level air, at 1.225 kg/m³ (), changes it by only 0.36 kPa, because water is about 816 times denser than air. The ears feel the difference.

  4. Where does the push on a dam holding 50 m of water act, and by what factor does its tipping moment grow if the water is twice as deep?

    Hint

    The push acts at the centre of the pressure triangle; the moment is push times height.

    16.7 m above the base, a third of the way up. Doubling the depth doubles that height and quadruples the push, so the moment grows 8 times, from 204 to 1,635 MN·m per metre.

  5. Is the water on an embankment's sloping upstream face a danger or a help to the dam's stability?

    Hint

    Split its push into the part across and the part down. What does each do?

    Mostly a help. On a 2:1 face the water presses down 2 times as hard as it pushes across (), and pressing down holds the dam onto its foundation. That is exact for a watertight face; in an earth dam some of the water soaks into the slope (). The sideways part still has to be resisted, and the help disappears when the reservoir is let down quickly, the subject of .
