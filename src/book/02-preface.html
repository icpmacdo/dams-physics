Preface

Stand on the crest of an earth dam and look down the far side. A slope of grass, a gravel road along the top, a ditch at the bottom where a little clear water runs. Behind you is a lake, perhaps tens of metres deep and several kilometres long. Between the two is a heap of soil and broken rock, much of it dug from the hills nearby and rolled flat in layers. That a heap of loose grains can hold a lake back for a century, and the few ways it stops doing so, is what this book is about.

It is written for a curious adult with school arithmetic: someone who can work out a percentage, read a graph and is willing to meet a tangent. The toolkit after checks every piece of maths the book uses, and that is all of it. If you are an engineering student you will find here the ideas of a first course in soil mechanics and dam engineering, worked with real numbers, along with plain statements of where the models are simplified.

Dams hold back more than drinking water and the water that turns turbines. They protect towns from floods, and at mines they hold what is left of the ore once the metal is out, ground to sand and silt and mixed with water. That waste has to stay put long after the mine closes. When a dam of either kind fails, everything it held comes out at once.

Most of the examples come from British Columbia. More than 1,900 dams there are regulated under the province's Water Sustainability Act,. Tailings dams at mines are not in that count; they are regulated under the Mines Act. and the dams at its mines come under a separate law. Four of them run through these pages. Mount Polley, whose night in August 2014 opens the book, is the one the whole book works toward. The W.A.C. Bennett Dam on the Peace River shows what each zone inside an embankment is for. Coquitlam shows that the way a dam was built can matter more than what it holds. Testalinden, a small dam near Oliver, shows that inspection and regulation are part of a dam as much as its soil is.

By the last chapter you will be able to read a dam's cross-section the way an engineer does, work out how hard the water pushes on it and where, follow water through and under it, and say why one soil is strong and another weak. You will be able to check whether a slope will stand, name the ways embankments fail, and design a small dam of your own. You will also be able to say what your own check cannot see.

One sentence holds the book together. It is on the title page: soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and the water between them can take that press away. The first chapters build it from a beach, a bucket and a block on a ramp; proves it; every chapter after that applies it. It does not cover everything that brings a dam down. A flood that pours over the crest can wash a heap away however hard its grains press, and deals with that separately.

The figures run in your browser, among them a seepage solver, a slope-stability solver and a box of simulated grains. Each number the text quotes from a figure is checked by a test that recomputes it from the code behind the figure, so the words and the drawings can't drift apart. Numbers from the literature carry a note in the margin that names their source. The seven narrated films were made separately; each is captioned, and each has a transcript.
