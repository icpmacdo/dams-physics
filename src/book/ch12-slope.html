A dam made all of compacted fill has a factor of safety of 1.12, with water breaking out 8 m up its downstream slope. Which change helps it most?

The drain does the most: the factor of safety goes from 1.12 to about 1.6. The flatter slope reaches only about 1.27, and the cutoff about 1.14. The fill is strong enough when it is dry. Its trouble is the water in it: on the critical slip surface, water pressure carries about a third of the soil's weight, so the grains press together with only two-thirds of it. A drain lets that water out before it reaches the downstream slope. You'll paint that drain yourself in .

What the check does, and what it leaves out

For circular slip surfaces the page uses Bishop's simplified method, the standard hand-and-computer method since 1955. It cuts the sliding mass into 40 vertical slices, works out how hard each slice presses on its base after taking away the water pressure there, and balances the turning effect of the whole mass about the circle's centre. On the weak-layer foundation it also tries sliding blocks with a flat base along the layer, using Spencer's method, which balances forces and turning effects together.

Water pressure on each slice comes straight from the seepage solution. Suction above the water level is ignored, as is usual practice, because it can vanish in a wet spell. For the upstream check the reservoir is emptied quickly: the free-draining materials (anything that passes water at 10⁻⁵ m/s or faster: rockfill, filter sand, tailings sand and the sand & gravel foundation) drain with it, while clay and fill keep their water pressure for a while (the classic rapid drawdown case). Designers accept a lower factor of safety for that short-lived condition, about 1.2. The workbench in fixes the upstream slope at 2:1, which is steep for an earth dam; real ones are often flatter for exactly this reason.
