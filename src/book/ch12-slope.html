One question is left to ask of any dam with water inside it: will its slope stand, and by how much?

An earth dam rarely announces that it is in trouble. The first sign is often a strip of its downstream face that stays green through a dry summer, or a band of dark, soft ground a few metres up the slope where water seeps out into the light. Walk our dam, the 24 m embankment of the last three chapters, built all of one compacted soil on clay ground, and you would find that band 8 m up its downstream face, exactly where the seepage lab of said the water would come out. Nothing is moving, and the crest road is level.

The engineer who checks the slope gets a single number back, a factor of safety of 1.12. Above 1 the slope stands, so this one stands, with 12% to spare. The owner has money for one fix. Make a choice before you read on:

A dam made all of compacted fill has a factor of safety of 1.12, with water breaking out 8 m up its downstream slope. Which change helps it most?

The drain does the most: the factor of safety goes from 1.12 to about 1.6. The flatter slope reaches only about 1.27, and the cutoff about 1.14. The fill is strong enough when it is dry. Its trouble is the water in it: on the critical slip surface, water pressure carries about a third of the soil's weight (0.35 of it), so the grains press together with only two-thirds of it. A drain lets that water out before it reaches the downstream slope. You'll paint that drain yourself in .

Whatever you chose, the choice rests on a number, and a number is only worth what you know about how it was made. To judge the fixes we need to make that number ourselves, starting where left off: with a slope so long and even that one small piece of it stands for the whole.

The long slope, scored

found the steepest face a sand can hold by treating one patch of the face as the block on its ramp. A designer doesn't build at the steepest face; she builds flatter and asks how much flatter. So ask the same patch a different question: how much grip does it have, against how much it needs? That ratio is the factor of safety, and for a long, even slope of grains with no glue between them, the slope called an infinite slope, it takes one line.

On a dry slope at angle β, a slab of soil parallel to the face presses into the slope below it with its weight times cos β, and is pulled down it by its weight times sin β. Friction offers tan φ′ times the press. The weight cancels, as it did for the block:

FS = tan φ′ ÷ tan β

φ′ the soil's friction angle · β the slope's angle; a slope of n:1 has tan β = 1/n, so FS = n · tan φ′. It holds dry, and it holds under still water, where buoyancy lightens the press and the pull alike.

The formula hides a pleasant fact: the factor of safety grows in proportion to how flat the slope is. Make a 2:1 slope into a 3:1 slope and its safety rises by half. But the water has its own say. Where water seeps out of the face, parallel to it, its drag adds to the pull and nothing to the press, and showed what that costs: the grip falls in the ratio of the soil's weight in water, its buoyant unit weight γ′, to its soaked weight γsat:

FS = (γ′ ÷ γsat) · tan φ′ ÷ tan β

γ′ = γsat − γw, the soaked weight less the water's, 9.81 kN/m³. For most soils γ′/γsat is close to one half.

What it means. Water seeping out of a face halves its safety. A slope that grips well enough dry can slide with water coming out of it, without a grain changing; only the water moved.

Slopes slide on curves

One could assume that every slope fails the way the long-slope rule pictures it, a thin skin peeling off its face like snow off a roof. Clean sand and rockfill do fail that way, which is why the rule and the solver agreed. Our dam's fill does not. It has a little cohesion, c′ = 10 kPa, a grip that doesn't depend on how hard its grains are pressed, and a thin skin has too little weight for its friction to matter and plenty of cohesion for its size. A deep slip is the cheaper failure: it has more weight pulling, through soil that is wetter and so presses less.

The shape engineers give a deep slip comes from Sweden. In March 1916, a year after it was built, the Stigberg Quay in Gothenburg harbour slid, and a committee was set up to find out why.Petterson told the story himself in English, nearly forty years later (). Wolmar Fellenius, who chaired Sweden's state geotechnical commission, extended the method to soils with both friction and cohesion in the 1920s and defined the factor of safety for a slope as the ratio of the moments holding it to those turning it (). The commission's final report of 1922 is the first publication known to use the word "geotechnical". The committee's analysis used a method worked out by Knut Petterson, an engineer with the harbour, and Sven Hultin, a professor at Chalmers: take the surface the ground slides on to be an arc of a circle. A circle has a convenient property: a mass sliding on one turns about the circle's centre as a solid piece, so its balance can be checked with one sum of turning effects. The Swedish engineers cut the sliding mass into upright slices and added them up. Most checks of an embankment slope still start there.

Look at one slice and you have met it before. It is 's block on a ramp, except that its ramp is a short stretch of the circle, tilted at an angle α that changes from slice to slice: steep under the crest, nearly level near the bottom of the circle, tilted the other way where the circle climbs back out beyond the toe. Each slice's weight W pulls it down its stretch with W sin α; the soil under it grips back with cohesion over the length of the base, l, plus friction from the grains' press on it, N′. This is the method of slices. The slip surface's factor of safety is the grip of all the slices over the pull of all of them:

FS = Σ (c′·l + N′·tan φ′) ÷ Σ W·sin α

Σ the sum over every slice · c′·l + N′·tan φ′ the grip on a slice's base (kN per metre of dam) · W·sin α its pull. The ratio comes from balancing turning effects about the circle's centre: each slice's weight acts at a lever arm of R sin α from it, each grip at R, the presses on the bases point at the centre and turn nothing, and the radius cancels.

The pull is easy: weights and angles, measured off the drawing. All the difficulty is in N′, how hard the grains on each base press, and that is where the water comes in.

One slice by hand

The water in the gaps pushes on the base of a slice with its pressure u over the base's length, u·l, and whatever part of the slice's load the water carries, the grains don't. The earliest method of slices took the press on the base to be the part of the weight at right angles to it, less the water: N′ = W cos α − u·l. That treats each slice as if its neighbours weren't there. But they are, and they push on it.

Alan Bishop's step, published in 1955, was to let them push, but only sideways, and to balance each slice up and down.Bishop, at Imperial College London, published the method in . Taking the slices' pushes on each other as horizontal is why it is called his "simplified" method; his paper also gave a fuller version that keeps their upright parts, which matter little on a circle. E. Spencer's method of 1967 () balances the forces and the turning effects together, taking the pushes as parallel, and works on surfaces of any shape. Sideways pushes have no upright part, so they drop out of that balance. What holds the slice up is then the water and the grains pressing on its base, both at right angles to it, and the grip along the base, which at the point of balance is the grip available divided by the factor of safety. Solve that balance for N′ and you get Bishop's method:

N′ = (W − u·b − c′·b·tan α ÷ FS) ÷ mα,  mα = cos α + sin α · tan φ′ ÷ FS

b the slice's width (m) · u·b the upright part of the water's push on the base · c′·b·tan α ÷ FS the upright part of the cohesion's share of the grip · mα the factor that turns what is left into a press at right angles to the base.

Look where FS is: on the right-hand side. To know how hard each slice presses, you must already know the factor of safety, and to know the factor of safety you must know how hard every slice presses. The way out is to guess, work every slice with the guess, add them up with , and use the answer as the next guess. Check. The water carries 103.8 of the slice's 319.0 kN, about a third, close to the share it carries on the whole circle (0.35). The solver, which carries every digit, gets 181.38 and 129.35 kN: the rounding on this page costs less than a tenth of a kilonewton.

What it means. On its own, slice 13 would slide: its grip is 0.77 of its pull. A slip circle stands or falls as a whole, and a third of this slice's weight is carried by water, not grains.

The slices that save slice 13 are the ones near the bottom of the circle. Where the circle climbs back up toward the toe, a slice's base tilts the other way, and its weight pushes back against the slide instead of driving it; 11 of the forty are like that. Their grip counts in full, and their pull is negative: it takes away from the total. The older method that ignored the neighbours scores this same circle at 0.978: it says the dam should already have slid. It errs on the safe side, by a lot when the water pressure is high, and that is what Bishop's sideways pushes fixed.

Check yourself If you started Bishop's loop at a guess of 1.0 instead of 1.5, would it end somewhere else?

No. The guess only sets the starting point; the loop keeps going until the slices return the number they were worked with, and only one number does that, 1.116. From 1.0 it climbs to it instead of falling.

What the water costs

Slice 13 lost about a third of its weight to the water. So did the whole circle, and that is the number that explains the fixes. Bishop and Morgenstern, writing in 1960, put a slope's water into a single ratio, the pore-pressure ratio ru, the share of the soil's weight that the water pressure carries.In ru is the pore pressure at a point divided by the weight of soil above it, and the paper's charts give a slope's factor of safety as a straight line falling with ru. The workbench reports one value for a whole surface, the slices' Σ u·b ÷ Σ W. The paper's second author, Norbert Morgenstern, is the engineer the Prologue met at the head of a review panel. Emptying the reservoir

So far the reservoir has been full and the danger on the downstream side, where the water comes out. The upstream face lives the other way round. A full reservoir is good for it: showed that water standing on a sloping face presses it down harder than it pushes it across, and under still water the grains of the upstream shell lose their press and their pull in the same proportion. The danger comes when the reservoir is let down fast, to reach a damaged outlet, to take the load off a dam in trouble, or because the water is needed downstream.

Emptying a reservoir takes days or weeks. Draining the water out of compacted fill takes far longer: at 10⁻⁷ m/s, the fill passes water a hundred times more slowly than the tailings sand and ten thousand times more slowly than the filter sand of a drain, and showed that the time a layer takes to drain grows with the square of its thickness. So the fill's water stays in while the reservoir's water goes. The water inside still has pressure, now with nothing outside to balance it; it seeps out through the face, and its drag pulls the face's grains downhill, exactly as on the beach face of . Its pressure has fallen only by the weight of the water taken off above it, while the reservoir that pressed on the face from outside has gone altogether. In 's terms, the upstream slope went from a heap under still water to a heap with water seeping out of its face. Engineers call this rapid drawdown, and the workbench checks it the classic way: water pressure on each slice comes straight from the seepage solution; the free-draining materials (anything that passes water at 10⁻⁵ m/s or faster: rockfill, filter sand, tailings sand and the sand & gravel foundation) drain with the reservoir, while clay and fill keep their water pressure, less only the weight of the water taken off above them. The reservoir is taken all the way down at once, and the fill is given no time at all to drain: a deliberately pessimistic picture of a drawdown that in life takes days or weeks.

What the check does, and what it leaves out

For circular slip surfaces the workbench uses Bishop's simplified method, the standard hand-and-computer method since 1955. It cuts the sliding mass into 40 vertical slices, works out how hard each slice presses on its base after taking away the water pressure there, and balances the turning effect of the whole mass about the circle's centre. It tries more than two thousand circles, from many entry and exit points and radii, then searches around the worst few, and keeps the lowest score.It ignores any slip shallower than 2 m. On a face of clean sand or rockfill the long-slope rule gives every depth the same score, so a search left to itself drifts to the thinnest skin it may, a few grains deep. Grains rolling off a face are called ravelling, and mending them is a maintenance job; the dam itself has not moved. On the weak-layer foundation it also tries sliding blocks with a flat base along the layer, using Spencer's method, which balances forces and turning effects together and so works on surfaces that are not circles.

Suction above the water level is ignored, as is usual practice, because it can vanish in a wet spell. Each set of conditions a slope is checked for is a load case, and the workbench checks two: the downstream slope with the reservoir full and the seepage steady, and the upstream slope after a rapid drawdown. It leaves out the others a designer must run: the end of construction, when the fill has only now been placed and the water squeezed into it by the weight of the layers above has not yet drained; an earthquake; a flood that raises the reservoir above its normal level. The workbench in fixes the upstream slope at 2:1, which is steep for an earth dam; real ones are often flatter because of rapid drawdown.

What 1.5 means

Each load case comes with a target. The US Army Corps of Engineers, whose manual on slope stability is a common reference, asks a new earth or rockfill dam for 1.5 under long-term steady seepage, 1.3 at the end of construction, and 1.1 to 1.3 right after a rapid drawdown., p. 3-2, its table of minimum factors of safety for new earth and rock-fill dams; 1.1 for drawdown from the maximum surcharge pool, 1.3 from the maximum storage pool. The manual says on p. 3-1 that the values "continue to be based on experience". The workbench's missions use 1.5 and, for the drawdown, 1.2.

A factor of safety of 1.5 means the grip available on the worst surface is one and a half times the grip needed to hold it. Turned round, the soil could lose a third of its strength before that surface moved. Nobody derived 1.5 from physics, and it says nothing about the chance of a failure. It is a margin chosen by experience to cover what nobody can measure exactly: the strength of soil that was sampled in a few places and tested in a few small pieces, water pressures that piezometers read at a few points, and a model that is only a model. Designers accept less for the short-lived cases partly because they last days or weeks rather than the life of the dam, and partly because the way they are checked is already pessimistic, as the workbench's instant, total drawdown is.

The margin covers ordinary uncertainty. It cannot cover a wrong input. The pull in is weights and angles, and those are hard to get badly wrong. The grip depends on the strength you feed it, c′ and φ′, and a factor of safety is only as good as that strength. showed that strength depends on how fast the soil is loaded: given time, the water in a soil leaves as the load comes on and the grains take the press; loaded fast, the water cannot leave, and in a soil whose grains want to pack closer it takes part of the press itself. A calculation fed the slow strength scores a slow loading. It says nothing about a fast one.

What to remember

  • A slope's factor of safety is its grip over its pull. For a long slope of clean grains it is n · tan φ′ for an n:1 slope, and water seeping out of the face multiplies it by γ′/γsat, about a half: the water adds pull and no press.
  • Real embankments slide on deep curves. Cut the mass into slices, each a block on a curved ramp, and add up their grips and pulls. Bishop's method balances each slice up and down, so the factor of safety sits on both sides of the sum and must be found by going round a loop.
  • On our dam's worst circle the water carries 0.35 of the slices' weight, and costs the slope a third of its score. A drain that takes the water away does more than a quarter more fill.
  • Rapid drawdown takes the reservoir's help away from the upstream face and leaves the fill's water in it. Only a free-draining shell gets its grip back as fast as the water falls.
  • 1.5 is a margin chosen by experience for what nobody can measure exactly. The number is only as good as the strength fed to it, and a soil's strength depends on how fast it is loaded.

    How long does each condition last, and how was each one checked?

    A full reservoir with steady seepage is the dam's everyday state for decades, so it gets the full margin. A rapid drawdown lasts days or weeks before the water drains out of the fill, it happens rarely, and the check for it already leans toward the worst: the reservoir falls all the way at once and the fill gets no time to drain. The manual's own range for it is 1.1 to 1.3 (, in its table of minimum factors).
