When the falling tide leaves the sand firm, as the foot falls on it the sand whitens, or appears momentarily to dry round the foot.

Osborne Reynolds, On the dilatancy of media composed of rigid particles in contact, 1885

So far. The Prologue left a dam that had passed its calculations and failed in a night. Following the engineers who worked out why takes a handful of ideas about soil and water, and the cheapest place to collect them is a beach.

After this chapter you can

  • describe the three ways one sand behaves, and name the one thing that changed between them;
  • measure an angle of repose, and write any slope both as an angle and as a ratio, across to up;
  • say why a damp sand wall stands 30 cm tall and never 30 m.

Walk down a beach at low tide, from the dunes to the sea, and the sand changes under you three times. Up by the dunes it is dry. Your foot sinks in, the sand pours back round your ankle, and when you kick it, it runs.

Halfway down, where the sea was an hour ago, the sand is dark and firm. It takes a crisp footprint, and as your weight comes down a pale ring spreads round your foot for a moment, as if the sand had dried where you stood. Then the colour comes back.

At the edge, where every wave lays down a fresh sheet of sand and soaks it, you can stand quite happily as long as you keep still. Wiggle your feet and the sand goes soft. You sink to the ankles, and it takes a tug to get out.

Three steps, one sand. The grains are near enough the same all the way down the beach. What changes is the water in the gaps between them. At the top there is almost none. In the middle every gap is still full, but the water is held up in the sand by the pull of its own surface, the way a sponge lifted out of the bath stays full, and the grains there are packed tight. At the edge the gaps are full too, and the grains lie loose.

Three sands

Take those three sands home in buckets. On the way the middle sand drains a little, as sand lifted out of water does, and arrives damp: air in most of its gaps, and water clinging only where the grains touch. Then the three keep their characters.

Dry sand pours. Tip it out and it spreads into a low heap whose sides settle at one slope, however carefully you pour.

Damp sand stands. Pack it into the bucket, turn it out, and you get a castle with walls that go straight up.

Loose wet sand flows. Where every gap is full of water and the grains were laid down loosely, the sand bears you while you keep still and turns to something like soup when you shake it.

Ask people what water does to sand and most of them say it makes it slippery, the way water makes a tiled floor slippery. Keep that answer in mind, because the castle is already evidence against it. Damp sand has water in it, and it stands steeper than either of the others. Whatever the water is doing in the castle, it is not greasing the grains. settles the question with a block on a ramp. For now, notice only that more water does not mean weaker sand, or stronger: the soup holds the most water and the castle the next most, and they are the weakest and the strongest of the three.

The three sands also differ in a second way, which the beach hides. Sand that a wave has just dropped lies loosely packed, with wide gaps. Sand that has been walked on, rained on and settled for a tide lies densely packed, each grain nestled into the hollows of its neighbours. Wet sand that is saturated (every gap full of water, no air at all) can be either.

Then notice the two wet states. Sand that is wet all through sounds as if it ought to slump flatter than dry sand; wet sand, after all, is supposed to be slippery. Pick Under still water and the heap stands about as steep as dry sand, at about 34°. Still water hardly changes its slope. Now pick Water draining out. This is the state of a beach face at low tide, where the water that soaked in at high tide seeps back out through the sand, and the heap slumps to about 19°. The water is the same water in both. In one it sits still; in the other it moves, and the moving water drags the grains downhill as it leaves. Why that drag costs about fifteen degrees is a sum of a few lines, worked in .

So saturation alone does not turn sand to soup; it takes loose sand as well. That is the water's edge and the firm middle of the beach in one experiment, and it is also the first hint about the pale ring round your foot, which explains.The pale ring was described in 1885 by Osborne Reynolds, the Manchester engineer whose name is on the Reynolds number of fluid flow, in the paper that coined the word dilatancy. The epigraph to this chapter is his description of it.

The angle of repose

The slope a dry heap settles at has a name, the angle of repose, and you can watch it form. Pour dry sand from a funnel onto a table. Grains land on the top and roll down the sides until the sides reach the steepest slope on which a grain can rest without rolling. Pour more and the heap grows, but its sides keep that one slope, because any extra steepness slides off in small avalanches. The slope of its sides is set by how hard the grains grip one another, not by how much sand you pour.Charles-Augustin Coulomb spent 1764 to 1772 building a fort on Martinique for the French army. In 1773 he read the Paris Academy a memoir on walls, arches and the earth behind them. In its worked examples, earth that, left to itself, settles at a slope of 45° is taken to have friction equal to the press on it: the slope of a loose heap measures the friction of its grains (, pp. 361–362).

Dry sands settle in the low-to-mid thirties of degrees. Angular, rough grains stand a little steeper than round, polished ones, and the lee faces of dunes, where wind-blown sand piles up until it avalanches, stand at about 32–34°.Dune faces, measured by laser scanning: , who give 32–33° for the faces they surveyed in Brazil and quote earlier surveys at 32° settled and 34° just before an avalanche. Grain shape and friction set the dry angle: . The sand drawn in settles at 34°, near the top of that range. Every slope in the figure follows from that one angle and the weights of sand and water.

The figure draws its heap as a slice. Picture the castle as a long wall of sand running away from you, like a sea wall, and the drawing as one slice cut straight across it. When the wall slumps, each slice keeps its own sand, so what stays the same is the area of the slice, which engineers call the section. Every drawing in this book is a section of something long, and every force and every flow is counted per metre of its length. A dam is drawn the same way.

That gives a sum you can do. The castle in the figure is a slice of known area. Poured dry, the same sand makes a triangular heap with 34° sides. How tall?

The idea
The dry heap's section is a triangle with 34° sides. Write its area in terms of its height, set it equal to the castle's, and solve for the height ().

  2. A = ½ × (2H ÷ tan β) × H = H² ÷ tan βa triangle's area is half its base times its height

Check. Put the area back together: ½ × 58.27 × 19.653 = 572.6 cm², the castle's area. And the limits behave: a gentler slope (smaller tan β) gives a lower, wider heap, and a slope near vertical gives a tall thin one, more like the castle.

What it means. The castle lost no sand when it dried; it lost the grip that held its walls up, and the same sand then needed about three times the ground. Soil that cannot hold a steep face has to be laid out wide. Most of the width of an earth dam is the price of its slopes.

You can measure an angle of repose at home with a funnel, a ruler and a cup of dry sand, sugar or rice. Pour a heap, measure its height and the width of its base, and the tangent of the angle is the height divided by half the base. The questions at the end of the chapter ask you to try.

A slope is a number

Angles are awkward on a building site. Nobody holds a protractor against a hillside of fill. Engineers write a slope as a ratio instead, how far across for each unit up, and they put the across first: a slope of 2H:1V (two across for every one up) climbs one metre for every two metres of ground it covers. This book writes it 2H:1V, or 2:1 for short. The bigger the first number, the flatter the slope.Road signs turn the same ratio upside down and give it as a percentage, rise over run: a 10% hill climbs 1 m for every 10 m across, which this book would write 10:1.

The ratio and the angle are tied by one piece of trigonometry. The tangent of a slope's angle β is the rise over the run, so for a slope written n:1, tan β = 1 ÷ n. A dam drawn at 2:1 and a heap at 34° can then be compared on the same footing:

The idea
tan β = rise ÷ run = 1 ÷ n, which works in both directions.

  2. β = arctan 0.5 = 26.6°the angle whose tangent is 0.5
  3. n = 1 ÷ tan 34° = 1 ÷ 0.6745 = 1.48across for one up, for the dry heap

Check. A 1:1 slope is 45°, as steep as it is wide, and a 3:1 slope is arctan ⅓ = 18.4°. A 2:1 slope must lie between them, and 26.6° does.

What it means. A 2:1 face built of this beach's sand would stand dry, with room to spare, and would slump wherever water seeped out of it. Keeping water from coming out of the face of a dam, or catching it safely where it does, is much of Part III of this book.

Check yourself A road sign warns of a 10% hill. Write the slope as a ratio, and as an angle.

10:1, ten across for each one up. tan β = 0.1, so β = 5.7°: a hill that feels steep in a car is far flatter than any heap of sand.

Why a castle stays small

Look again at the close-up in with the sand set to Damp. The water does not fill the gaps. It gathers in small rings where one grain touches the next, and the surface of each ring is curved and pulled tight, like the skin on a drop hanging from a tap. That skin pulls the two grains together. Every grain has several, and a handful of sand holds a great many grains, so together the rings give damp sand a grip that dry sand lacks, and that grip holds a wall up where dry grains would roll away.It takes remarkably little liquid. Films of oil less than 50 nanometres thick on plastic beads about 0.8 mm across raised the steepest slope a heap would hold sharply (). Columns of beach sand were strongest with about 1% water by volume, and the tallest column that would stand grew as its base radius to the power ⅔ ().

But the grip is small, and it does not grow as the castle does. Each ring pulls with the same tiny force however much sand sits above it. The weight that the bottom of a wall must carry grows with every centimetre of height. A wall 30 cm tall puts a hundred times less weight on each square metre of its base than a wall 30 m tall of the same sand, and somewhere between the two the weight wins. Sand sculptors do build castles several metres high, but they build them on broad bases that lean on their own width.Sandcastles "of several meters high", and the rule that the safe height depends on the width of the base: . puts a number on the grip.

There is a second reason a dam engineer never counts on it. The rings exist only while the sand is damp. A week of rain fills the gaps, the rings vanish, and the grip goes with them; a drought dries them out and does the same. A castle slumps as the rising tide soaks it for the same reason, before the waves reach it. Dams are designed as if the grip were not there.

Check yourself Of the three beach sands, which holds the most water, and which stands steepest?

The soup at the water's edge holds the most, and it stands at nothing at all once shaken. The damp castle holds a little and stands vertical. Water can make sand stronger or weaker; what matters is where the water sits and what it is doing.

The beach inside a dam

Now scale the heap up to a hill, and lean a lake against one side of it. That is an earth dam. The water in the reservoir soaks into the soil and creeps slowly through it, so inside the dam there is a boundary between soil that is full of water and soil above it that is not. Engineers call that boundary the phreatic surface: the water line inside the dam. It starts at the reservoir level on the upstream face, the side the water comes from, and falls as it crosses toward the downstream face, the side the water is trying to reach. (Every section in this book is drawn with the water on the left, so it flows left to right.) Here is a dam with the three beach sands mapped onto it:

Notice where the water comes out. At the foot of the downstream face, the soil is in the state that slumped the heap in from 34° to 19°, and the face there is 2:1, 26.6°. A dam of beach sand would crumble at its foot, grain by grain, and the slope above would follow. Real dams are built of better-chosen soils, and a well-designed one catches the water in drains and filters inside the dam before it can reach the face, and how they do it is the work of . But the danger is the beach's, at the scale of a hill.

Notice too that the upstream face, under the reservoir, is the heap under still water: soaked, and standing about as steep as it would dry. The water there is not quite still, since it is soaking slowly into the dam, and follows where it goes. The damp soil above the water line has the castle's grip, and it is the grip a designer leaves out. Everything below the line is held up by grains pressing on grains, with water filling every gap between them.

One sentence for the whole book

All of this can be said in one sentence. It is printed on the title page, and every chapter after this one comes back to it:

Soil holds only as well as its grains press on one another, and the water between them can take that press away.

You can't prove it yet, and you shouldn't believe it yet. The castle seems to contradict it: there the water makes the grains press harder. By the end of you will be able to prove it, castle included, with a block on a ramp and a column of soil. The sentence does not explain every way a dam can fail. Floods that pour over the top are a different story, told in . But it explains most of what this book asks of soil.

What to remember

  • One sand pours, stands or flows, and only the water between its grains changed.
  • Dry sand stands at its angle of repose, 34° here. Still water barely changes that; water seeping out of a face drags the grains downhill and the face slumps to about 19°.
  • Saturated sand turns to soup when shaken only if it is loosely packed.
  • A slope is written across to up, n:1, with tan β = 1 ÷ n; 2:1 is 26.6°.
  • Damp sand's grip is small and does not grow with height, so castles stay small and dams are designed without it.

    Hint
