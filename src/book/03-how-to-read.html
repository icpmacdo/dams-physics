How to read this book

This is a book to read and a set of instruments to play. A few habits get the most out of both, and each is shown here once, working.

Commit before you look

Some figures wait behind a question. Pick an answer before you open them. A wrong guess you committed to teaches more than a right answer you only nodded at, because it shows you exactly which picture in your head needs mending. Try this one:

A glass is filled to the brim with dry sand. Can you still pour water into it without spilling any?

About a third. Sand grains can't fill space: they touch at points and leave gaps between them, and in the sand this book uses the gaps make up 35% of the volume. Every drop of water in this book lives in gaps like those, and almost everything that goes wrong with a dam starts there.

Figures you can play with

Most figures are instruments. The strip across the top gives each one its number, and on the right says whether it is drawn to scale or simplified. , on the next pages, is a still drawing; is live, and its sliders move a sensor through a lake. Every caption opens with the figure's claim in bold, says how to read the drawing, gives the one number worth keeping and ends with a short list of things to try. On a phone, every figure wider than the text has an Expand button that opens it full screen; turn the phone sideways to read it.

Each chapter opens with a small drawing of a dam that grows a piece at a time. starts with bare ground, and by it is the whole section; the words under it say what that chapter adds.

Every number the text quotes from a figure is the figure's own. A test recomputes each one from the code that draws the figure, so when says a dam's slope has a factor of safety of 1.12 (its strength divided by the push on it; explains it), the workbench in the last chapter shows 1.12 for the same dam.

Worked examples, and Set the figure

Each chapter works two to five problems with real numbers, one step to a line, with the reason for each step beside it (under it, on a phone). Most end with a Set the figure button, which loads the example's numbers into the live figure and takes you there; a pill at the foot of the screen brings you back. Here is the smallest one in the book:

How hard does the water press 10 m down?

Given
A sensor 10 m below the surface of still water. Water weighs 9.81 kN per cubic metre.
Find
The water pressure on the sensor.
The idea
Every square metre at that depth carries the weight of the column of water standing on it.
  1. column above one square metre: 10 m × 1 m² = 10 m³the water standing on the sensor's patch
  2. its weight: 10 × 9.81 = 98.1 kNeach cubic metre weighs 9.81 kN
  3. p = 98.1 kN ÷ 1 m² = 98.1 kPapressure is force per area; 1 kPa is 1 kN/m²

The water presses 98.1 kPa on the sensor.

Check. Set the figure: it rounds to whole kilopascals and shows 98. Double the depth and the column doubles, so at 20 m it shows 196 kPa; at the surface, with no column, zero.

What it means. Ten metres of water press about as hard as the whole height of the air above us. The size of the lake appears nowhere in the sum. asks whether that can really be right.

Notes in the margin

Notes sit in the right-hand margin beside the line they belong to, and on a phone they fold away behind a small number or ⊕ that you tap to open.Like this one. Nothing the argument needs is ever in a note; you can skip every one and lose nothing but the pleasure. They come in four kinds. Source notes name where a number from the literature comes from, with the page or table when there is one; has several. Units notes translate a unit into something you can feel.A kilopascal is a kilonewton spread over a square metre: about the weight of a 100 kg mass, 0.981 kN, resting on a square metre. History notes say who found something out, and when. For the curious notes hold a derivation or an aside for anyone who wants to go a step further.

Check yourself, and the questions at the end

Inside each chapter, a few one-line questions fold open to show their answer:

Check yourself A dam's crest is 24 m above the ground, and the lake behind it is 20.4 m deep. How far below the crest is the water?

3.6 m. That margin has a name, the freeboard, and shows it on this very dam.

Each chapter ends with a box of what to remember and then four to six questions. A tag says what kind each one is, and the dots say how hard:

Every question has a hint under it, and the answers, with their working, are collected at the back of the book. After the questions come two or three readings, each marked gentle, serious or primary source, and a card that points to the next chapter.

Films

Seven narrated films, 58 minutes in all, retell the story with moving drawings. Each sits in the chapter it retells most closely, and several range over other chapters as well; the note before each film says where. Before it plays, a film asks its question. Under it, chapter marks jump to the part you want, each linked back to the figure it retells, and a transcript is there for reading instead of watching. is the first you will meet.

Words, scale and typical values

A dotted underline marks a word defined in the Glossary at the back: hover over it, or tap it on a phone, to read the definition, as with crest. names the parts of a dam and gives the book's three conventions.

All drawings are simplified. Those marked "drawn to scale" are drawn to scale, from the code's own dimensions or from published ones; the rest are schematic, and say so. The soil properties are typical values for teaching, not design values: a real dam is designed from tests on its own soils.

Time, and a tutor

Plan on eight to ten hours for the whole book, more if you work the questions, plus the films. Each chapter's opening page says how long it takes to read, and the bar at the top of the screen shows where you are and opens the Contents. If you are reading on claude.ai, each live figure also has a Why did that happen? button that asks Claude about exactly what the figure is showing you; the standalone page does not have it.
