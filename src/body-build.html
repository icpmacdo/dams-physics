Step 9

Build your own dam

You now have every idea you need to design an embankment. Water pressure grows with depth. Water seeps through soil at a rate set by its permeability and by how steeply the head drops. The grains hold a dam up by pressing on each other, and water pressure in the pores pushes them apart. Zones put each material where it does its best work.

Here you put those ideas to work. You paint the inside of a 24 m embankment with five materials, choose the ground it sits on, and fill the reservoir. Then two real calculations test it.

The first is the seepage solver from the seepage lab in Step 8. It finds where the water inside your dam settles, how much leaks through, and how hard it pushes on the soil where it comes out.

The second asks whether the downstream slope would slide. It tries more than a thousand curved surfaces through the slope. Each one is a possible slip surface. Along each surface it compares two things. One is the grip the soil can offer: friction plus a little stickiness. The other is the pull of the soil's weight trying to slide downhill. Friction depends on how hard the grains press together, so wherever the seepage solution puts water pressure on the surface, the grip drops.

Grip divided by pull is the factor of safety. Below 1 the slope slides. Dam engineers usually want 1.5 or more with the reservoir full, to cover what they can't know about the soil.

Leaks

Seepage in litres per day for each metre of dam. Some is normal. A lot means lost water, or polluted water for a tailings dam.

Pipes

Where water leaves unprotected soil, it pushes up on the grains. Safety against piping is the push that would float sand away divided by the push it gets. Designers want 3 or more.

Slides

The lowest factor of safety of any slip surface at least 2 m deep. Shallower slips are surface damage that maintenance fixes.

A dam made all of compacted fill has a factor of safety of 1.12, with water breaking out 8 m up its downstream slope. Which change helps it most?

The drain does the most: the factor of safety goes from 1.12 to about 1.6. The flatter slope reaches only about 1.27, and the cutoff about 1.14. The fill is strong enough when it is dry. Its trouble is the water in it: on the critical slip surface, water pressure carries about a third of the soil's weight, so the grains press together with only two-thirds of it. A drain lets that water out before it reaches the downstream slope. Try it below: test Homogeneous fill, then paint the drain.

What the check does, and what it leaves out

For circular slip surfaces the page uses Bishop's simplified method, the standard hand-and-computer method since 1955. It cuts the sliding mass into 40 vertical slices, works out how hard each slice presses on its base after taking away the water pressure there, and balances the turning effect of the whole mass about the circle's centre. On the weak-layer foundation it also tries sliding blocks with a flat base along the layer, using Spencer's method, which balances forces and turning effects together.

Water pressure on each slice comes straight from the seepage solution. Suction above the water level is ignored, as is usual practice, because it can vanish in a wet spell. For the upstream check the reservoir is emptied quickly: the free-draining materials drain with it, while clay and fill keep their water pressure for a while (the classic rapid drawdown case). Designers accept a lower factor of safety for that short-lived condition, about 1.2. This sandbox fixes the upstream slope at 2:1, which is steep for an earth dam; real ones are often flatter for exactly this reason.

Material strengths and permeabilities are typical values, not design values. The sandbox does not check earthquakes, the liquefaction of loose saturated sand (Step 4), or whether each zone is a proper filter for the one beside it. In a real dam, clay painted straight against rockfill would wash into it, which is why real cores have filters.
