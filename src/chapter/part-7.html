A concrete dam holds by its weight

A gravity dam is a wall of concrete heavy enough that the water can't push it over or slide it along the rock. It is the usual dam for hydroelectric power, where the water itself is the product: its height above the turbines is stored energy, released on demand.

Water enters the intake, drops through a steel pipe called the penstock to a turbine in the powerhouse at the foot of the dam, and leaves through the tailrace. Move the reservoir level and the turbine flow. The power is the efficiency × the density of water × gravity × the flow × the head of water, P = η ρ g Q H; this drawing assumes the turbine and generator together turn 90% of the water's energy into electricity (η = 0.90).

The reservoir level also sets the load on the dam, by the rule from Part 1: the pressure at the dam's upstream foot (its heel) is about 9.8 kPa for every metre of depth, nearly 1 MPa (1,000 kPa) for a 100 m reservoir. A grout curtain in the rock under the heel, and a drainage gallery inside the dam, deal with the water that seeps under it. The next page shows why they matter.

Uplift: the block on the cushion

A concrete dam can't erode, but water seeping under its base still pushes up on it. That push is called uplift. It cancels part of the dam's weight, and weight is what stops the dam sliding. It is the block on the water cushion from page , at full size.

The drawing is a 60 m dam, drawn to scale, per metre length of dam. A line of drains, drilled from a gallery just behind the grout curtain, relieves the pressure under the base. A common design assumption, used by the US Bureau of Reclamation among others, is that working drains cut the pressure at the drain line to the tailwater level plus a third of the difference. Switch the drains to Clogged and the uplift nearly doubles, and the sliding safety drops.

The sliding check is the friction of the dam's weight, less the uplift, on the rock, divided by the water's push. This drawing assumes concrete on rock has a friction angle of 45°, so the friction is the press itself. Many design guides look for a sliding factor of safety of about 1.5. The combined push of weight and water should also pass through the middle third of the base, so the heel stays pressed down and no crack opens to let full reservoir pressure in underneath.
