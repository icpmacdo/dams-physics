Why a dam is built from several soils

An embankment dam is a dam made of soil and rock rather than concrete. Large ones are rarely one material. They are zoned: each zone is a different soil or rock, chosen for one job. A zone either holds water back, lets water out without letting soil out, or supplies weight.

The drawing is a typical 60 m embankment dam, cut across the valley and drawn to scale, with its reservoir on the left. Press Build to watch it go up in the order it is built.

In the middle is the core of compacted clay: the clay plug from page , a few metres thick and 57 m tall. On each side of it are thin bands of sand and fine gravel, the filters and transitions. Outside those are the shells, great wedges of rockfill that give the dam its weight. Under the core, a cutoff trench and a curtain of grout seal the ground beneath.

Read the section from the middle outward and the pattern repeats: tight in the centre, then coarser and more free-draining toward the faces. In the language of Part 3, the core takes the water's pressure, the filters and drains keep the water pressure low in the outer zones, and the shells, kept dry, have grains pressing hard on each other and plenty of grip.

What each zone does

The table lists every zone in the drawing, with the code that labels it. The permeabilities run from rockfill, which water pours through, to the core, which it barely enters.

The dashed blue line is the phreatic surface: the water table inside the dam. Below it, every gap between the grains is full of water. Above it, the soil is damp or dry. It is level with the reservoir in the upstream shell, drops steeply through the core, and runs out almost flat along the bottom of the downstream shell. That drop is the clay plug at work, and it leaves the downstream shell dry and strong.

Why a filter works

The most important zone in most embankments is a few metres of carefully graded sand. Water leaving the core pushes on every soil grain it passes. If the next material has big gaps, as rockfill does, the fine core grains are carried into them. The core erodes from the inside, which is called internal erosion.

A filter has gaps smaller than the larger core grains. Those grains lodge at the boundary and form a stable layer that stops the finer grains behind them. Water still passes, so the filter also acts as a drain. That is why the filter beside the core is drawn as part of the drainage of the dam: it is the chimney drain.

For the record, the classic rule (Terzaghi's) is written in grain sizes. D15 is the size that 15% of a soil by weight is finer than. A filter holds a soil back if its D15 is no more than 4 to 5 times the soil's D85, and still drains if its D15 is at least 4 to 5 times the soil's D15. Sherard and Dunnigan refined the rules for clay cores in 1989.
