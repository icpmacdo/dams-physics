What tailings are

A mine digs up ore and grinds it finer than flour to get the metal out. What is left is tailings: ground rock mixed with the water used in the mill, pumped out as a slurry. A big mine makes tens of thousands of tonnes of it a day, for decades. Nobody wants it back. The job of a tailings dam is to keep it in place permanently.

The slurry is let out through pipes along the crest of the dam, called spigots. The coarse sand drops out first, near the spigots, and builds a gently sloping beach against the dam. The finest particles, called slimes, ride the water on to a pond at the far end, where a barge pumps the water back to the mill. Watch the grains sort themselves.

That sorting matters. The beach sand next to the dam is coarser and drains better than the slimes, so it is stronger. Much of a tailings dam is eventually built on top of the tailings, or out of the tailings sand itself, separated from the slimes by a machine called a cyclone. The deposit is the dam's main load, and often part of its foundation.

How a tailings dam differs from a water dam

The physics is the same on both sides of the crest. What differs is almost everything else.

Water damTailings dam
Holds backWater, released through turbines or to farms and towns on demandGround rock (silt to fine sand size) and process water, settling into a soft deposit
BuiltUsually to full height in one go, before the reservoir fillsRaised in stages for the life of the mine, often for decades, while tailings keep arriving
Made ofConcrete, or earth and rockfill from quarries and pits, specified and testedOften the tailings themselves (cycloned sand), mine waste rock and soil dug nearby

Raising a tailings dam: upstream

A tailings dam is never finished while the mine runs. The mill produces tailings every day, so the dam has to grow with them. Construction starts with a starter dam of ordinary compacted soil. When the tailings behind it near the crest, the dam is raised. There are three ways to do it, named for the direction the crest moves.

In an upstream raise, the crest steps toward the pond. Each raise is a small dyke built on the tailings beach.

Press Play to build it. The vertical scale is stretched 1.5 times so the stages are easier to see. It is the cheapest method by far, because each raise is small and can be made of tailings sand. But look at what each raise stands on: the red lines mark fill resting on tailings. By the end, most of the dam is founded on loose, saturated tailings, the kind of soil that liquefied in the sandbox on page .

Downstream and centreline

The other two methods avoid building on tailings. In a downstream raise the crest steps away from the pond, and each raise is built over the previous downstream slope, on compacted fill and natural ground. In a centreline raise the crest rises straight up, and each raise straddles the beach and the downstream slope.

All three end at the same height above the same starter dam. The fill numbers are cross-section areas, the volume per metre length of dam: the downstream dam needs about four times the fill of the upstream one, and its crest ends up about 100 m further from the pond.

Upstream

For

  • Least fill by far. Cheapest and fastest.
  • Can be built from the tailings themselves.

Against

  • Each raise rests on loose, saturated tailings that can liquefy.
  • The pond must be kept far from the crest and raising must be slow.
  • Drains can't be extended upward.
  • New upstream dams are banned in Chile (since 1970) and Brazil (since 2019).

Downstream

For

  • Every raise is founded on compacted fill or natural ground.
  • A core and drains can be extended with each raise, like a water dam.
  • Best in earthquakes; can store water.

Against

  • Most fill: the cross-section grows with the square of the height.
  • The foot of the dam moves outward, so land downstream has to be set aside from the start.

Centreline

For

  • A middle path on fill and footprint.
  • The crest stays put, so pipelines and roads don't move.
  • A vertical chimney drain can be raised with every lift.

Against

  • The upstream edge of each raise sits on the beach, so the beach next to the crest must be sandy, drained and compacted.

Shake all three

The three dams are finished. Now press Earthquake.

The loose, saturated tailings under the upstream raises liquefy, and the raises go with them. In the other two dams every raise rests on compacted, drained fill, and they ride out the shaking. Press Reset to put them back.

Why tailings dams fail differently

A reservoir of water can't lose strength. A deposit of loose, saturated tailings can. When loose grains are sheared, they try to pack tighter. The water in the gaps can't escape fast enough, so its pressure rises, the grains stop pressing on each other, and the deposit briefly behaves like a heavy fluid. That is the liquefaction of page , at the scale of a mine.

It can be set off by an earthquake, or with no shaking at all, by a rising water level, slow creep, or a small slip of the slope. Liquefaction without an earthquake is called static liquefaction, and it can take a dam in seconds.

  • 1985Stava, Italy. Two upstream-raised tailings dams collapsed one into the other; 268 people killed.
  • 1994Merriespruit, South Africa. A gold tailings dam overtopped after heavy rain and flowed into a town; 17 killed.
  • 2014Mount Polley, Canada. The embankment slid on a weak clay layer in its foundation (page ), releasing about 25 million m³ of tailings and water. No deaths.
  • 2015Fundão, Mariana, Brazil. An upstream-raised dam liquefied; 19 killed. Tailings travelled roughly 650 km down the Rio Doce to the Atlantic.
  • 2019Brumadinho, Brazil. Dam I, upstream-raised, failed by static liquefaction in seconds; 270 killed. The disaster led to the GISTM and to Brazil banning upstream dams.

Coquitlam Dam: a water dam built like a tailings dam

Coquitlam Dam, British Columbia · built 1914, backed up 2007

Coquitlam Dam was built by hydraulic fill: soil washed into place with water, which leaves it loose and saturated, much like an upstream tailings raise. Modern investigations found a loose zone that could liquefy in a large earthquake.

Rather than repair the old fill, BC Hydro built a new compacted earth-core rockfill dam against its downstream foot, on dense glacial soil. The lesson runs through this whole chapter: loose and wet versus dense and drained matters more than whether a dam holds water or tailings.
