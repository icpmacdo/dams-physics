Sheet 1

Two dams, two jobs

A hydroelectric dam holds back water, and the water is the product. Its height above the turbines is stored energy, released on demand. A tailings dam holds back mine waste: rock ground finer than flour, pumped out of the mill as a slurry with process water. Nobody wants it back. The job is to keep it in place permanently.

Same physics on both sides of the crest. Water pressure grows linearly with depth, p = ρgh, about 9.8 kPa per metre, so nearly 1 MPa at the foot of a 100 m reservoir. And water seeps through anything that isn't perfectly tight.

Why tailings dams fail differently

A reservoir of water cannot lose strength. A deposit of loose, saturated tailings can. When loose grains are sheared, they try to pack tighter; the water in the pores can't escape fast enough, so pore pressure rises, the grain-to-grain contact force drops toward zero, and the deposit briefly behaves like a heavy fluid. This is liquefaction. It can be triggered by an earthquake, or with no shaking at all (static liquefaction) by a rising water table, creep, or a small slope movement.

  • 1985Stava, Italy. Two upstream-raised fluorite tailings dams collapsed one into the other; 268 people killed.
  • 1994Merriespruit, South Africa. A gold tailings dam overtopped after heavy rain and flowed into a town; 17 killed.
  • 2014Mount Polley, Canada. The embankment slid on a weak glacial-lake clay layer in its foundation, releasing about 25 million m³ of tailings and water. No deaths.
  • 2015Fundão, Mariana, Brazil. An upstream-raised dam liquefied; 19 killed. Tailings travelled roughly 650 km down the Rio Doce to the Atlantic.
  • 2019Brumadinho, Brazil. Dam I, upstream-raised, failed by static liquefaction in seconds; 270 killed. The disaster led to the GISTM and to Brazil banning upstream dams.

Upstream, downstream, centreline

A tailings dam is never finished while the mine runs. The mill produces tailings every day, so the dam has to grow with them. Construction starts with a starter dam built from borrow material, and tailings are spigotted from its crest. When the deposit nears the crest, the dam is raised. The direction each raise moves gives the method its name:

  • Upstream: the crest steps toward the pond. Each raise is a small dyke built on the tailings beach.
  • Downstream: the crest steps away from the pond. Each raise is built over the previous downstream slope, on compacted fill and natural ground.
  • Centreline: the crest rises vertically. Each raise straddles the beach and the downstream slope.

Upstream

For

  • Least fill by far: each raise is a small dyke. Cheapest and fastest.
  • Can be built from the tailings themselves.

Against

  • Each raise rests on loose, saturated tailings that can liquefy, statically or in an earthquake.
  • Needs a long dry beach and a pond kept far from the crest to hold the phreatic surface down; raising must be slow.
  • Drains can't be extended upward; can't store much water.
  • New upstream dams are banned in Chile (since 1970) and Brazil (since 2019).

Downstream

For

  • Every raise is founded on compacted fill or natural ground.
  • A core and drains can be extended with each raise, like a water dam.
  • Best earthquake performance; can store water.

Against

  • Most fill: the cross-section grows with the square of the height.
  • The toe marches outward, so land downstream has to be reserved from the start.

Centreline

For

  • A middle path on fill volume and footprint.
  • The crest stays put, so tailings pipelines and access roads don't move.
  • A vertical chimney drain can be raised with every lift.

Against

  • The upstream edge of each raise sits on the beach, so the beach next to the crest must be sandy, drained and compacted.

Dam zones

Large embankment dams are rarely one material. They are zoned: each zone is a different soil or rock, chosen for one job. A zone either holds water back, lets water out without letting soil out, or supplies weight. Read a section from the middle outward and the pattern repeats: tight in the centre, then progressively coarser and more free-draining toward the faces.

Pick a dam type, then click any zone (or its chip) to see what it's made of and what it's for. Build replays the construction sequence; Explode pulls the zones apart.

Why a filter works

The most important zone in most embankments is a few metres of carefully graded sand. Water leaving the core carries a force on every soil grain it passes. If the next material has big voids, as rockfill does, the grains are carried into them: the start of internal erosion. A filter has pores smaller than the larger core grains, so those grains lodge at the interface and form a stable layer that stops the finer grains behind them. Water still passes.

Retention:    D15(filter) ≤ 4–5 × D85(soil)
Permeability: D15(filter) ≥ 4–5 × D15(soil)Terzaghi's criteria. Dx = the grain size that x% of the material (by weight) is finer than. A filter's pore throats are roughly a fifth of its D15. Sherard & Dunnigan (1989) refined the rules for clay cores.

Seepage paths

Every dam leaks. The design questions are where the water goes, how much pressure it carries, and whether it takes soil with it. Flow through soil follows Darcy's law:

q = k · i · A    with  i = Δh / Lq flow rate · k hydraulic conductivity (m/s) · i hydraulic gradient: head lost per metre travelled · A area

The conductivity k spans ten orders of magnitude: gravel around 10⁻² m/s, clean sand 10⁻⁴, silt 10⁻⁷, compacted clay 10⁻⁹. That range is the whole reason zoning works. A clay core a few metres thick can carry almost the entire head while the rockfill beside it carries almost none.

Water takes several routes past a dam. The five main ones are below.

Seepage lab: a real calculation

The next drawing isn't a cartoon. It solves the steady-state seepage equation, ∇·(k ∇h) = 0, on a 1 m grid under a 24 m embankment, with the phreatic surface found as part of the solution (a saturated–unsaturated model, the way commercial seepage software does it). Lines of equal head are drawn every 10% of the reservoir head. Flow lines are spaced so each pair carries the same flow, as in a hand-drawn flow net.

When seepage carries soil: piping

Water flowing upward out of the ground at the toe pushes on the sand grains it passes. When the upward gradient reaches the critical gradient, the water carries the full weight of the soil, effective stress drops to zero, and the sand boils:

ic = (Gs − 1) / (1 + e) ≈ (2.65 − 1) / (1 + 0.65) ≈ 1.0Gs specific gravity of the grains · e void ratio · safety factor against heave = i_c / i_exit; designers want 3 or more at an unfiltered exit

A boil at the toe is where backward erosion piping begins. Grains wash out, a small channel forms, and the channel works its way upstream under the dam, getting shorter and steeper as it goes. Erosion of this kind is one of the two big killers of embankment dams. In Foster, Fell and Spannagle's 2000 study of embankment-dam failures, piping through the embankment, through the foundation, or from one into the other caused roughly half of all failures, about as many as overtopping. Teton Dam in Idaho failed this way on its first filling in 1976.

Seepage under a concrete dam: uplift

A concrete dam can't erode, but water seeping under its base still pushes up on it. That uplift cancels part of the dam's weight, and weight is what stops it sliding. A curtain of drains, drilled from a gallery just behind the grout curtain, relieves the pressure. A common design assumption, used by the US Bureau of Reclamation among others, is that working drains cut the pressure at the drain line to tailwater plus one-third of the head difference.

Five ways to manage seepage

Cut it offCore, cutoff trench, slurry wall, grout curtain, face slab, geomembrane.
Lengthen the pathAn upstream impervious blanket makes the water travel further, lowering the gradient.
Drain itChimney, blanket and toe drains, relief wells, gallery drains: control where the phreatic surface sits and bleed off pressure.
Filter every exitWherever water leaves soil, it passes a filter so it leaves without the soil.
Weigh it downA berm at the toe adds weight against uplift and heave.
Watch itPiezometers measure pore pressure; weirs measure flow; cloudy water means soil is moving.
